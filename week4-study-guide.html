Part 1A — Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Topic 1, first half: what ALS is, its risk factors, the motor neuron pathology and the excitotoxicity (glutamate) hypothesis, the clinical manifestations, and riluzole.

ALS: What It Is

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

  • Also called Lou Gehrig's disease (after the Yankees baseball player who had it).
  • A neurodegenerative, progressive muscle weakness. The cause is unknown.
  • It is fatal, usually from respiratory failure caused by the progressive muscle weakness. Death usually occurs about 3 years after diagnosis.
Risk FactorWhat the Lecture Says
AgeTypically diagnosed between 40 and 70; peaks around 60 to 69.
SexMore diagnoses in males.
GeneticsA familial form with a genetic mutation accounts for about 10% of diagnoses.
SmokingSome research suggests it is a risk factor.

ALS: Pathology & Cause

  • The principal feature is degeneration of the upper and lower motor neurons, so the voluntary muscles are affected.
  • Large motor neurons decrease in the spinal cord, brainstem and cerebral cortex, followed by degeneration of the remaining motor neurons.
  • Motor neuron death leads to axonal degeneration, demyelination and sclerosis (scarring). Damaged motor neurons stop sending messages, so the muscles can't function.
The excitotoxicity hypothesis (the current working hypothesis): excessive levels of glutamate, an excitatory neurotransmitter, start a series of events that eventually kill the neuron. It came from finding elevated glutamate in the CSF of people with ALS, and riluzole, which reduces glutamate, improves survival.

ALS: Clinical Manifestations

Signs vary greatly from person to person, depending on which neurons are affected. Initial symptoms relate to upper motor neuron dysfunction, lower motor neuron dysfunction, or both.

Stage or AreaWhat the Lecture Says
Spinal form (about half)Weakness begins in the arms and legs and progresses to muscle atrophy: spasticity, lost dexterity, impaired gait, trouble walking, tripping and falling, hand weakness.
As it progressesDifficulty chewing, swallowing and speaking; cognitive and behavioral changes in some; constipation (immobility, diet, muscle weakness); sleep problems.
EventuallyBreathing becomes the main issue: ALS paralyzes the breathing muscles, and respiratory failure is why people with ALS die.

ALS: Riluzole

FeatureWhat the Lecture Says
StatusThe only FDA-approved drug for ALS.
How it worksA glutamate inhibitor: blocks glutamate receptor sites and helps reduce damage to the motor neurons.
BenefitProlongs survival and slows the deterioration of function, but not by long: about 6 months to a year at most.
Side effectsDizziness, GI upset, nausea, vomiting. Hepatotoxic: watch liver function.

Part 1B — Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

Topic 1, second half: what GBS is and what triggers it, the ascending pattern and its timing, the manifestations, and treatment and prognosis.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome: What It Is

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), also called acute ascending polyneuritis

  • A rare autoimmune disorder that causes demyelination: autoantibodies damage the myelin sheath.
  • Myelin covers the axons and lets impulses travel almost instantaneously. As it is destroyed, impulses are delayed or completely absent.
TriggerWhat the Lecture Says
Viral infectionThe usual trigger, a few days to weeks earlier, typically after a respiratory tract or GI infection.
Bacterial infectionSome cases; particularly Campylobacter jejuni, a foodborne illness.
SurgeryOccasionally, days to weeks after surgery.
Flu vaccinationRarely.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome: Pattern & Manifestations

The ascending pattern

  • Symptoms usually start in the lower extremities (weakness and tingling, pins and needles) and ascend up the body.
  • Recovery goes the opposite direction: strength returns from the top down (upper body, lungs, then the legs).
  • Severity ranges from tingling and weakness to full leg paralysis, complete quadriplegia, and respiratory insufficiency.

Timing

  • Most worsen over hours to weeks.
  • Some decline rapidly, over 24 hours; these clients may end up intubated on a ventilator.
  • Recovery is just as variable; some need a tracheostomy after weeks of ventilation.
The concern is the ascending weakness reaching the breathing muscles. Once they're affected, GBS can cause respiratory insufficiency and ultimately respiratory failure needing mechanical ventilation.

Manifestations

  • Uncoordinated movements, loss of strength, difficulty walking: a high fall risk.
  • Decreased sensation, so clients may not notice injury.
  • Loss of bowel and bladder control.
  • Blurred vision, which doesn't always fit the ascending pattern and can be an early sign.
  • Problems breathing, swallowing and chewing, so an aspiration risk.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome: Treatment & Prognosis

  • No cure. The goal is to manage severity and speed recovery.
  • Acute phase: steroids (high dose, titrated down as the client recovers) and IV immunoglobulin (IVIG), which shorten the severity and duration of symptoms; high-dose IVIG can shorten hospital stays.
  • Most people get GBS only once; some have recurrences.
OutcomeAbout How Many
Survive95%
Return to baseline function75%
Residual weakness after about 3 years25 to 30%; may never return to baseline
Relapses of weakness and tingling many years laterAbout 3%

Part 2 — Neuro Infectious Diseases

Topic 2: meningitis (the meninges, causes, who gets it and how it spreads, pathophysiology, signs and the Kernig and Brudzinski tests, bacterial vs viral, treatment and prevention), encephalitis, and brain abscess.

Meningitis: What It Is & What Causes It

  • The lecture defines meningitis as "an inflammation of the brain or the spinal cord." It is primarily an infection of the pia mater and subarachnoid space, the ventricular system and the CSF.
  • Infectious meningitis is caused by bacteria: from an infection elsewhere (such as pneumonia), the bloodstream, or a penetrating wound that lets bacteria reach the CSF or brain.
  • Most common pathogens in adults: Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococci) and Neisseria meningitidis (meningococci).
  • It may also be caused by viruses (enteroviruses in particular), fungi, parasites or toxins, but bacteria are the most common.
Meningeal LayerWhat It Is
Pia materThe layer closest to the brain.
Arachnoid materThe middle layer; the subarachnoid space is filled with cerebrospinal fluid.
Dura materThe outer layer: really tough and fibrous.

Who Gets Meningitis & How It Spreads

When and who

  • Typically fall or winter, when people stay inside with the windows closed.
  • Typically follows otitis or sinusitis (an ear or sinus infection), an immunocompromised state, or pneumonia.
  • Pneumococcal meningitis mostly affects young people and those older than 40 (as stated in the lecture).
  • Outbreaks in college dorms, prisons, military bases: anywhere large groups live and sleep together. It is very communicable.

How it spreads

  • Respiratory droplets, or contact with contaminated saliva or respiratory secretions.
  • Some carriers of meningococcal bacteria never develop the disease but can still pass it on.

Meningitis: Pathophysiology

StepWhat Happens
1Meningococci or pneumococci are inhaled and attach to the epithelial cells of the nasopharynx.
2They cross the mucosal barrier and enter the bloodstream.
3They travel to the brain, cross the blood-brain barrier and infect the meninges, starting in the arachnoid mater and the CSF of the subarachnoid space.
4Large numbers of neutrophils are recruited; the inflammation causes brain edema, and the exudate thickens the CSF and can impair its flow.
5Increased intracranial pressure: an adult's skull is a closed system with no room for more.

Meningitis: Clinical Manifestations

The classic triad: Fever (infection), Headache (meningeal irritation; often throbbing), and Stiff neck / nuchal rigidity (tucking the chin to the chest is painful because it stretches the inflamed meninges).

Other manifestations

  • Change in mental status.
  • Nausea and vomiting (can happen with increased ICP).
  • Severe photophobia.
  • Decreased consciousness, up to coma or death, from increased ICP.
  • Seizures in about 30% of cases.
  • Specific to meningococcus: a petechial or purpuric rash covering the skin and mucous membranes.

Tests for Meningeal Irritation

TestHow It's DonePositive Result
Kernig signClient supine; one leg extended, the other flexed 90° at the hip and 90° at the knee. Support the heel and knee of the bent leg and try to straighten it toward the ceiling.Pain (the client may even flex the neck).
Brudzinski signClient supine; place a hand under the base of the skull and flex the neck.Neck flexion causes the hips and knees to flex, to reduce the pain of meningeal irritation.

Bacterial vs Viral Meningitis

Bacterial

  • More common, with a higher fatality rate if treatment isn't started quickly: it can kill within hours.
  • The petechial rash is associated with this form. It is non-blanchable: press a glass over it and a petechial or purpuric rash won't fade.
  • Can cause septic emboli: tiny microemboli that lodge in capillaries and block blood flow, so adults and children can lose fingers or limbs.
  • Long-term effects: hearing loss, seizures, brain damage.

Viral (acute viral meningitis)

  • The milder form.
  • Often no elevated white cell count in the CSF on lumbar puncture.
  • Typically no long-term effects.
Bacterial meningitis: give antibiotics as soon as possible and do not hold them for any reason. It can kill within a matter of hours.

Treating & Preventing Meningitis

TreatmentWhat the Lecture Says
IV antibioticsAggressive therapy with multiple IV antibiotics; two of the most common are ceftriaxone and vancomycin (vancomycin for a MRSA form).
AcyclovirGiven frequently to prevent herpetic encephalitis, which is deadly, in case the cause is viral. Stopped once the CSF results show it isn't viral.
SteroidsTo reduce inflammation.
Anti-seizure medicationsOften during the acute phase of meningitis and encephalitis, because increased ICP and inflammation raise seizure risk.
Vaccines (the best prophylaxis)Available for meningococcus, pneumococcus and H. influenzae. The meningitis vaccine is first given around age 11 or 12, with a booster around 16; a different version is given to people who are immunocompromised or have no spleen.

Encephalitis

Inflammation of the brain tissue

  • An acute condition, usually caused by a virus.
  • Causes: West Nile virus (from mosquitoes; the lecture mentions a 2016 outbreak), measles, chickenpox, mumps. It can happen very rapidly, and patients can die.
  • Herpes simplex virus 1 causes herpetic encephalitis: very severe, with rapidly appearing manifestations, unlike viral meningitis, which is relatively mild.
  • With severe symptoms, clients are often started on both antibiotics and acyclovir until the cause is known.
FeatureWhat the Lecture Says
OnsetSigns typically appear about 2 to 3 days after infection, once enough virus builds up.
ManifestationsFrom mild up to mental status changes and coma; fever and headache, nausea and vomiting; CNS changes such as seizures (common); others depending on where the infection is. These are vague and nonspecific; you don't need to know the full slide list in detail.
AcyclovirFor the HSV type in particular, but not effective on other viruses. In herpetic encephalitis it reduces mortality but doesn't necessarily reduce neurological complications.
Supportive careFluids, acetaminophen, antiemetics for vomiting, and often anti-seizure medications.

Brain Abscess

FeatureWhat the Lecture Says
What it isAn accumulation of pus within the brain tissue, from a localized infection (like encephalitis) or a systemic infection.
Common sourcesEar, tooth, mastoid or sinus infections, because of their proximity to the brain.
Major culpritsStreptococci and Staphylococcus aureus, bacteria found on the skin that typically cause ear, tooth and sinus infections.
ImagingThe abscess walls are visible, and the midline can be shifted by the pressure from pus and inflammation.
TreatmentDrain the abscess to reduce pressure (the brain is a closed system, so this lowers the risk of secondary injury from swelling), then put antibiotics into the area.
ManifestationsSimilar to meningitis and encephalitis: headache, fever, nausea, vomiting; signs of increased ICP (drowsiness, confusion, up to seizures). Other symptoms depend on location; for example, a temporal lobe abscess causes visual field defects.

Part 3A — Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)

Topic 3, first voiceover (TIA): what a TIA is, carotid vs vertebrobasilar presentations, the Bell's palsy mimic, the key terms, and TIA diagnosis and treatment.

Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)

  • A type of cerebrovascular disease: a focal, acute cerebral insufficiency. Usually only one vessel gets closed off, clotted off or has a spasm, so only that area of the brain is affected.
  • Typically lasts less than 24 hours: a transient episode of neurologic dysfunction with no acute infarction. Symptoms are often gone by the time the client reaches the ED, with no residual effects.
  • A stroke, by contrast, causes neurological deficits that last longer than 24 hours.
  • Fairly common; seen more often in males.
  • After a TIA, the client is at increased risk for a stroke, especially within the first month. Monitor and teach them to go to the ED if they suspect another TIA, because it might be a full-blown stroke.
  • Presentation depends on which vessel is involved (a vessel supplying the speech area causes difficulty forming words). Recurrent TIAs usually produce the same symptoms each time. Onset is abrupt, and recovery is abrupt and rapid.

The lecture also says a CT can show blood in the brain tissue with a TIA, though "not as clear as it is with an ischemic stroke." That is kept as stated; check the slide wording.

Carotid area

  • Weakness or heaviness in the arms, legs or face.
  • Numbness.
  • Vision loss.

Vertebrobasilar area (back of the brain)

  • Blurry vision.
  • Vertigo.
  • Ataxia.
  • Motor or sensory changes.

TIA mimic: Bell's palsy

A problem with the facial nerve causes facial paralysis (drooping mouth, drooping face), so these clients are often evaluated for a stroke or TIA. Bell's palsy recovers, but it takes time and requires treatment; a TIA recovers much more quickly.

Key Terms: Dysphagia, Dysphasia, Contralateral & Ipsilateral

TermMeaningWhy It Matters
Dysphagia (with a G)Difficulty swallowing. Memory trick: G for gastric.Keep NPO: high risk for aspiration (a foreign substance entering the lungs from eating and drinking without swallowing properly), which can inflame and damage the lungs.
Dysphasia (with an S)Difficulty with speech.A communication problem.
ContralateralCrossing the midline: the opposite side.Manifestations appear on the opposite side of the body from the brain damage.
IpsilateralStaying on the same side.Manifestations appear on the same side as the damage.

TIA: Diagnosis & Treatment

DiagnosticPurpose
History and physicalIs there a pattern of other vascular problems?
Quick CT scan firstRule out hemorrhage, which is easy to see on CT. Then look for other evidence of CVA, such as lacunar infarcts (tiny infarcts that may cause no symptoms).
MRA (MRI with angiography)Uses dye to highlight aneurysms.
Carotid Doppler studiesLook for buildup in the carotid arteries reducing blood flow to the brain.
EchocardiogramLook for a cardiac cause, such as a clot on the left side of the heart that could break off and travel to the brain.

Treatment depends on the cause; the goal is reducing the risk of a full stroke

  • Small doses of aspirin.
  • Clopidogrel (Plavix): an antiplatelet that keeps platelets from sticking together.
  • If the TIA is cardiac-related: heparin or warfarin to prevent coagulation.
  • If caused by carotid narrowing or stenosis: carotid endarterectomy (opens the artery by removing the plaque), or carotid angioplasty (a balloon catheter fed up through usually the femoral artery and inflated, with a filter to catch plaque pieces that break free).
  • Education on lifestyle changes, especially the modifiable risk factors for stroke.

Part 3B — Stroke & Ischemic Stroke

Topic 3, second voiceover (Stroke and hemorrhage 1): stroke types and statistics, risk factors, signs and the first CT, ischemic stroke (thrombotic and embolic), and the penumbra.

Stroke (CVA): Overview & Statistics

  • Also called a cerebrovascular accident (CVA), the term used in hospitals, or a "brain attack" (a term that didn't stick).
  • The result of a problem with blood supply to the brain. Two types: ischemic, about 87% of strokes, and hemorrhagic, about 13%.
  • About 800,000 strokes a year; for over half a million people it's their first stroke. Risk rises with age, but about a third of people with a first stroke are under 65.
  • A leading cause of disability. Third leading cause of death in women, fifth in men, fourth overall. Someone has a stroke about every 40 seconds and someone dies from one every 4 minutes; about $36.5 billion in healthcare costs a year.

Stroke Risk Factors

Risk FactorWhy It Raises Risk
HypertensionEspecially uncontrolled or undiagnosed. Commonly leads to hemorrhagic stroke, often by way of an aneurysm that ruptures, or by weakening the arterial wall until it ruptures. More than doubles the risk of intracranial hemorrhage.
Atherosclerosis and hyperlipidemiaVessel damage leads to plaque formation and clots, in brain vessels as in the heart.
Tobacco useDamages the endothelial lining of blood vessels.
DiabetesDamages blood vessel walls, which can lead to plaque formation.
Oral contraceptivesParticularly estrogen and progesterone: increase plasma fibrinogen and coagulation factor activity.
Age (starred)Advanced age: higher risk of stroke but a better chance of recovery, because brain atrophy leaves more room in the closed skull for swelling and bleeding.
Male sexMales are at higher risk overall.
Sickle cell anemiaIn crisis, blood cells clump and can lodge in brain vessels.
Atrial fibrillationQuivering atria let blood pool and clot; the clot can travel to the brain. Often treated with anticoagulants such as warfarin, which bring their own bleeding risk.

Signs of a Stroke & the First Test

Clinical manifestations

  • Numbness or weakness of the face, arm or leg, usually on one side (asymmetrical weakness).
  • Sudden confusion; difficulty speaking or understanding speech; slurred speech (dysarthria).
  • Vision trouble in one or both eyes.
  • Trouble walking, balance problems, dizziness, lack of coordination (ataxia).
  • A sudden, severe headache with no known cause, particularly important in hemorrhagic stroke.

CT without contrast, done very quickly

  • Part of the stroke alert protocol (with attention to airway and breathing as needed); it drives the rest of the treatment plan.
  • Blood shows up right away as white; an ischemic stroke shows as a darkened area.
  • We must know ischemic vs hemorrhagic: never anticoagulate or give a thrombolytic to a hemorrhagic stroke. It makes the bleeding worse and could kill the client.

Ischemic Stroke

  • An occlusion of a cerebral artery by a thrombus or an embolus; everything distal to the occlusion loses its blood supply.
  • Both types typically present the same way, based on which part of the brain isn't perfused, but the etiology guides treatment: atherosclerosis? a hypercoagulability problem? a thrombus or embolism elsewhere?
  • On CT, a darkened area that isn't prominent at first but becomes more noticeable if blood flow isn't restored.
  • A tiny vessel occlusion may go unnoticed (evidence of an old stroke is sometimes found on an unrelated CT). A larger vessel occlusion produces symptoms based on its location.
  • Neuro deficits start within about one minute of oxygen deprivation; without reestablished blood flow, the damage becomes irreversible.
  • Hemorrhagic conversion: after an ischemic stroke, blood from the peripheral circulation extravasates across a disrupted blood-brain barrier into the brain tissue once reperfusion is established, naturally or as a result of therapy. A hemorrhage then occurs where there was an ischemic event.
TypeCauses
ThromboticArterial obstruction from thrombus formation. Atherosclerosis: vessel-lining damage leads to plaque that narrows or blocks the vessel; if the plaque ruptures, a clot forms. Hypercoagulable states: cancer, oral contraceptives, and more recently some patients with COVID.
EmbolicLook at the heart first:
  • Mural thrombus: a clot in the ventricle (the image shows the left ventricle); a piece breaks off and travels through the aorta and carotids to the brain.
  • Atrial fibrillation: a left atrial thrombus from pooled blood. Usually arterial clots, on the left side of the heart.
  • Patent foramen ovale: a birth-defect hole in the septum between the atria lets a venous clot (such as a DVT piece) pass from the right atrium to the left atrium instead of going to the lungs as a PE; the left ventricle then sends it to the brain.
  • Valve infection, particularly the mitral valve: vegetation forms, and a piece breaks off as an embolus.
  • Mechanical valve replacement (more than a porcine valve): clots can form around the valve.
  • Carotid artery plaque can rupture, and the clot travels to the brain.

The lecture describes the mitral valve as "between the right and left ventricle." It is kept out of the table as a likely slip; check the slide.

Assessment clues for embolic risk

  • Carotid bruit: a swishing sound on auscultation from turbulent flow through a vessel narrowed by a growing plaque.
  • ECG: in A-fib, little quivering lines replace the P waves (the atria aren't contracting properly). These clients are at higher risk for embolic stroke, especially if newly diagnosed and not yet anticoagulated long enough.

Time Is Tissue: The Penumbra

Dead tissue (core)

Injured beyond repair.

Penumbra

The extended area of injured but salvageable tissue around the core.

Goal for ischemic stroke: treatment within 3 hours of symptom onset, to reestablish blood flow and save the penumbra. Once a stroke protocol is activated, the client goes to CT first (often bypassing a room), labs are drawn quickly and the neuro team sees them quickly.

Part 3C — Hemorrhagic Stroke & Intracranial Bleeds

Topic 3, third voiceover (Stroke and hemorrhage 2): hemorrhagic stroke, epidural, subdural and subarachnoid bleeds, subarachnoid hemorrhage management, berry aneurysms and AVMs, and BE FAST.

Hemorrhagic Stroke

  • A ruptured vessel with bleeding within the brain tissue (the parenchyma).
  • Leading cause: untreated hypertension; over time the pressure weakens vessels until they rupture. Clients with aneurysms are also at increased risk.
  • About 38% mortality, within minutes to hours; in the brainstem, death can occur within seconds. Mortality depends on the size of the area affected.
  • Blood is very irritating to brain tissue, so inflammation causes edema; blood plus edema in the confined skull sharply increases ICP. The brain tissue can collapse on itself (herniation), which usually results in death.
  • Usually ages 30 to 60, younger than ischemic stroke clients. High morbidity and mortality, with serious disabilities.

Prognosis depends on

  1. Age: older clients have brain atrophy and more room, so a better prognosis.
  2. Location and size: the brainstem has a very poor prognosis.
  3. How rapid the bleeding is, and whether it causes brain distortion or shift. A midline shift (a bleed pushing one side across the center line, seen on a CT report) means a very poor prognosis.

Presentation

  • The worst headache of their life.
  • A rapid change in level of consciousness.
  • With subdural, epidural or subarachnoid bleeds, the client presents like meningitis from meningeal irritation: nuchal rigidity and photophobia.

Other Intracranial Hemorrhages

Bleeding in the layers covering the brain, named by location. A bleed in the brain tissue itself is a hemorrhagic stroke.

BleedLocationKey Features
EpiduralBetween the skull and the dura mater.Usually with skull fractures (think trauma). Tend to be arterial, so even a less severe injury can mean a lot of blood loss.
SubduralBelow the dura.Tend to be venous, e.g., injury to bridging veins. Severe injury can cause rapid decline, but venous blood may accumulate slowly: a client hits their head, seems fine, and symptoms appear later.
SubarachnoidBetween the arachnoid mater and the pia mater, where the CSF lives.Usually from an aneurysm or an AVM. Causes bloody CSF.

Treatment for epidural and subdural bleeds is to evacuate the collecting blood and relieve pressure on the brain. Blood bleeding into the brain tissue cannot be evacuated.

Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

Diagnosis by lumbar puncture

Normal CSF is clear or slightly yellow. The first tube may have a little blood from needle trauma, but it should clear. With a subarachnoid bleed, the fluid stays bloody. A subarachnoid hemorrhage is identified by lumbar puncture about 12 hours after symptom onset.

ManagementWhat the Lecture Says
Bed rest and BP controlAvoid big BP fluctuations: keep it managed to prevent further bleeding, but high enough to perfuse the brain. The nurse is often given very specific BP parameters.
SurgeryIf an aneurysm is the culprit, surgical intervention (clipping or coiling), usually done ASAP.
AnticonvulsantBlood irritates the brain, so seizure risk rises.
Calcium channel blockerAfter clipping or coiling, to help treat the symptoms of the subarachnoid hemorrhage.
Triple HKeeps the brain perfused: pressors to keep BP high enough, colloids and a hypervolemic state, and some expected hemodilution.

The lecture describes the calcium channel blocker as working "by narrowing and slowing that blood flow." It is kept out of the table as stated; check the slide for the intended mechanism.

Berry Aneurysms, AVMs, Clipping & Coiling

Lesion or ProcedureWhat the Lecture Says
Berry aneurysmThe most common aneurysm that ruptures in the brain: little berries hanging off the artery that rupture when big enough.
AVM (arteriovenous malformation)Normally arteries divide into arterioles, capillaries, venules, then veins. In an AVM the junction is a jumbled clump, so high-pressure arterial blood goes directly into the vein without slowing through capillaries. The pressure weakens vessel walls, raising rupture risk.
ClippingA clip is placed around the aneurysm to block blood flow into it.

Part 3D — Stroke Treatment & Consequences

Topic 3, fourth voiceover (Stroke treatment): the first steps, hemorrhagic vs ischemic treatment, alteplase, and the consequences of stroke.

Stroke Treatment: First Steps

  1. ABCs: can they maintain their airway, are they breathing, is there a pulse? A GCS below 8 raises concern about airway protection, so the client is intubated. Stroke clients may also have decreased respiratory drive.
  2. Stroke alert / protocol: labs, CT, the neuro team, and a blood sugar check, because hypoglycemia can mimic a stroke.
  3. CT scan without contrast, done first to exclude hemorrhagic stroke: fast, and best in the acute stage. An MRI gives better images later, but it is slower and less available, so it isn't the first choice in an emergency.

Hemorrhagic stroke

  • Reverse anticoagulation: is there a reversal agent or antidote for the client's medications?
  • Manage hypertension, the likely cause.
  • Manage ICP: elevate the head of the bed, give antipyretics for fever, keep the head in a neutral position.

Ischemic stroke

  • Thrombolytics (clot busters) to open the occlusion and protect and reperfuse the penumbra.
  • Mechanical thrombectomy: a surgeon removes the clot manually (also called a penumbra procedure).

Alteplase (tPA) for Ischemic Stroke

  • Heparin and antiplatelets don't dissolve a clot; they only prevent one from forming. Fibrinolytics actually break up clots.
  • Alteplase (tPA, tissue plasminogen activator), the most commonly used fibrinolytic, converts plasminogen to plasmin; plasmin digests the fibrin.
  • Used in severe thrombotic events: acute MI, PE, ischemic stroke, occasionally femoral artery clots or a clotted central line. Given IV.
Nursing PointWhat the Lecture Says
Monitor BPIt can cause hypotension.
Monitor HR and rhythmArrhythmias can develop with reperfusion.
Bleeding riskIncreased risk of intracranial bleeding. Don't give it to clients at high risk for bleeding (such as a previous hemorrhagic stroke or a GI bleed).
CT firstAlways rule out hemorrhagic stroke before giving it.
With heparinOften given with heparin to prevent new clots from forming.
AntidoteAminocaproic acid, an antithrombolytic: blocks plasminogen binding and activation to plasmin, reducing fibrinolysis to stop bleeding. Give it only if the hemorrhage is life-threatening.

Consequences of Stroke

Left side of the brain affected

Difficulty with communication (verbal and written) and with more analytical activities.

Right side of the brain affected

Difficulty with things like imagination, music and art.

ConsequenceWhat the Lecture Says
DysphagiaA swallow evaluation comes before giving food or advancing a diet. Speech pathology does a barium swallow study under real-time x-ray with different thicknesses of food and liquid, then recommends a diet: regular, thickened liquids, or NPO (with a possible feeding tube) if dysfunction is severe.
Motor and sensory deficitsNeglect of body parts no longer getting sensory input; poor coordination (fall risk); loss of muscle groups, which atrophy over time.
Flaccidity → spasticity → contracturesFlaccid (limp, weak) muscles on the contralateral side become spastic (painful), then contractures (permanently shortened muscles, joints stuck in abnormal positions), typically within the first 6 weeks.
Homonymous hemianopiaContralateral field blindness: loss (or blurring) of up to half the vision, on the same side of both visual fields.
AphasiaThe global term for affected language: dysphasia (speech), receptive aphasia (understanding), reading and writing problems; one or all.
DepressionVery common, from significant disabilities and loss of independence.
Memory problemsEven well-known things like family members' names.
Behavior changesIncreased emotional response (upset, angry or crying more easily); slower reaction times; hesitance or caution; confusion or disorientation; apathy.

2. Neuro Infectious DiseasesMeningitis: S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis; respiratory droplets; fall and winter; dorms, prisons, military. Triad: fever, headache, nuchal rigidity; photophobia; seizures in 30%; non-blanching petechial rash (meningococcal); Kernig and Brudzinski. Bacterial kills in hours (antibiotics ASAP: ceftriaxone + vancomycin, plus acyclovir until viral is ruled out, steroids); viral is milder. Vaccine at 11–12, booster at 16. Encephalitis: viral (West Nile, HSV-1 is severe); acyclovir lowers HSV mortality. Brain abscess: pus from ear, tooth, mastoid or sinus; strep and staph aureus; drain plus antibiotics.
3A. TIAFocal, acute, one vessel; under 24 hours, no infarction, no residual effects. Stroke risk highest in the first month. Carotid: weakness, numbness, vision loss. Vertebrobasilar: blurry vision, vertigo, ataxia. Bell's palsy mimic. Dysphagia (G, swallowing, NPO) vs dysphasia (speech); contralateral = opposite. CT first, MRA, carotid Doppler, echo. Aspirin, clopidogrel, heparin or warfarin if cardiac, endarterectomy or angioplasty.
3B. Stroke & Ischemic Stroke87% ischemic, 13% hemorrhagic; leading cause of disability. Risks: HTN, atherosclerosis, tobacco, diabetes, OCs, age (higher risk, better recovery), males, sickle cell, A-fib. Non-contrast CT first: blood is white; never anticoagulate a bleed. Thrombotic (atherosclerosis, hypercoagulable) vs embolic (mural thrombus, A-fib, PFO, valve vegetation, mechanical valve, carotid plaque/bruit). Deficits within 1 minute; save the penumbra within 3 hours.
3C. Hemorrhagic Stroke & BleedsRuptured vessel; untreated HTN; 38% mortality; ages 30–60; edema and ICP → herniation. Prognosis: age, location and size, bleeding speed and midline shift. Worst headache of their life; meningeal signs. Epidural (skull/dura, arterial, fracture), subdural (venous, slow), subarachnoid (CSF, bloody LP about 12 hours, aneurysm or AVM; BP parameters, clip or coil, anticonvulsant, CCB, Triple H). BE FAST; last known normal.
3D. Stroke Treatment & ConsequencesABCs (GCS below 8 → intubate), blood sugar, non-contrast CT first. Hemorrhagic: reverse anticoagulation, manage BP and ICP (HOB up, antipyretics, neutral head). Ischemic: alteplase (plasminogen → plasmin; watch BP, HR, rhythm, intracranial bleeding; often with heparin; antidote aminocaproic acid only for life-threatening bleeds) or mechanical thrombectomy. Swallow study before food; neglect; flaccid → spastic → contractures within 6 weeks; homonymous hemianopia; aphasia; depression; memory and behavior changes.

What is ALS also called, and what usually causes death?
Lou Gehrig's disease; respiratory failure from progressive muscle weakness.
Which neurons degenerate in ALS?
Upper and lower motor neurons (so the voluntary muscles are affected).
What is the excitotoxicity hypothesis of ALS?
Excessive glutamate starts a series of events that eventually kills the neuron.
What must be monitored with riluzole?
Liver function (it is hepatotoxic).
What does GBS damage?
The myelin sheath of peripheral nerves (autoimmune demyelination).
What usually triggers GBS?
A viral infection days to weeks earlier, typically respiratory or GI.
Which way does GBS weakness spread, and which way does recovery go?
Weakness ascends from the legs up; recovery goes from the top down.
What are the acute-phase treatments for GBS?
High-dose steroids (titrated down) and IV immunoglobulin (IVIG).
Which bacteria most commonly cause meningitis in adults?
Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis.
What is a positive Kernig sign?
Pain when straightening the client's leg that is flexed 90° at the hip and knee.
What signals meningococcal meningitis on the skin?
A non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash.
What are the most common sources of a brain abscess?
Ear, tooth, mastoid or sinus infections.
What separates a TIA from a stroke?
TIA: under 24 hours, no infarction, no residual effects. Stroke: deficits beyond 24 hours.
Name the vertebrobasilar TIA presentations.
Blurry vision, vertigo, ataxia, motor or sensory changes.
Which antiplatelet drugs are used after a TIA?
Low-dose aspirin and clopidogrel (Plavix).
What does contralateral mean?
The opposite side of the body from the damage.
How does a patent foramen ovale cause a stroke?
A venous clot crosses from the right atrium to the left atrium and travels to the brain.
On a non-contrast CT, how do hemorrhagic and ischemic strokes look?
Hemorrhage shows white; ischemia shows as a darkened area.
What is hemorrhagic conversion?
Bleeding into brain tissue across a disrupted blood-brain barrier after reperfusion of an ischemic stroke.
Why is newly diagnosed A-fib especially risky?
The client hasn't been on anticoagulants long enough to be anticoagulated.
Which intracranial bleed is usually arterial and linked to skull fracture?
An epidural bleed.
Why can a subdural bleed show symptoms later?
It is venous, so blood may accumulate slowly.
What is the Triple H used for after a subarachnoid hemorrhage?
Keeping the brain perfused: pressors (BP), hypervolemia with colloids, and some hemodilution.
What is an AVM?
A jumbled artery-vein junction where arterial blood goes straight into the vein, bypassing capillaries.
At what GCS is a stroke client intubated?
Below 8.
Why check a blood sugar during a stroke alert?
Hypoglycemia can mimic a stroke.
Name three ways to manage ICP in hemorrhagic stroke.
Elevate the head of the bed, give antipyretics for fever, keep the head neutral.
When do flaccid muscles typically progress to contractures after a stroke?
Within the first 6 weeks.
