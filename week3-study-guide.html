Part 1 — Migraines

Topic 1: the definition and diagnostic criteria, types, risk factors and triggers, pathophysiology, the four phases, abortive therapy (analgesics, triptans, CGRP antagonists, antiemetics) and preventive therapy.

What a Migraine Is

Migraines are the most common complaint neurologists see day to day. A migraine is an episodic neurologic disorder characterized by a headache that lasts anywhere from 4 to 72 hours.

At least TWO of these

  • Unilateral head pain (characteristic of migraine)
  • Throbbing pain
  • Headache that worsens with activity
  • Pain rated moderate to severe

Plus at least ONE of these

  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Photophobia: sensitivity to (avoidance of) light, such as sunglasses, dark rooms, pulled curtains
  • Phonophobia: avoidance of sound; everything feels loud and grating

Types of Migraine

TypeWhat the Lecture Says
With auraAn aura is present (about a third of people with migraines).
Without auraNo aura. The most common kind of migraine.
ChronicMigraines on about 15 days a month over 3 months. They usually start out episodic, and their frequency increases over time until they reach that threshold.

Risk Factors & Triggers

Risk factors

  • Family history.
  • Estrogen and progesterone: females are more susceptible. Migraines can occur more right before and during menstruation, and often decrease during pregnancy and menopause. The theory is that cyclical withdrawal of these hormones over the menstrual cycle triggers migraines.
  • Other genetic and environmental factors.

Common triggers

  • Fatigue; sleep disturbances, meaning too little or too much sleep (e.g., 15 hours when that isn't normal)
  • Missing a meal; overexertion
  • Weather changes (rainy, cold weather)
  • Stress; hormonal changes
  • Alcohol (red wine is a common one)
  • Bright lights or strong smells (stores with strong odors)

Pathophysiology

Not well understood; there is no well-established, identifiable pathology. Migraines are associated with changes in brain metabolism and blood flow, and current theories involve changes in neurotransmitter levels and blood vessel tone.

StepWhat Happens
1Increased neuronal depolarization results in hyperactivity.
2Excitatory neurotransmitters are released in the brain.
3They activate the vascular system, stimulating vasodilation, which results in the migraine.

The Four Phases of a Migraine

PhaseWhen and WhoSymptoms
1. PremonitoryHours or days before the headache; about a third of people with migrainesFatigue, irritability, loss of concentration, stiff neck, food cravings.
2. AuraOnly in people with aura (about a third); lasts up to about an hourVisual: shimmering lights, flashing spots, blind spots. Sensory: numbness, tingling, pins and needles. Motor: weakness, difficulty with speech, coordination problems.
3. Headache4 to 72 hoursThrobbing pain, typically starting on one side and spreading to the whole head; fatigue, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, hypersensitivity to touch on the head.
4. RecoveryA few hours up to a few days to fully resolveIrritability, fatigue, depression.

Abortive (Symptomatic) Therapy

Abortive therapy treats the symptoms, and it is more effective when given early in the course of the headache.

SituationWhat Is Used
Mild to moderate, no severe nausea or vomitingBasic analgesics: aspirin, acetaminophen, NSAIDs, or a combination. Tried first because they can be really effective, are readily available and are inexpensive. If they don't work alone, a triptan is tried, and a triptan plus an analgesic can be more effective than either alone.
Moderate to severe, no nausea or vomitingAn oral migraine-specific agent: the triptans, often combined (e.g., sumatriptan with naproxen), since the combination is more effective.
Can't take or tolerate triptansA CGRP antagonist (calcitonin gene-related peptide antagonist).
Severe nausea and vomiting (any severity of migraine)Antiemetics are added, such as ondansetron or promethazine (Phenergan).

Triptans (Serotonin 1B/1D Receptor Agonists)

FeatureWhat the Lecture Says
How they workPromote vasoconstriction of the intracranial blood vessels (vasodilation has a role in causing migraine). They also suppress inflammatory neuropeptides in the brain and block the pain pathway.
RoutesSubcutaneous, PO or intranasal. Nasal spray has fewer side effects than subQ. SubQ works most quickly. PO has side effects similar to subQ, but they occur less often.

No triptans for clients with ischemic stroke, heart disease or angina. Triptans work by constricting blood vessels.

CGRP Antagonist: Rimegepant (Nurtec)

  • Used when triptans aren't effective or are contraindicated: CAD, heart disease, angina, ischemic stroke.
  • Given PO.
  • Side effects: GI upset (abdominal pain, nausea, dyspepsia).
  • A CYP substrate: use caution with any inhibitors or inducers of that system.

Preventive Therapy

When it's used

  • More than 4 migraines in a month, or
  • Migraines that last longer than 12 hours.

Drugs named

  • Beta blockers: propranolol
  • Tricyclic antidepressants: amitriptyline
  • Anti-epileptic drugs
  • Estrogen

Goals of preventive therapy

  • Reduce migraine frequency, severity and duration.
  • Improve the response to treatment of acute attacks.
  • Improve function and reduce disability.
  • Prevent episodic migraines from turning into chronic migraines.

Part 2A — Parkinson's Disease

Topic 2, first voiceover: what PD is, primary vs secondary parkinsonism, risk factors, the dopamine–acetylcholine imbalance, the clinical manifestations and the classic triad, and complications.

What Parkinson's Disease Is

Parkinson's disease (PD)

  • A complex motor disorder with other systemic non-motor and neurologic symptoms.
  • Progressive and degenerative: it keeps getting worse, and some symptoms worsen faster than others.
  • Characterized by tremors, rigidity and bradykinesia. The tremor is notably more present when the muscles are at rest.
  • A disorder of the basal ganglia, which help produce smooth, coordinated movements (the lecture places them in the cerebellum). The concern is the substantia nigra inside the basal ganglia, which contains the cells that produce dopamine.

Primary vs Secondary Parkinsonism

Parkinsonism is the umbrella term. Parkinson's disease is the leading cause of parkinsonism.

Primary parkinsonism

  • The etiology is unknown (idiopathic); this is Parkinson's disease.
  • Also includes genetic components and sporadic onset.
  • Defining feature: no defining event led to the onset of symptoms.
  • There is damage to the dopamine-producing cells.

Secondary (acquired) parkinsonism

  • Results from something else: an event that caused the onset.
  • Infection, head trauma, drug intoxication.
  • Some antiemetics and some antidepressants are the most common cause. Symptoms usually go away after the medication is stopped.
  • Any drug that interferes with dopamine transmission can cause it.

Dopamine, Acetylcholine & the Imbalance

Dopamine

  • Has both inhibitory and excitatory effects in the brain, depending on the receptor it binds.
  • In PD, it's the inhibitory effects that are lacking.
  • Helps control movement and balance by inhibiting unnecessary movements, which improves coordination, balance and muscle control.

Acetylcholine (ACh)

  • An excitatory neurotransmitter: stimulates muscle movement and can cause uncoordinated movement.
  • Essential for all muscle function, and works with dopamine. It's all about the balance.
StepWhat Happens
1The substantia nigra in the basal ganglia is destroyed.
2Dopamine levels decrease, because the dopamine-producing cells are in the substantia nigra.
3An imbalance develops: a relative excess of acetylcholine.
4Extra muscle movements that dopamine would normally inhibit are no longer inhibited: loss of controlled movement and balance.

Clinical Manifestations

They may develop alone or in combination, and diagnosis often takes a while because onset is insidious. At first it may involve only one side of the body; it typically becomes bilateral as the disease progresses. Later, all of them appear.

Common signs

  • Bradykinesia: slowness of movement.
  • Cogwheel rigidity: stiff muscles; movements like bending the elbow aren't smooth.
  • Resting tremor.
  • Shuffling (Parkinsonian) gait: they don't lift their feet.
  • Mask-like expression: loss of small facial movements, a flat affect.
  • Postural instability.
The classic triad, the hallmark of PD: Tremor, Rigidity, Bradykinesia.

Tremor

  • Often the first sign. It is a rest tremor: most noticeable when the body part is supported against gravity but not doing purposeful activity, and it decreases with movement.
  • An early sign is the pill-rolling tremor: the thumb looks like it's rolling a small object against the finger. In the dominant hand, handwriting may suffer.
  • Worse with stress or concentration, such as trying to control the movement.
FeatureEssential TremorParkinson's Tremor
CauseFaulty neurological impulsesDopamine deficiency and the ACh imbalance
WhenOccurs with motor function; fine motor movements are really difficultOccurs at rest; tends to improve with movement
Other signsNo other manifestations of Parkinson'sComes with the other manifestations of Parkinson's

Rigidity

  • Resistance to passive movement: when the provider flexes the client's arm at the elbow, there's unintentional resistance.
  • Cogwheel rigidity: slow, jerky movements like a cogwheel.
  • Cause: excess acetylcholine stimulates the muscles continuously, so they never get to relax (sustained contraction). Clients report muscle soreness, aches and pains.
  • Often unilateral at first, becoming bilateral over time.

Bradykinesia

Loss of automatic movement, or slowness of movement. Arguably the major cause of disability in Parkinson's: a lack of the spontaneous movements we take for granted.

Lost Automatic MovementConsequence
BlinkingDry eye, more susceptible to abrasion.
Arm swing while walkingReduced or absent, adding to balance problems.
Swallowing salivaDrooling.
Facial micro-expressionsFlat affect, mask-like expression.

Postural Instability & Gait

  • Postural instability: a decreased ability to prevent falling while moving, from impaired postural reflexes.
  • Gait disturbances: short steps, or shuffling without picking up the feet.

Complications

ComplicationWhat the Lecture Says

FatigueFrom sleep disturbances: the tremor picks up while lying at rest trying to fall asleep.

AnxietyThe second most frequent psychiatric disturbance in PD, after depression.

Part 2B — Parkinson's Pharmacology

Topic 2, second voiceover: the goals of treatment, Sinemet and its loss of effect, adverse effects and interactions, Duopa, the dopamine agonists, rotigotine and apomorphine.

Goals & Strategy of Drug Therapy

  • Goal: help clients keep motor control as long as possible, decrease symptoms, and maintain as much independence as possible. Because PD is progressive and degenerative, treatment is staving it off as much as possible.
  • When to start: once symptoms affect the dominant hand or interfere with work, ADLs, or social or leisure activities.
  • Strategy: correct the imbalance, either by enhancing dopamine (dopaminergic drugs) or by blocking acetylcholine (anticholinergic drugs).

Sinemet (Levodopa-Carbidopa)

The gold standard of treatment for Parkinson's disease.

Levodopa

  • Once it reaches the brain, it is converted to dopamine and activates the dopamine receptors, increasing dopamine activity.

Carbidopa

  • Prevents levodopa from being broken down before it reaches the brain, so more dopamine is sustained for longer.
  • One of the most effective PD drugs, but it takes several months for symptoms to improve while dopamine builds up. Teach clients it can take up to months.
  • It does not work long-term: as PD progresses, fewer dopaminergic terminals survive to take up levodopa and convert it to dopamine, so the effect is lost.

Loss of Drug Effect

TypeWhat HappensWhat Helps
Abrupt loss ("on-off" phenomenon)Like flipping a switch: the drug is working, then suddenly it isn't and the symptoms are back. Impossible to predict, can happen at any point in the dosing interval, and over time the "off" episodes get more frequent and last longer.Other drugs and high-protein meals reduce the effect: do a medication reconciliation and teach diet changes.
Gradual loss ("wearing off")Each dose wears off sooner and sooner, even as the dose is increased; symptoms gradually return. Differs between individuals.Shorter dosing intervals (taking it more often), or changes in the dose amount.

Part 3 — Myasthenia Gravis

Topic 3: what MG is, the neuromuscular junction attack, clinical manifestations, myasthenic crisis, immunosuppressants and cholinesterase inhibitors, cholinergic crisis, and how to tell the two crises apart.

What Myasthenia Gravis Is

Myasthenia gravis (MG)

  • An acquired autoimmune disease characterized by fluctuating weakness of certain muscle groups.
  • Commonly affected: ocular muscles (the eyes), bulbar muscles (brainstem region), and the limb and respiratory muscle groups.
  • The weakness comes from an antibody-mediated attack on the postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular junction: a breakdown in communication between nerve cells and muscle cells.

How It Progresses

  • Variable from person to person. It often appears first as a mild, insidious case that's easy to push off ("just a little muscle weakness").
  • It can spontaneously go into remission (back to baseline), stabilize (plateau), or cycle through relapses, stable periods and symptom-free periods lasting weeks to months.
  • It can also progress severely and rapidly.
  • Because the course is hard to predict, being honest up front with clients about that is key.

Who Gets It

  • Rare; more common in females and in people aged 10 to 65 (a broad range).
  • Why the antibodies attack isn't known; there seems to be a genetic component and possible environmental triggers.
  • Manifestations often first appear during pregnancy or postpartum, or after exposure to certain types of anesthesia.

What Happens at the Neuromuscular Junction

StepWhat Happens
1Antibodies form against the nicotinic acetylcholine postsynaptic receptors at the neuromuscular junction of skeletal muscle; the receptors are no longer recognized as self.
2Anti-acetylcholine antibodies attach to the receptor sites and block them from binding acetylcholine.
3Eventually the antibodies destroy the receptor sites.
4The body makes enough acetylcholine, but there aren't enough sites for it to bind, so nerve impulse transmission to the muscle is diminished.

Acetylcholine is an excitatory neurotransmitter responsible for muscle contraction (the lecture says smooth muscle). It also has a role in vasodilation, increasing body secretions, and slowing heart rate, which matters for the drugs below.

Clinical Manifestations

AreaWhat the Lecture Says
PatternInsidious onset with fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness; clients can feel weakness starting, rest, and the strength comes back. Symptoms can be vague.
Eye, face, mouth, throat, neckOften affected first: drooling, difficulty chewing and swallowing, which affects nutrition and raises aspiration risk.
Respiratory musclesWeak diaphragm and intercostal muscles can impair the ability to breathe or ventilate appropriately.
Shoulders and hipsNot typically affected, but muscle fatigue after exercise can occur there.
The video (Carrie)Slow speech, slow blinking, a tracheostomy to help clear secretions, a possibly weak cough, difficulty swallowing.

Myasthenic Crisis

An acute exacerbation of muscle weakness

  • Can develop as MG progresses, typically triggered by a stressor: infection, surgery, emotional distress, pregnancy, a menstrual cycle, or inadequate pharmacotherapy or other drugs (not on enough medication, or not taking it appropriately).
  • In the second video, a simple cold sent Carrie into crisis: difficulty expanding her lungs, difficulty breathing, needing oxygen.
Myasthenic crisis: watch the respiratory muscles. The big concern is respiratory insufficiency, and these clients are at higher risk for respiratory arrest.

Drug Therapy

Drug therapy is not a cure; the hope is to alter and alleviate symptoms. Several administration routes can be used.

Immunosuppressants

  • MG is autoimmune, so immunosuppressants, particularly steroids, are used frequently to calm the immune response.

Cholinesterase inhibitors

  • Cholinesterase is the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine.
  • Blocking it lets acetylcholine stay in the synaptic junction longer, so it can hopefully find a receptor the antibodies haven't damaged.
  • They also intensify the effects of the acetylcholine released, hopefully increasing muscle strength.

Neostigmine

  • A cholinesterase inhibitor (anticholinesterase drug), so essentially a cholinergic drug, used to treat MG symptoms.
  • Facilitates impulse transmission across the neuromuscular junction, increasing cholinergic action.
  • At therapeutic levels it affects both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.

Nicotinic receptors

  • Adrenal and skeletal muscle.
  • These are the receptors affected by MG.

Muscarinic receptors

  • Sweat glands and other glands (and the effects on the GI tract, bladder, heart, lungs and eyes below).
  • Neostigmine stimulates these too, which causes most of its side effects.

Cholinergic vs Anticholinergic Effects

Body SystemCholinergic (Neostigmine)Anticholinergic (Atropine)

Use the symptoms to pick the drug: symptomatic bradycardia → an anticholinergic like atropine raises the heart rate. Atropine is also used to dry up secretions, for example for clients on comfort care or hospice who are choking on secretions they can't swallow.

SLUDGE: the cholinergic side effects. Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Diaphoresis, GI distress, Emesis.

Adverse Effects of Neostigmine

Muscarinic stimulation

  • Increased secretions and GI motility (diarrhea).
  • Urinary urgency.
  • Slowed heart rate.
  • Bronchoconstriction: consider carefully before giving to clients with asthma or other lung disease.
  • Miosis.

Nicotinic (neuromuscular) stimulation

  • Therapeutic dose: increased muscle contraction, the desired effect.
  • Toxic dose: decreased contraction; the overstimulated muscles wear out and can fail, leading to a cholinergic crisis.

Cholinergic Crisis

  • Extreme muscle weakness or paralysis, plus signs of excessive muscarinic stimulation: increased intestinal motility (diarrhea, cramping), bradycardia, pupil constriction, lots of saliva, diaphoresis.
  • It arises from anticholinesterase medications: essentially a drug toxicity, from excessive acetylcholine accumulation.
  • Risk of respiratory arrest; the client may need mechanical ventilation.
  • Antidote for the muscarinic signs: atropine (an anticholinergic).
Teach clients with MG to wear a medical alert bracelet at all times.

Myasthenic Crisis vs Cholinergic Crisis

Both present with respiratory insufficiency and extreme muscle weakness, heading the same direction for opposite reasons.

Typical causeA stressor, or inadequate medicationAnticholinesterase drug toxicity

Edrophonium is a very short-acting cholinesterase inhibitor that raises acetylcholine temporarily. Give it when one of the two crises is suspected: improvement means myasthenic crisis; worsening means cholinergic crisis.

Part 4 — Multiple Sclerosis

Topic 4: what MS is, risk factors, pathophysiology, the types of MS, clinical manifestations, disease-modifying drugs, acute relapse treatment and symptom management.

What Multiple Sclerosis Is

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

  • A chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease involving degeneration of CNS myelin (loss of the myelin sheath), scarring (sclerosis or plaque formation) and loss of axons.
  • A progressive disease, with several types.
  • Features: lots of inflammation, especially in the white matter of the brain and spinal cord; demyelination of nerve fibers; and scar tissue called gliosis. Glial cells myelinate neurons in the CNS; on imaging, the white spots are areas of scar tissue.
  • Demyelinating disorders damage the myelin sheath and impair neural signal transmission. MS affects the central nervous system; Guillain-Barré affects the peripheral nervous system (a later lecture).

Who Gets MS

The etiology is largely unknown, most likely a combination of genetic predisposition, an immune system problem and an environmental trigger (such as an infection).

Risk FactorWhat the Lecture Says
AgeOnset typically between 20 and 40.
SexMore common in females, but males who get MS typically have a more severe, progressive course.
LocationHigher prevalence in moderately cool climates, especially the northern U.S.: above the 40th parallel north and below the 40th parallel south. Areas in between have lower prevalence.
RacePeople who are white are more likely to have MS.
Family historyIncreases risk.
Possible (associated)Smoking, vitamin D deficiency, obesity, infection. Not concrete risk factors, but there is evidence of an association.

Pathophysiology

MS is a diffuse, progressive CNS autoimmune inflammatory disease affecting white and gray matter throughout the brain and spinal cord. The attack targets the myelin sheath and involves T lymphocytes and antibodies.

StepWhat Happens
1T lymphocytes migrate to the CNS, cross the blood-brain barrier, and increase inflammation and breakdown of the myelin sheath.
2An antigen-antibody reaction in the CNS attacks the myelin, releasing inflammatory mediators.
3The oligodendrocytes (myelin-producing cells) are lost.
4Axons are demyelinated, and plaque and scar tissue form (sclerotic lesions).
5The axons die: nerve signals are first delayed, then lost. Neurons die, and the brain atrophies overall.
Stage of the NerveWhat It Looks LikeEffect on the Impulse
NormalAn axon wrapped in an intact myelin sheath.Quick, almost instantaneous transmission.
Inflamed (early)The nerve fiber is inflamed but intact.Still transmitted, maybe a little slower; the client may notice some weakness.
DetachedThe myelin detaches and the underlying axon is damaged; scarring and axon death follow.Completely blocked: a permanent loss of function.

By diagnosis, clients are usually quite symptomatic. Looking back, many realize they'd had weakness much longer than they thought, but brushed it off until they couldn't ignore it.

Types of MS

Most types have exacerbations (relapses or flares): a temporary worsening of symptoms, mild or serious, lasting several days to several weeks, which may improve and may be followed by progressively worsening symptoms.

TypeThe Pattern as Described
Benign MSExacerbations, but after each flare the client returns to baseline: no loss of function.
Relapsing-remitting (RRMS)The most common type, about 80–90% of cases. Long remissions with a few exacerbations; pretty stable between them, with minimal disability or progression. They don't necessarily return fully to baseline: a little weaker after each exacerbation, but not as dramatic as other types.
Primary progressive (PPMS)Gradual, steady progression from the start, with no periods of remission or exacerbation (no flare-ups).
Secondary progressive (SPMS)Starts with relapses and remissions, with some loss of function each time, then shifts to a more progressive course without remissions.
Progressive relapsing (PRMS)Gradual progression of symptoms with exacerbations, and fewer remissions overall.
  • The type is determined by how often exacerbations happen and how long remissions last.
  • Relapses vary: for about 20% of people they last less than a week; for others 1 to 2 weeks, or 2 weeks to a month.
  • If relapses get shorter after starting treatment (e.g., from a month down to a week or less), that suggests the medication is working.

Clinical Manifestations

No finding is unique to MS, but some are highly characteristic. The big takeaway: symptoms depend on which nerves are affected.

Common initial symptoms

  • Paresthesias of the face, trunk and limbs; numbness, tingling, weakness, loss of muscle function.
  • Optic neuritis and unilateral vision loss.
  • Impaired gait.
  • Urinary incontinence.
  • Some develop seizures.

Symptoms to monitor closely

  • Cognitive "cog fog": difficulty thinking clearly and concentrating, not necessarily a memory problem.
  • Vision problems.
  • Depression, related to loss of function.
  • Fatigue: needs more rest periods.
  • Pain: chronic (continuous) or acute (with exacerbations); burning or stabbing.
  • Bowel: constipation most common, possibly incontinence. Bladder: incontinence or retention.
  • Sexual dysfunction; muscle stiffness or spasms.
  • Walking and balance issues, vertigo: high fall risk; some eventually need a wheelchair.
Clients with MS are at high risk for falls. Most have walking and balance issues, and some have vertigo.

Goals of Drug Therapy

  • There is no cure. Goals: slow disability and keep the client functioning at the highest possible capacity.
  • Decrease relapse frequency, lengthen remissions, and reduce new brain lesions (which lead to more dysfunction and symptoms).
  • Three jobs: modify the disease process, treat acute exacerbations, and manage symptoms.

Treating an Acute Relapse

TreatmentWhat the Lecture Says
High-dose IV glucocorticoids ("pulse-dose steroids")MS is autoimmune, so very high doses calm the immune response: about 500 mg to 1 g daily for 3 to 5 days (vs 40 to 60 mg for asthma). Not for long-term or frequent use; usually reserved for exacerbations. They raise blood glucose, so diabetic clients may need extra insulin or an insulin infusion.
IV gamma globulin (IVIG)For clients who can't tolerate high-dose glucocorticoids.
ACTH (H.P. Acthar Gel)An adrenocorticotropic hormone gel with prolonged ACTH release after injection. Mechanism not clear, but it treats acute relapses. For clients who can't tolerate glucocorticoids, or when steroids aren't (or weren't) effective.
PlasmapheresisA dialysis-like process: blood is removed through a catheter, the plasma is separated from the red cells and replaced with donor plasma, and the blood is reinfused.

Whole-Week Quick Reference

One row per part: the points to have cold before an exam. Every detail is taught in full in the parts above.

PartWhat to Remember
1. Migraines4 to 72 hours; at least two of unilateral, throbbing, worse with activity, moderate to severe, plus at least one of nausea and vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia. Without aura is most common; chronic = 15 days a month for 3 months. Vasodilation drives the pain. Abortive: analgesics first, then triptans (vasoconstrict; never with ischemic stroke, heart disease or angina; subQ fastest), rimegepant if triptans can't be used, antiemetics for severe vomiting. Preventive (more than 4 a month or longer than 12 hours): propranolol, amitriptyline, anti-epileptics, estrogen.
2A. Parkinson's DiseaseSubstantia nigra loss → low dopamine → relative excess acetylcholine. Triad: rest tremor (pill-rolling, better with movement, worse with stress), rigidity (cogwheel) and bradykinesia (the major cause of disability: no blinking, no arm swing, drooling, masked face), plus shuffling gait and postural instability. Antiemetics and antidepressants are the most common cause of secondary parkinsonism. Risks: age over 50, males, pesticides, head trauma, low estrogen; coffee protects. About 40% develop dementia (Lewy bodies); aspiration, UTIs, skin breakdown.
2B. Parkinson's PharmacologyCorrect the imbalance: boost dopamine or block acetylcholine. Sinemet is the gold standard (levodopa → dopamine; carbidopa protects levodopa); months to work; on-off and wearing-off loss of effect; avoid high protein; take with a little food; orthostatic hypotension, dyskinesias, 20% psychosis, dark sweat and urine, skin check for melanoma. B6, antipsychotics and protein decrease it; MAOIs and anticholinergics push it toward toxicity. Duopa: J-tube gel up to 16 hours a day, no MAOI within 2 weeks, sudden sleep. Pramipexole: early or young monotherapy, restless legs, gambling and compulsions. Apomorphine for "off" episodes.
3. Myasthenia GravisAutoimmune attack on nicotinic ACh receptors at the neuromuscular junction (ACh supply is fine). Fluctuating weakness that improves with rest; eyes and face first; chewing and swallowing (aspiration); respiratory muscles. Myasthenic crisis = too little ACh (triggers: infection, surgery, stress, pregnancy, menses, too little medication). Cholinergic crisis = neostigmine toxicity (SLUDGE, bradycardia, miosis), and the antidote is atropine. Edrophonium: improves a myasthenic crisis, worsens a cholinergic one. Medical alert bracelet.
4. Multiple SclerosisT cells cross the blood-brain barrier and attack CNS myelin; oligodendrocytes are lost; scarring; axon death means permanent loss. Age 20–40, female (males worse), cool climates, white, family history. RRMS is most common (80–90%); PPMS has no flares. Symptoms depend on the nerves affected: paresthesias, optic neuritis, cog fog, fatigue, bladder and bowel problems, falls. Interferon (flu-like, liver, marrow, depression), glatiramer (post-injection reaction), fingolimod, dimethyl fumarate, natalizumab (PML; TOUCH; monotherapy). Relapse: pulse IV steroids (watch glucose), IVIG, ACTH, plasmapheresis.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer. Four cards from each of the five parts above: a whole-week spot check.

Which hormones' cyclical withdrawal is thought to trigger migraines?
Estrogen and progesterone.
Name two common migraine triggers.
Any two of: fatigue, too little or too much sleep, a missed meal, overexertion, weather changes, stress, hormonal changes, alcohol, bright lights or strong smells.
When is abortive migraine therapy most effective?
Early in the course of the headache.
What CYP caution applies to rimegepant (Nurtec)?
Use caution with CYP inhibitors or inducers (it's a CYP substrate).
What is primary parkinsonism?
Idiopathic parkinsonism with no defining event (Parkinson's disease).
What is the pill-rolling tremor?
The thumb looks like it's rolling a small object against the finger: an early PD rest tremor.
What causes the rigidity in Parkinson's?
Excess acetylcholine keeps stimulating the muscles, so they can't relax.
What is the second most frequent psychiatric disturbance in PD?
Anxiety (after depression).
How long does Sinemet take to improve symptoms?
Up to several months.
What is the "on-off" phenomenon?
An abrupt, unpredictable loss of drug effect: the symptoms suddenly return.
Within how long of an MAOI must Duopa not be taken?
2 weeks.
Which PD drug is also used for restless leg syndrome?
Pramipexole (Mirapex).
Which receptors does myasthenia gravis affect?
Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction.
Name two times MG manifestations often first appear.
Any two of: during pregnancy, postpartum, or after certain anesthesia.
Why use caution with neostigmine in a client with asthma?
Muscarinic stimulation causes bronchoconstriction.
Atropine is given in a cholinergic crisis. What else is it used for, per the lecture?
Symptomatic bradycardia, and drying secretions (e.g., in hospice).
Above which latitude is MS prevalence higher?
The 40th parallel (north, and below the 40th south).
What happens to the nerve impulse once the axon is damaged?
It is completely blocked: a permanent loss of function.
Which oral MS drug keeps lymphocytes in the lymph nodes?
Fingolimod.
What dose range is a pulse-dose steroid for an MS relapse?
About 500 mg to 1 g daily for 3 to 5 days.
