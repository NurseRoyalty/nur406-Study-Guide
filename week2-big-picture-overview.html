The Lectures' Own Order, Applied to the Two Topics

Each topic page holds two voiceovers, and each voiceover builds directly on the one before it.

StepWhere It LivesThe Common Thread
The Scale and the AcidsTopic 1, Acid-Base Balance (the sections from "What pH Is" through "Volatile vs Non-Volatile Acids")pH is a measure of hydrogen ions, inversely and logarithmically. The serum has to stay between 7.35 and 7.45, because pH reaches enzymes, electrolytes, hormones and oxygen delivery. Acids come in two kinds: carbonic acid, which the lungs can blow off as CO2, and the non-volatile acids, which only the kidneys can remove.
The DefensesTopic 1, Acid-Base Balance (from "The Three Lines of Defense" to the end)Three lines of defense on three timescales: buffers in seconds, lungs in minutes to hours, kidneys in hours to days. The carbonic acid equation ties them together: the lungs pull on the CO2 end, and the kidneys pull on the hydrogen and bicarb end. When one side is the problem, the other side compensates.
Reading the GasTopic 2, ABGs & ABG Interpretation (from "Why We Draw ABGs" through "Metabolic Acidosis")The ABG measures the equation's two ends: PaCO2 is the respiratory side and HCO3 is the metabolic side. That gives four disorders, each with a primary event behind it (hypoventilation, hyperventilation, vomiting, diarrhea and so on), its own signs, and its own fix.
Putting It TogetherTopic 2, ABGs & ABG Interpretation (from "How Compensation Works" to the end)A four-step method (pH, PaCO2, HCO3, compensation), four levels of compensation, worked examples, and two shortcuts. Everything from the first three voiceovers is applied here.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, follow the lecture order. The interpretation steps only make sense once ROME and the carbonic acid equation do.

The Two Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1Acid-Base BalanceMore hydrogen ions mean a lower pH, and each step on the scale is tenfold. Serum pH must stay at 7.35–7.45 (below 6.8 or above 7.8 is incompatible with life); acidic blood releases oxygen to tissues, alkalotic blood holds on to it. Buffers (bicarbonate–carbonic acid outside cells; phosphate and protein inside) work instantly but briefly; the lungs control carbonic acid by changing rate and depth; the kidneys control bicarb, take hours to days, and handle acidosis better than alkalosis. In acidosis, potassium leaves the cells.
2ABGs & ABG InterpretationKnow pH 7.35–7.45, PaCO2 35–45 and HCO3 24–29. ROME sorts the four disorders: respiratory acidosis (hypoventilation, overdose, COPD), respiratory alkalosis (hyperventilation, panic, high vent settings), metabolic acidosis (renal failure, DKA, diarrhea, lactic acid) and metabolic alkalosis (vomiting, NG suction, loop diuretics, antacids). Interpret in four steps and grade compensation as uncompensated, partial, compensated or corrected.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

The danger callouts and emphasized statements from both pages, condensed to a phrase, one row per topic. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation. Click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Acid-Base BalanceA pH below 6.8 or above 7.8 is incompatible with life; outside 7.35–7.45, get back to normal as quickly as possible. Avoid alkalosis in an ill patient (hemoglobin holds on to oxygen). Acidosis can cause hyperkalemia and dysrhythmias, especially with kidney dysfunction; as the pH corrects, working kidneys can leave the patient hypokalemic. The kidneys handle alkalosis poorly. Renal failure patients can't maintain pH without help.
ABGs & ABG InterpretationUse an ABG, not a VBG, when treatment changes depend on the result in a critically ill patient. Metabolic alkalosis can cause hypokalemia and cardiac issues. Ventilator settings can cause respiratory alkalosis. Respiratory acidosis from overdose or oversedation brings dysrhythmias and a falling level of consciousness, up to coma. Metabolic acidosis brings hypotension and hyperkalemia. A COPD patient can tip from compensated to acidotic when breathing worsens (an infection), because the kidneys can't catch up quickly.

Patterns You'll Notice Across These Rules

"Fast fixes are short; slow fixes last." Buffers act in seconds but only make small, temporary changes; cellular compensation is temporary; sodium bicarb in the ICU is a quick fix. The lungs act in minutes, but can't compensate for long. The kidneys take hours to days but are the long-term regulators.

"The organ that isn't the problem does the compensating." A lung problem is compensated by the kidneys (bicarb moves); a metabolic problem is compensated by the lungs (CO2 moves). That's why, in every disorder, the compensating value moves in the same direction as the primary change.

"Breathing is both the cause and the cure." Hypoventilation causes respiratory acidosis and is how the lungs compensate for metabolic alkalosis; hyperventilation causes respiratory alkalosis and is how the lungs compensate for metabolic acidosis (Kussmaul breathing). The treatments follow: a paper bag or calming for too much breathing, stimulation, deep breathing or a higher vent rate for too little.

"Potassium follows hydrogen in the opposite direction." Acidosis drives hydrogen into cells and potassium out (hyperkalemia); alkalosis pulls hydrogen out and potassium in (hypokalemia). Both can affect the heart.

"Chronic looks calmer than acute." A chronic problem gives the kidneys time, so the pH drifts back toward normal (the compensated COPD patient). An acute problem doesn't, so the pH swings (the respiratory arrest, the panic attack, the COPD patient with a new infection).
