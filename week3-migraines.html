Migraines

This page follows the migraine voiceover: the definition and diagnostic criteria, the types, risk factors and triggers, the (still poorly understood) pathophysiology, the four phases, and the two kinds of drug therapy: abortive (symptomatic) and preventive.

What a Migraine Is

Migraines are the most common complaint neurologists see day to day. A migraine is an episodic neurologic disorder characterized by a headache that lasts anywhere from 4 to 72 hours.

At least TWO of these

  • Unilateral head pain (characteristic of migraine)
  • Throbbing pain
  • Headache that worsens with activity
  • Pain rated moderate to severe

Plus at least ONE of these

  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Photophobia: sensitivity to (avoidance of) light, such as sunglasses, dark rooms, pulled curtains
  • Phonophobia: avoidance of sound; everything feels loud and grating

Types of Migraine

TypeWhat the Lecture Says
With auraAn aura is present (about a third of people with migraines).
Without auraNo aura. The most common kind of migraine.
ChronicMigraines on about 15 days a month over 3 months. They usually start out episodic, and their frequency increases over time until they reach that threshold.

Risk Factors & Triggers

Risk factors

  • Family history.
  • Estrogen and progesterone: females are more susceptible. Migraines can occur more right before and during menstruation, and often decrease during pregnancy and menopause. The theory is that cyclical withdrawal of these hormones over the menstrual cycle triggers migraines.
  • Other genetic and environmental factors.

Common triggers

  • Fatigue; sleep disturbances, meaning too little or too much sleep (e.g., 15 hours when that isn't normal)
  • Missing a meal; overexertion
  • Weather changes (rainy, cold weather)
  • Stress; hormonal changes
  • Alcohol (red wine is a common one)
  • Bright lights or strong smells (stores with strong odors)

Pathophysiology

Not well understood; there is no well-established, identifiable pathology. Migraines are associated with changes in brain metabolism and blood flow, and current theories involve changes in neurotransmitter levels and blood vessel tone.

StepWhat Happens
1Increased neuronal depolarization results in hyperactivity.
2Excitatory neurotransmitters are released in the brain.
3They activate the vascular system, stimulating vasodilation, which results in the migraine.

The Four Phases of a Migraine

PhaseWhen and WhoSymptoms
1. PremonitoryHours or days before the headache; about a third of people with migrainesFatigue, irritability, loss of concentration, stiff neck, food cravings.
2. AuraOnly in people with aura (about a third); lasts up to about an hourVisual: shimmering lights, flashing spots, blind spots. Sensory: numbness, tingling, pins and needles. Motor: weakness, difficulty with speech, coordination problems.
3. Headache4 to 72 hoursThrobbing pain, typically starting on one side and spreading to the whole head; fatigue, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, hypersensitivity to touch on the head.
4. RecoveryA few hours up to a few days to fully resolveIrritability, fatigue, depression.

Abortive (Symptomatic) Therapy

Abortive therapy treats the symptoms, and it is more effective when given early in the course of the headache.

SituationWhat Is Used
Mild to moderate, no severe nausea or vomitingBasic analgesics: aspirin, acetaminophen, NSAIDs, or a combination. Tried first because they can be really effective, are readily available and are inexpensive. If they don't work alone, a triptan is tried, and a triptan plus an analgesic can be more effective than either alone.
Moderate to severe, no nausea or vomitingAn oral migraine-specific agent: the triptans, often combined (e.g., sumatriptan with naproxen), since the combination is more effective.
Can't take or tolerate triptansA CGRP antagonist (calcitonin gene-related peptide antagonist).
Severe nausea and vomiting (any severity of migraine)Antiemetics are added, such as ondansetron or promethazine (Phenergan).

Triptans (Serotonin 1B/1D Receptor Agonists)

FeatureWhat the Lecture Says
How they workPromote vasoconstriction of the intracranial blood vessels (vasodilation has a role in causing migraine). They also suppress inflammatory neuropeptides in the brain and block the pain pathway.
RoutesSubcutaneous, PO or intranasal. Nasal spray has fewer side effects than subQ. SubQ works most quickly. PO has side effects similar to subQ, but they occur less often.
Side effectsInjection site reactions (subQ), chest pressure, flushing, weakness, headache; a bad taste with the nasal form. These make sense for a drug that constricts blood vessels.
Avoid inIschemic stroke, heart disease or angina.
No triptans for clients with ischemic stroke, heart disease or angina. Triptans work by constricting blood vessels.

CGRP Antagonist: Rimegepant (Nurtec)

  • Used when triptans aren't effective or are contraindicated: CAD, heart disease, angina, ischemic stroke.
  • Given PO.
  • Side effects: GI upset (abdominal pain, nausea, dyspepsia).
  • A CYP substrate: use caution with any inhibitors or inducers of that system.

Preventive Therapy

When it's used

  • More than 4 migraines in a month, or
  • Migraines that last longer than 12 hours.

Drugs named

  • Beta blockers: propranolol
  • Tricyclic antidepressants: amitriptyline
  • Anti-epileptic drugs
  • Estrogen

Goals of preventive therapy

  • Reduce migraine frequency, severity and duration.
  • Improve the response to treatment of acute attacks.
  • Improve function and reduce disability.
  • Prevent episodic migraines from turning into chronic migraines.
