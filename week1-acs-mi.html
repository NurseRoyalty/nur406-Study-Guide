Acute Coronary Syndrome & Myocardial Infarction

This page follows the ACS voiceover: what ACS is, the spectrum from Prinzmetal's angina to STEMI, what makes plaque unstable and why it ruptures, unstable angina, how ACS differs from stable angina, the signs of MI, what happens to heart tissue minute by minute, the zones of damage, STEMI vs NSTEMI, and the LAD "widow-maker." The drugs are on the next page.

What ACS Is

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)

  • An umbrella term for situations in which the blood supply to the heart muscle is suddenly blocked or compromised.
  • It is certainly a medical emergency.
  • The blockage can come and go, or it can be sudden and complete. Either way, cardiac tissue is dying.
  • ACS covers three conditions: unstable angina, non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).
  • The three have a similar pathophysiology, so the signs and symptoms can look similar. The ECG is what helps determine the next step: send the patient to the cath lab for reperfusion, or treat medically?
"Time is tissue." The instructor says it several times: treat the blockage as quickly as possible to save as much tissue as possible.

What Makes Plaque Unstable

Usually, when plaque becomes unstable, people develop ACS. These are people who already have atherosclerotic plaque in their coronary vessels.

Stable plaque

  • A pretty thick fibrous cap on top of the fat, which contributes to its stability.

Unstable plaque

  • The lipid core (the fat inside the vessel) gets bigger.
  • As the core grows, the fibrous cap gets thinner.
  • There is a component of inflammation; C-reactive protein (CRP) is a marker of inflammation in the body.
  • Smooth muscle cells proliferate into the intima (the lecture describes the intima as the middle layer of the blood vessel). These cells create the fibrous cap and bring in inflammatory components, increasing the risk of plaque rupture.

Assessing Unstable Plaque

  • A study that puts dye into the vessel shows how much narrowing is happening, so we can roughly estimate whether the lipid core is larger or smaller.
  • CRP levels show how much inflammation is going on.

Why Plaque Ruptures

Rupture risk is higher with unstable plaque: a large lipid core, a thin fibrous cap, and ongoing inflammation. The slide walks through the theory step by step.

StepWhat Happens
1. TriggerPsychological stress, exercise, or circadian rhythms stimulate the sympathetic nervous system.
2. Sympathetic responseEpinephrine and norepinephrine are released: blood pressure and heart rate rise, and the heart works harder with stronger contractions.
3. Force on the vesselsPushing out more blood with stronger contractions puts more force on the vessels, including the coronary vessels themselves.
4. RuptureThat force can lead to rupture of the plaque.
5. PlateletsPlatelets come to the site and adhere to the ruptured plaque. They release substances that attract more platelets and can contribute to vasospasm.
6. ThrombusA thrombus forms in that same area.

Unstable Angina

What counts as unstable angina

  • Chest pain occurring for the first time. Consider it unstable immediately, until proven otherwise; it is a medical emergency because no pattern or history has been established.
  • In a patient who normally has stable angina, pain that is more severe than normal: for example, they have taken their first nitro and five minutes later the pain hasn't stopped or is getting worse.
  • Pain that is different: usually in the chest, but this time down one arm, down both arms, or radiating into the jaw.
  • It may happen at rest; the person may be awakened in the middle of the night.

What's happening in the vessel

  • The plaque ruptures and a thrombus forms. That rupture and clot formation is the real worry.
  • The size of the clot and of the blockage determine how much blood gets past to perfuse the tissue on the other side. A 30% occluded artery may still allow some flow around a clot; a 90% blockage leaves little room.
  • If there is no infarction, the blockage wasn't complete or the clot dissolved, and some tissue was still perfused.
  • The ECG changes and then goes back to normal, and we typically don't see troponin changes.
Unstable angina is a medical emergency. Teach patients to recognize the signs, recognize it as an emergency, and call 911 right now. They should most definitely not drive themselves to the ER.

Stable Angina vs ACS

Teach patients the difference so they can identify it and seek care. The big difference is the pain: its severity and its duration.

Stable angina

  • Pain should last about 5 minutes, and be gone by maybe about 15 minutes.
  • May feel like a little bit of chest tightness.
  • Flares up with strenuous activity.

ACS

  • Chest pain lasts a lot longer.
  • No relief with nitroglycerin.
  • More severe: the sensation of an elephant sitting on your chest.
  • Other symptoms: diaphoresis, shortness of air, nausea and vomiting.
  • An impending sense of doom: they know something is not right but can't pinpoint it. Take them very, very seriously.

Signs in Men and Women

Research over roughly the last 50 years shows different signs and symptoms of ACS between men and women. These symptoms aren't limited to one or the other.

Typically associated with men

  • Chest pain.
  • Tingling or discomfort in the back, arms, neck or jaw.

Other symptoms (often reported by women)

  • Dizziness, nausea, vomiting, tiredness.
  • Heartburn: a lot of women with ACS say they took some Tums that morning because they thought it was heartburn.

Some of these can feel similar to stable angina. The particular concern is when the experience changes or gets worse.

Signs & Symptoms of Myocardial Infarction

What the lecture lists

  • Diaphoresis and dyspnea.
  • Extreme anxiety; restless, can't sit still, moving around.
  • Impending sense of doom ("I know something's wrong") without being able to pinpoint it.
  • Levine sign: the fist to the chest, the "Hollywood" heart attack sign.
  • Pallor and weak pulses, from perfusion issues because the heart isn't working right.
  • Retrosternal (behind the sternum) crushing chest pain that can radiate to several parts of the body; the elephant on the chest.

Acute Myocardial Infarction

What an acute MI is

  • Ruptured plaque plus thrombus that severely reduces or completely blocks blood flow to the heart muscle.
  • Think of ACS as a continuum; MI is at the end of that continuum.
  • It is ACS with prolonged ischemia that results in the death of myocardial tissue: an irreversible ischemic necrosis. These cells are never going to function properly again.
  • Infarction happens either because blood flow disruption goes on over a very long period of time, or because of a complete and total blockage.
  • How much tissue is lost depends on: where the infarction is (smaller or larger vessel, left or right side of the heart), how long blood supply has been affected, and how complete the blockage is. Partial flow over a few days can damage tissue, and so can a more immediate, complete occlusion.
  • MIs are classified by ECG findings: STEMI and NSTEMI.

Ischemia, Infarction & ATP

Ischemia

  • When the oxygen supply does not meet the heart's metabolic demands.

Infarction

  • Tissue death that results from that ischemia.

Why ATP Matters

  • ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a molecule that stores and transfers energy in cells; it is the main energy source for cellular function.
  • Myocardial cells cannot store ATP. They must be continuously fed ATP, so they need a continuous supply of oxygen to make it.
  • When oxygen goes down, ATP supply goes down, but the heart can't stop beating while ATP isn't being produced.

What Happens to Heart Tissue Over Time

TimeWhat Happens
Acute ischemiaATP depletes instantly.
1 to 2 minutesDecreased ability of the heart to contract.
30 minutes up to 4 hoursIrreversible injury to cardiac tissue. By 4 hours, necrosis has begun and the tissue begins to die.
1 to 2 weeksThe necrotic tissue is cleared away; the myocardium is very weak and susceptible to rupture.
About 6 weeksThe necrotic tissue has been replaced by tough scar tissue.
Reperfusion: as quickly as possible, at least within 4 hours. Most guidelines state reperfusion therapy should be done within four hours. Within 30 minutes would be ideal, since irreversible injury can occur that quickly.

Scar tissue is thickened and tougher, so electrical impulses have a harder time traveling through it. That is why ECGs after an MI can show permanent changes.

Zones of Damage

ZoneWhat's HappeningCan It Recover?
InfarctionNecrosis; dead cells, where the MI happened.No. The goal is to stop it from increasing.
InjuryNot dead yet, but the tissue has suffered injury.Some recovery is still possible; it can be perfused and restored to be viable.
IschemiaThe outer zone.Full recovery is possible if it is perfused again.

The goal is to not extend the size of the infarct: increase oxygen and decrease the demand on the heart. The instructor compares this with an ischemic stroke: the same concept of tissue dying from lack of oxygen, where time is of the essence.

Three Factors That Decide the Extent of Damage

  • The location or level of the occlusion in the coronary artery.
  • The length of time it has been occluded.
  • The availability of collateral circulation. In people with atherosclerotic plaque the heart can start to develop collateral circulation. The lecture calls it "typically a bad thing," but here it creates ways to bypass the blockage and still get some oxygen and blood to the tissue, which can help limit the damage.

STEMI vs NSTEMI

The main difference is the ST segment on the ECG.

FeatureSTEMINSTEMI
ST segmentElevated.Normal, or a slight ST depression.
QRS complexUsually a wide QRS that develops over hours.Normal.
T wavePeaked, then over time less peaked, then inverted.Inverted the entire time.
TroponinElevated; still elevated after 2 hours, with a sharper increase than in NSTEMI.Elevated.
Infarct sizeTypically larger; more blockage, more tissue dying.Smaller.
OutcomesWorse.Better than STEMI.
The "tombstone." Nurses describe the ST elevation of a STEMI as looking like a tombstone, because the prognosis is not good if it isn't treated very quickly. Seeing it on an ECG should throw up red flags: investigate, and do it quickly.

On the ECG images, an NSTEMI can show ST depression or T wave inversion; a STEMI shows ST elevation.

Pericarditis can also show ST elevation and can look like an MI. See Myocarditis, Pericarditis & Cardiac Tamponade

The LAD: The "Widow-Maker"

Coronary blood supply named in the lecture

  • The right coronary artery.
  • The left main artery, which branches into the left circumflex artery and the left anterior descending (LAD) artery.
  • The LAD supplies almost the entire front side of the heart, much of the sidewall, and most of the left ventricle.
The LAD is the vessel most commonly involved in MI, and one of the most damaging. It is a really large vessel feeding most of the left ventricle, so a blockage there is particularly detrimental and often very lethal if not treated very quickly. An LAD MI is called a widow-maker heart attack because of its very poor prognosis if no one acts quickly.
