Week 5

Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new. It's a map of Week 5's three topics, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. The Comprehensive Study Guide is separate; it pulls the whole week together on its own page. Week 5 is all endocrine emergencies: each topic is a chronic endocrine disorder (diabetes, thyroid disease, adrenal insufficiency) pushed to a life-threatening extreme, usually by a stressor or by missed or stopped medication. No flashcards or quiz questions here, just the map.

The Lectures' Own Order, Applied to the Three Topics

StepWhere It LivesThe Common Thread
When Insulin FailsTopic 1, DKA & HHS (physiology and labs first, then presentation, treatment and resolution)Not enough working insulin plus rising counter-regulatory hormones. No insulin at all makes ketones (DKA); a little insulin stops the ketones but not the glucose (HHS). Either way, osmotic diuresis dehydrates the client, and potassium is the trap during treatment.
When the Thyroid Swings Too FarTopic 2, Thyroid Crisis (thyroid storm, then myxedema coma)The two ends of one spectrum: everything revved up (fever, tachycardia) or everything slowed down (hypothermia, bradycardia). The drugs either shut hormone down or put it back.
When the Adrenal Cortex Can't RespondTopic 3, Addisonian Crisis (Addison's disease and replacement first, then the crisis)No cortisol and no aldosterone, and no stress boost. Replacement has to be raised under stress and never stopped suddenly; the crisis is treated with hydrocortisone, saline and glucose.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, follow the lecture order. Read Addisonian crisis before the myxedema coma treatment if the hydrocortisone there seems to come out of nowhere: the coexisting adrenal insufficiency is the reason.

The Three Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1DKA & HHSDKA: type 1, under 65, fast (about 24 hours), ketones, elevated anion gap acidosis, fruity breath, Kussmaul respirations, glucose about 350–450. HHS: type 2, over 65, slow (days), no ketones, glucose over 1,000, hyperosmolarity and neuro changes. Treat with fluids first, then potassium, then IV insulin until the gap closes, adding dextrose at 200–250.
2Thyroid CrisisThyroid storm: fever 104–105, tachycardia above 140, hypertension then hypotension, death within 48 hours untreated; beta blocker, PTU, iodine an hour later, glucocorticoids, bile acid sequestrants. Myxedema coma: confusion more than coma, hypothermia, bradycardia, hypotension, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, hypoventilation; treat before labs with IV T4 plus T3 and IV hydrocortisone.
3Addisonian CrisisAddison's: sugar, salt and sex hormones all low; hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypotension, hypoglycemia, hyperpigmentation; lifelong hydrocortisone (plus fludrocortisone), stress dosing (3 x 3), never stop abruptly. Crisis: sudden pain, vomiting and diarrhea, hypotension, confusion; hydrocortisone 100 mg IV then 50 mg every 6 hours, normal saline, glucose.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

The danger callouts and safety statements from across the three pages, condensed to a phrase, one row per topic. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation. Click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
DKA & HHSFluids first. Check potassium before insulin: below 3.3, replace first (UK protocol: replace if below 5 before insulin). Insulin drives potassium into the cells, so watch for severe hypokalemia and EKG changes. Don't treat the early high potassium unless symptomatic. Hourly glucose; add dextrose at 200–250 so the glucose doesn't fall too fast. Keep insulin going until the anion gap closes. Aggressive fluids in HHS, especially in the elderly.
Thyroid CrisisThyroid storm: death within about 48 hours untreated; ICU; iodine only after the first PTU dose (about 1 hour). Myxedema coma: treat before lab confirmation; opioids and sedatives can trigger it; hypoventilation may need a ventilator; give hydrocortisone until adrenal insufficiency is ruled out.
Addisonian CrisisNever stop steroids abruptly (taper); increase the dose in stress (3 x 3 rule); keep injectable plus oral emergency supply; medical alert bracelet. In crisis: rapid hydrocortisone, saline and glucose; EKG for hyperkalemia.

Patterns You'll Notice Across These Rules

"Stress uncovers the weakness." Each crisis starts with a chronic disorder that was holding steady until infection, surgery, trauma or illness asked the body for more than it could give: more insulin, more thyroid control, more cortisol.

"Missed medication is a trigger too." Skipped insulin, irregular antithyroid drugs and abruptly stopped steroids each set off their own crisis.

"The treatment can create the next problem." Insulin drops the potassium, and dropping the glucose too fast causes hypoglycemia; that is why potassium comes before insulin and dextrose joins the drip.

"Opposite ends, mirror-image signs." Thyroid storm and myxedema coma flip almost every vital sign: fever vs hypothermia, tachycardia vs bradycardia.

"Fluids, electrolytes, glucose, monitor." Every crisis this week gets IV fluids, and close electrolyte, glucose and vital sign monitoring.
