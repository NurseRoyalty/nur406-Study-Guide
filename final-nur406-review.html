Myocarditis, Pericarditis & Cardiac Tamponade

Topic 1, the first lecture of NUR 406: myocarditis, then the pericardium, how inflammation leads to pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade, the bedside signs, pulsus paradoxus, and treatment.

Myocarditis

Think back to normal A&P: the myocardium is the muscle layer of the heart, the muscle cells. Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease of the myocardium.

FeatureWhat the Lecture Says
Range of severityA wide variety of symptoms: it can be a pretty mild disorder with little to no discomfort, all the way up to heart failure and a lethal condition.
What happens to the cellsThe inflammation comes with degeneration and necrosis of the cardiac myocytes. The inflammation causes direct injury to the myocytes, and over time it can create fibrosis and scarring that lead to structural changes as well.
ConductionWe often see a disruption in conduction through the heart. The tissue changes, inflammatory cytokines and T cell activation in the myocardium can result in abnormal electrical conduction.
CauseTypically viruses. In the United States and Europe, viruses are the leading cause of myocarditis. The Coxsackie virus A and B are the majority of cases.
TreatmentDepends on how severe the symptoms are. Treated by giving antivirals; anti-inflammatories can also be used.

The Pericardium & Pericardial Fluid

The pericardium is a sac-like membrane that surrounds the heart and the roots of the major blood vessels, and it helps the heart function properly. Between its inner and outer layers is the pericardial space.

StageFluid in the Pericardial SpaceWhat It Does to the Heart
NormalA small amount, about 15 to 50 mL.Acts like a lubricant between the inner and outer layers, for normal function of the heart.
Pericardial effusionWhen the pericardium is inflamed, fluid accumulates in excess of what is normal. Effusion is that excess fluid accumulation.Can compress the heart chambers and limit cardiac filling.
Cardiac tamponadeAbout 200 mL.The fluid compresses the heart. The chambers are restricted and can't stretch and fill with blood as they normally would.
Cardiac tamponade is a medical emergency. The fluid has to be removed quickly, because the heart chambers can't stretch and fill with blood.

Acute Pericarditis vs Cardiac Tamponade

The lecture describes what acute pericarditis looks like, then what changes once it progresses to cardiac tamponade and cardiac output falls.

Acute pericarditis

  • Chest pain: a sharp pain that worsens with deep breathing.
  • Fever, because of the inflammatory process.
  • Dyspnea (the lecture glosses it as "some painful breathing").
  • Pericardial friction rub on auscultation: a scratching sound, almost like rubbing on a balloon.
  • Muffled heart sounds, because you are listening to the heart pump through a layer of fluid.
  • ECG changes: elevations and also depressions.
  • Pulsus paradoxus is also named as a sign of acute pericarditis.

Cardiac tamponade

  • Signs of decreased cardiac output: hypotension, and the usual signs of a patient who is not being perfused appropriately, such as confusion and dizziness.
  • Jugular vein distension, from blood backing up in the system because of the decreased cardiac output.
  • Muffled heart sounds, from listening through a layer of fluid.
  • Pulsus paradoxus occurs in about 70 to 80 percent of patients experiencing cardiac tamponade.

Acute Coronary Syndrome

Topic 2, first half: what ACS is, the spectrum from Prinzmetal's angina to STEMI, what makes plaque unstable and why it ruptures, unstable angina, and how ACS differs from stable angina.

What ACS Is

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)

  • An umbrella term for situations in which the blood supply to the heart muscle is suddenly blocked or compromised.
  • It is certainly a medical emergency.
  • The blockage can come and go, or it can be sudden and complete. Either way, cardiac tissue is dying.
  • ACS covers three conditions: unstable angina, non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).
  • The three have a similar pathophysiology, so the signs and symptoms can look similar. The ECG is what helps determine the next step: send the patient to the cath lab for reperfusion, or treat medically?
"Time is tissue." The instructor says it several times: treat the blockage as quickly as possible to save as much tissue as possible.

What Makes Plaque Unstable

Usually, when plaque becomes unstable, people develop ACS. These are people who already have atherosclerotic plaque in their coronary vessels.

Stable plaque

  • A pretty thick fibrous cap on top of the fat, which contributes to its stability.

Unstable plaque

  • The lipid core (the fat inside the vessel) gets bigger.
  • As the core grows, the fibrous cap gets thinner.
  • There is a component of inflammation; C-reactive protein (CRP) is a marker of inflammation in the body.
  • Smooth muscle cells proliferate into the intima (the lecture describes the intima as the middle layer of the blood vessel). These cells create the fibrous cap and bring in inflammatory components, increasing the risk of plaque rupture.

Assessing Unstable Plaque

  • A study that puts dye into the vessel shows how much narrowing is happening, so we can roughly estimate whether the lipid core is larger or smaller.
  • CRP levels show how much inflammation is going on.

Why Plaque Ruptures

Rupture risk is higher with unstable plaque: a large lipid core, a thin fibrous cap, and ongoing inflammation. The slide walks through the theory step by step.

StepWhat Happens
1. TriggerPsychological stress, exercise, or circadian rhythms stimulate the sympathetic nervous system.
2. Sympathetic responseEpinephrine and norepinephrine are released: blood pressure and heart rate rise, and the heart works harder with stronger contractions.
3. Force on the vesselsPushing out more blood with stronger contractions puts more force on the vessels, including the coronary vessels themselves.
4. RuptureThat force can lead to rupture of the plaque.
5. PlateletsPlatelets come to the site and adhere to the ruptured plaque. They release substances that attract more platelets and can contribute to vasospasm.
6. ThrombusA thrombus forms in that same area.

Unstable Angina

What counts as unstable angina

  • Chest pain occurring for the first time. Consider it unstable immediately, until proven otherwise; it is a medical emergency because no pattern or history has been established.
  • In a patient who normally has stable angina, pain that is more severe than normal: for example, they have taken their first nitro and five minutes later the pain hasn't stopped or is getting worse.
  • Pain that is different: usually in the chest, but this time down one arm, down both arms, or radiating into the jaw.
  • It may happen at rest; the person may be awakened in the middle of the night.

What's happening in the vessel

  • The plaque ruptures and a thrombus forms. That rupture and clot formation is the real worry.
  • The size of the clot and of the blockage determine how much blood gets past to perfuse the tissue on the other side. A 30% occluded artery may still allow some flow around a clot; a 90% blockage leaves little room.
  • If there is no infarction, the blockage wasn't complete or the clot dissolved, and some tissue was still perfused.
  • The ECG changes and then goes back to normal, and we typically don't see troponin changes.
Unstable angina is a medical emergency. Teach patients to recognize the signs, recognize it as an emergency, and call 911 right now. They should most definitely not drive themselves to the ER.

Stable Angina vs ACS

Teach patients the difference so they can identify it and seek care. The big difference is the pain: its severity and its duration.

Stable angina

  • Pain should last about 5 minutes, and be gone by maybe about 15 minutes.
  • May feel like a little bit of chest tightness.
  • Flares up with strenuous activity.

ACS

  • Chest pain lasts a lot longer.
  • No relief with nitroglycerin.
  • More severe: the sensation of an elephant sitting on your chest.
  • Other symptoms: diaphoresis, shortness of air, nausea and vomiting.
  • An impending sense of doom: they know something is not right but can't pinpoint it. Take them very, very seriously.

Signs in Men and Women

Research over roughly the last 50 years shows different signs and symptoms of ACS between men and women. These symptoms aren't limited to one or the other.

Typically associated with men

  • Chest pain.
  • Tingling or discomfort in the back, arms, neck or jaw.

Other symptoms (often reported by women)

  • Dizziness, nausea, vomiting, tiredness.
  • Heartburn: a lot of women with ACS say they took some Tums that morning because they thought it was heartburn.

Some of these can feel similar to stable angina. The particular concern is when the experience changes or gets worse.

Myocardial Infarction

Topic 2, second half: the signs of MI, what an acute MI is, what happens to heart tissue over time, the zones of damage, STEMI vs NSTEMI, and the LAD.

Signs & Symptoms of Myocardial Infarction

What the lecture lists

  • Diaphoresis and dyspnea.
  • Extreme anxiety; restless, can't sit still, moving around.
  • Impending sense of doom ("I know something's wrong") without being able to pinpoint it.
  • Levine sign: the fist to the chest, the "Hollywood" heart attack sign.
  • Pallor and weak pulses, from perfusion issues because the heart isn't working right.
  • Retrosternal (behind the sternum) crushing chest pain that can radiate to several parts of the body; the elephant on the chest.

Acute Myocardial Infarction

What an acute MI is

  • Ruptured plaque plus thrombus that severely reduces or completely blocks blood flow to the heart muscle.
  • Think of ACS as a continuum; MI is at the end of that continuum.
  • It is ACS with prolonged ischemia that results in the death of myocardial tissue: an irreversible ischemic necrosis. These cells are never going to function properly again.
  • Infarction happens either because blood flow disruption goes on over a very long period of time, or because of a complete and total blockage.
  • How much tissue is lost depends on: where the infarction is (smaller or larger vessel, left or right side of the heart), how long blood supply has been affected, and how complete the blockage is. Partial flow over a few days can damage tissue, and so can a more immediate, complete occlusion.
  • MIs are classified by ECG findings: STEMI and NSTEMI.

Ischemia, Infarction & ATP

Ischemia

  • When the oxygen supply does not meet the heart's metabolic demands.

Infarction

  • Tissue death that results from that ischemia.

Why ATP Matters

  • ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a molecule that stores and transfers energy in cells; it is the main energy source for cellular function.
  • Myocardial cells cannot store ATP. They must be continuously fed ATP, so they need a continuous supply of oxygen to make it.
  • When oxygen goes down, ATP supply goes down, but the heart can't stop beating while ATP isn't being produced.

What Happens to Heart Tissue Over Time

TimeWhat Happens
Acute ischemiaATP depletes instantly.
1 to 2 minutesDecreased ability of the heart to contract.
30 minutes up to 4 hoursIrreversible injury to cardiac tissue. By 4 hours, necrosis has begun and the tissue begins to die.
1 to 2 weeksThe necrotic tissue is cleared away; the myocardium is very weak and susceptible to rupture.
About 6 weeksThe necrotic tissue has been replaced by tough scar tissue.
Reperfusion: as quickly as possible, at least within 4 hours. Most guidelines state reperfusion therapy should be done within four hours. Within 30 minutes would be ideal, since irreversible injury can occur that quickly.

Scar tissue is thickened and tougher, so electrical impulses have a harder time traveling through it. That is why ECGs after an MI can show permanent changes.

What the lecture says

  • When myocardial tissue that has been deprived of oxygen suddenly receives oxygenated blood, it can become stunned, and that can cause heart failure.

Key Takeaways

Acute MI by the numbers

  • Acute MI is a leading cause of death in the United States and throughout the world.
  • A big reason for that mortality is hesitating or delaying care ("it might pass," "it's not serious").
  • With treatment, survival is great: about 90 to 95% for treated patients in the US. Teach patients: if you suspect an MI, seek treatment. Better safe than sorry.
  • About half of people with MI are younger than 65.
  • People older than 65 and people with diabetes can have an MI and not know it: a silent or asymptomatic MI, found later, with no signs or symptoms at the time.

What an Aneurysm Is

Definition and cause

  • A localized dilation or outpouching of a vessel wall.
  • A true aneurysm involves all three layers of the arterial wall. There are also false aneurysms (below).
  • Aneurysms are primarily a result of hypertension. Prolonged, uncontrolled high blood pressure puts constant pressure on the arterial wall: the vessel dilates, the wall weakens, a pouch or bulge forms, and eventually the pouch can burst.
  • Aneurysms cause turbulent blood flow, which makes them susceptible to rupture.
  • Most commonly seen in the aorta and the cerebral arteries, but they can occur elsewhere.

pH, Hydrogen & Acids

Topic 1, first voiceover (Acid base 1): what pH is, why hydrogen matters, normal serum pH and how pH varies in the body, what pH affects (including oxygen delivery), the survivable limits, and volatile vs non-volatile acids.

What pH Is

pH, acids and bases

  • pH is the potential, or the power, of a hydrogen ion. It is a scale that identifies whether a solution is more acidic or more alkaline, based on the hydrogen ions present.
  • The bottom of the scale is more acidic, the middle is neutral, and the higher the number, the more basic (alkaline).
  • pH is inversely related to hydrogen ion concentration: when hydrogen ions go up, pH goes down (more acidic); when hydrogen ions go down, pH goes up (more basic).
  • The scale is logarithmic, not linear: each number is 10 times more acidic or alkaline than the one before it. A pH of 5 is 10 times more acidic than a pH of 6; going from 7 to 6 is a tenfold increase in hydrogen ions.
  • An acid can donate (release) hydrogen ions. A base can accept hydrogen ions; bases have fewer hydrogen ions and give up a hydroxide ion (OH⁻).
  • A strong acid completely dissociates in solution (gives up all its hydrogen ions). A weak acid only partially dissociates.

Where Common Things Fall on the Scale

Acidic side (lower numbers)

  • Milk, coffee, tomato, lemon
  • Stomach acid (hydrochloric acid)
  • Battery acid

Neutral and alkaline side

  • Water: 7, neutral
  • Blood: on the alkaline side; the slide shows it at 8, but it is really a little lower than 8
  • Baking soda, stomach tablets (like Alka-Seltzer), soap
  • Drain cleaner: 14

Why Hydrogen Matters

What hydrogen does in the body

  • Helps maintain cell membranes.
  • Helps with enzymatic activity.
  • Is a component of water and keeps the body hydrated.
  • Helps with energy production.
  • Is a component of the sugars, proteins, starches and fats we consume.

The body works to stay in homeostasis and functions best within certain ranges, like a heart rate of 60 to 100 or sodium of 135 to 145. pH is one of those ranges.

Normal pH & How It Varies in the Body

Normal serum (blood) pH: 7.35 to 7.45. Most of the body's enzymes function within that very narrow range.

pH isn't the same everywhere. The stomach is the most acidic place in the body, with hydrochloric acid at a pH more like 1. The stomach is uniquely designed for it: mucous cells secrete mucus and bicarbonate at the cell surface to protect the epithelial cells. Outside the stomach, acid that strong causes damage: if the lower esophageal sphincter lets stomach acid into the esophagus, it can cause ulcers and erosion, and acid can further damage an already-damaged stomach lining (a stomach ulcer).

Volatile vs Non-Volatile Acids

Volatile acids

  • Can be eliminated as carbon dioxide (CO2) gas, by the lungs (pulmonary ventilation).
  • The only volatile acid in the body is carbonic acid (H2CO3).
  • Carbonic acid is a weak acid: it does not easily release its hydrogen ion.
  • In the presence of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, it readily dissociates into CO2 and water.

Non-volatile acids

  • Can be eliminated only by the kidneys.
  • Most are strong acids: they readily release their hydrogen ions.

Why the Body Leans Acidic

  • At baseline, the body produces more acids than bases: from the food we eat and the drinks we consume, from the metabolism of lipids and proteins, and from cellular metabolism, whose waste product is CO2.
  • We want to avoid alkalosis, especially if a person is ill, because hemoglobin holds on to oxygen and tissue oxygenation suffers. An alkaline pH is also really difficult to correct.

Respiratory Control of pH

  • The body produces CO2 continuously, which forms carbonic acid; in the lungs it breaks back down into water and CO2, and the excess CO2 is exhaled.
  • This works only on volatile acids, not on non-volatile acids like lactic acid.
  • The lungs adjust serum pH by changing the rate or depth of breathing. The brain and arterial receptors are stimulated by the pH change.
  • On mechanical ventilation in the ICU, these adjustments can be made through the ventilator settings.

pH too low (acidosis)

  • Breathing rate or depth increases → more CO2 blown off → less carbonic acid → pH rises.

pH too high (alkalosis)

  • Breathing rate or depth decreases → CO2 retained → more carbonic acid → pH falls.

Renal Control of pH

  • The kidneys are the last to alter pH and are the long-term regulators. They can eliminate large amounts of acid by getting rid of hydrogen ions, using an active transport mechanism.
  • They can also excrete bicarb (a base) if the pH is too high, or retain bicarb if the pH is too low.
  • Example: in a COPD patient retaining CO2, over time the kidneys compensate by holding on to more bicarb, so this patient has higher CO2 and higher bicarb to balance the serum pH.
  • Kidney function is extremely important: in renal failure, patients can no longer maintain a normal pH and often take bicarb daily.

ABG Interpretation

How Compensation Works

Respiratory compensation (lungs)

  • Triggered by a metabolic problem.
  • Rapid: within a few minutes to a few hours.
  • Hyperventilation (blow off CO2) or hypoventilation (conserve CO2), depending on whether it's correcting an acidosis or an alkalosis.

Metabolic compensation (kidneys)

  • Triggered by a respiratory problem.
  • Slow: hours to days.
  • Retains or excretes hydrogen ions and bicarb as needed.

The Four Levels of Compensation

LevelpHCO2 and Bicarb
UncompensatedAbnormalOnly one (the acid or the base component) is abnormal, not both.
Partially compensatedStill abnormalBoth are abnormal: the body has started to respond, moving the second component to pull the pH back.
CompensatedWithin normal limitsStill abnormal, but balanced. The imbalance is neutralized, which is not the same as corrected.
CorrectedWithin normal limitsAlso within normal limits, after whatever caused the imbalance has been corrected.

Not everyone returns to "corrected." A COPD patient holds on to CO2, and their bicarb stays high to balance it; because COPD is chronic, you will never see a fully corrected ABG.

Interpreting an ABG Step by Step

StepWhat to Do
1. pHAcidic, basic or normal? If the pH is normal but the CO2 and bicarb are not, compensation has occurred. Then decide which way the normal pH leans: treat 7.40 as neutral; 7.35 to 7.40 leans acidic, above 7.40 to 7.45 leans basic (e.g., 7.36 and 7.38 lean acidic; 7.45 leans basic).
2. PaCO2 (35 to 45)Acidic (high), basic (low) or normal? If it is abnormal and matches the pH, it's a respiratory problem: an inverse relationship with pH (Respiratory Opposite).
3. HCO3 (24 to 29)A calculated value. Basic (high), acidic (low) or normal? If it matches the pH, it's a metabolic problem: a direct relationship with pH (Metabolic Equal).

With compensation, the pH points the way. When the pH is normal, the side it leans toward (acidic or basic) tells you which value is the primary problem and which is compensating.

Migraines

What a Migraine Is

Migraines are the most common complaint neurologists see day to day. A migraine is an episodic neurologic disorder characterized by a headache that lasts anywhere from 4 to 72 hours.

At least TWO of these

  • Unilateral head pain (characteristic of migraine)
  • Throbbing pain
  • Headache that worsens with activity
  • Pain rated moderate to severe

Plus at least ONE of these

  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Photophobia: sensitivity to (avoidance of) light, such as sunglasses, dark rooms, pulled curtains
  • Phonophobia: avoidance of sound; everything feels loud and grating

Types of Migraine

TypeWhat the Lecture Says
With auraAn aura is present (about a third of people with migraines).
Without auraNo aura. The most common kind of migraine.
ChronicMigraines on about 15 days a month over 3 months. They usually start out episodic, and their frequency increases over time until they reach that threshold.

Risk Factors & Triggers

Risk factors

  • Family history.
  • Estrogen and progesterone: females are more susceptible. Migraines can occur more right before and during menstruation, and often decrease during pregnancy and menopause. The theory is that cyclical withdrawal of these hormones over the menstrual cycle triggers migraines.
  • Other genetic and environmental factors.

Common triggers

  • Fatigue; sleep disturbances, meaning too little or too much sleep (e.g., 15 hours when that isn't normal)
  • Missing a meal; overexertion
  • Weather changes (rainy, cold weather)
  • Stress; hormonal changes
  • Alcohol (red wine is a common one)
  • Bright lights or strong smells (stores with strong odors)

Pathophysiology

Not well understood; there is no well-established, identifiable pathology. Migraines are associated with changes in brain metabolism and blood flow, and current theories involve changes in neurotransmitter levels and blood vessel tone.

3They activate the vascular system, stimulating vasodilation, which results in the migraine.

Parkinson's Disease

What Parkinson's Disease Is

Parkinson's disease (PD)

  • A complex motor disorder with other systemic non-motor and neurologic symptoms.
  • Progressive and degenerative: it keeps getting worse, and some symptoms worsen faster than others.
  • Characterized by tremors, rigidity and bradykinesia. The tremor is notably more present when the muscles are at rest.
  • A disorder of the basal ganglia, which help produce smooth, coordinated movements (the lecture places them in the cerebellum). The concern is the substantia nigra inside the basal ganglia, which contains the cells that produce dopamine.

Primary vs Secondary Parkinsonism

Parkinsonism is the umbrella term. Parkinson's disease is the leading cause of parkinsonism.

Primary parkinsonism

  • The etiology is unknown (idiopathic); this is Parkinson's disease.
  • Also includes genetic components and sporadic onset.
  • Defining feature: no defining event led to the onset of symptoms.
  • There is damage to the dopamine-producing cells.

Secondary (acquired) parkinsonism

  • Results from something else: an event that caused the onset.
  • Infection, head trauma, drug intoxication.
  • Some antiemetics and some antidepressants are the most common cause. Symptoms usually go away after the medication is stopped.
  • Any drug that interferes with dopamine transmission can cause it.

Dopamine, Acetylcholine & the Imbalance

Dopamine

  • Has both inhibitory and excitatory effects in the brain, depending on the receptor it binds.
  • In PD, it's the inhibitory effects that are lacking.
  • Helps control movement and balance by inhibiting unnecessary movements, which improves coordination, balance and muscle control.

Acetylcholine (ACh)

  • An excitatory neurotransmitter: stimulates muscle movement and can cause uncoordinated movement.
  • Essential for all muscle function, and works with dopamine. It's all about the balance.
StepWhat Happens
1The substantia nigra in the basal ganglia is destroyed.
2Dopamine levels decrease, because the dopamine-producing cells are in the substantia nigra.
3An imbalance develops: a relative excess of acetylcholine.
4Extra muscle movements that dopamine would normally inhibit are no longer inhibited: loss of controlled movement and balance.

Clinical Manifestations

They may develop alone or in combination, and diagnosis often takes a while because onset is insidious. At first it may involve only one side of the body; it typically becomes bilateral as the disease progresses. Later, all of them appear.

Common signs

  • Bradykinesia: slowness of movement.
  • Cogwheel rigidity: stiff muscles; movements like bending the elbow aren't smooth.
  • Resting tremor.
  • Shuffling (Parkinsonian) gait: they don't lift their feet.
  • Mask-like expression: loss of small facial movements, a flat affect.
  • Postural instability.
The classic triad, the hallmark of PD: Tremor, Rigidity, Bradykinesia.

Tremor

  • Often the first sign. It is a rest tremor: most noticeable when the body part is supported against gravity but not doing purposeful activity, and it decreases with movement.
  • An early sign is the pill-rolling tremor: the thumb looks like it's rolling a small object against the finger. In the dominant hand, handwriting may suffer.
  • Worse with stress or concentration, such as trying to control the movement.

Goals & Strategy of Drug Therapy

  • Goal: help clients keep motor control as long as possible, decrease symptoms, and maintain as much independence as possible. Because PD is progressive and degenerative, treatment is staving it off as much as possible.
  • When to start: once symptoms affect the dominant hand or interfere with work, ADLs, or social or leisure activities.
  • Strategy: correct the imbalance, either by enhancing dopamine (dopaminergic drugs) or by blocking acetylcholine (anticholinergic drugs).

Sinemet (Levodopa-Carbidopa)

The gold standard of treatment for Parkinson's disease.

Levodopa

  • Once it reaches the brain, it is converted to dopamine and activates the dopamine receptors, increasing dopamine activity.

Carbidopa

  • Prevents levodopa from being broken down before it reaches the brain, so more dopamine is sustained for longer.
  • One of the most effective PD drugs, but it takes several months for symptoms to improve while dopamine builds up. Teach clients it can take up to months.
  • It does not work long-term: as PD progresses, fewer dopaminergic terminals survive to take up levodopa and convert it to dopamine, so the effect is lost.

Myasthenia Gravis

What Myasthenia Gravis Is

Myasthenia gravis (MG)

  • An acquired autoimmune disease characterized by fluctuating weakness of certain muscle groups.
  • Commonly affected: ocular muscles (the eyes), bulbar muscles (brainstem region), and the limb and respiratory muscle groups.
  • The weakness comes from an antibody-mediated attack on the postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular junction: a breakdown in communication between nerve cells and muscle cells.

How It Progresses

  • Variable from person to person. It often appears first as a mild, insidious case that's easy to push off ("just a little muscle weakness").
  • It can spontaneously go into remission (back to baseline), stabilize (plateau), or cycle through relapses, stable periods and symptom-free periods lasting weeks to months.
  • It can also progress severely and rapidly.
  • Because the course is hard to predict, being honest up front with clients about that is key.

Who Gets It

  • Rare; more common in females and in people aged 10 to 65 (a broad range).
  • Why the antibodies attack isn't known; there seems to be a genetic component and possible environmental triggers.
  • Manifestations often first appear during pregnancy or postpartum, or after exposure to certain types of anesthesia.

What Happens at the Neuromuscular Junction

StepWhat Happens
1Antibodies form against the nicotinic acetylcholine postsynaptic receptors at the neuromuscular junction of skeletal muscle; the receptors are no longer recognized as self.
2Anti-acetylcholine antibodies attach to the receptor sites and block them from binding acetylcholine.
3Eventually the antibodies destroy the receptor sites.
4The body makes enough acetylcholine, but there aren't enough sites for it to bind, so nerve impulse transmission to the muscle is diminished.

Acetylcholine is an excitatory neurotransmitter responsible for muscle contraction (the lecture says smooth muscle). It also has a role in vasodilation, increasing body secretions, and slowing heart rate, which matters for the drugs below.

An acute exacerbation of muscle weakness

  • Can develop as MG progresses, typically triggered by a stressor: infection, surgery, emotional distress, pregnancy, a menstrual cycle, or inadequate pharmacotherapy or other drugs (not on enough medication, or not taking it appropriately).
  • In the second video, a simple cold sent Carrie into crisis: difficulty expanding her lungs, difficulty breathing, needing oxygen.
Myasthenic crisis: watch the respiratory muscles. The big concern is respiratory insufficiency, and these clients are at higher risk for respiratory arrest.

Drug Therapy

Drug therapy is not a cure; the hope is to alter and alleviate symptoms. Several administration routes can be used.

Immunosuppressants

  • MG is autoimmune, so immunosuppressants, particularly steroids, are used frequently to calm the immune response.

Cholinesterase inhibitors

  • Cholinesterase is the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine.
  • Blocking it lets acetylcholine stay in the synaptic junction longer, so it can hopefully find a receptor the antibodies haven't damaged.
  • They also intensify the effects of the acetylcholine released, hopefully increasing muscle strength.

Neostigmine

  • A cholinesterase inhibitor (anticholinesterase drug), so essentially a cholinergic drug, used to treat MG symptoms.
  • Facilitates impulse transmission across the neuromuscular junction, increasing cholinergic action.
  • At therapeutic levels it affects both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.

Nicotinic receptors

  • Adrenal and skeletal muscle.
  • These are the receptors affected by MG.

Muscarinic receptors

  • Sweat glands and other glands (and the effects on the GI tract, bladder, heart, lungs and eyes below).
  • Neostigmine stimulates these too, which causes most of its side effects.

Adverse Effects of Neostigmine

Muscarinic stimulation

  • Increased secretions and GI motility (diarrhea).
  • Urinary urgency.
  • Slowed heart rate.
  • Bronchoconstriction: consider carefully before giving to clients with asthma or other lung disease.
  • Miosis.

Nicotinic (neuromuscular) stimulation

  • Therapeutic dose: increased muscle contraction, the desired effect.
  • Toxic dose: decreased contraction; the overstimulated muscles wear out and can fail, leading to a cholinergic crisis.

Cholinergic Crisis

  • Extreme muscle weakness or paralysis, plus signs of excessive muscarinic stimulation: increased intestinal motility (diarrhea, cramping), bradycardia, pupil constriction, lots of saliva, diaphoresis.
  • It arises from anticholinesterase medications: essentially a drug toxicity, from excessive acetylcholine accumulation.
  • Risk of respiratory arrest; the client may need mechanical ventilation.
  • Antidote for the muscarinic signs: atropine (an anticholinergic).
Teach clients with MG to wear a medical alert bracelet at all times.

Multiple Sclerosis

What Multiple Sclerosis Is

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

  • A chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease involving degeneration of CNS myelin (loss of the myelin sheath), scarring (sclerosis or plaque formation) and loss of axons.
  • A progressive disease, with several types.
  • Features: lots of inflammation, especially in the white matter of the brain and spinal cord; demyelination of nerve fibers; and scar tissue called gliosis. Glial cells myelinate neurons in the CNS; on imaging, the white spots are areas of scar tissue.
  • Demyelinating disorders damage the myelin sheath and impair neural signal transmission. MS affects the central nervous system; Guillain-Barré affects the peripheral nervous system (a later lecture).

Who Gets MS

The etiology is largely unknown, most likely a combination of genetic predisposition, an immune system problem and an environmental trigger (such as an infection).

RacePeople who are white are more likely to have MS.

Pathophysiology

MS is a diffuse, progressive CNS autoimmune inflammatory disease affecting white and gray matter throughout the brain and spinal cord. The attack targets the myelin sheath and involves T lymphocytes and antibodies.

Types of MS

Most types have exacerbations (relapses or flares): a temporary worsening of symptoms, mild or serious, lasting several days to several weeks, which may improve and may be followed by progressively worsening symptoms.

  • Relapses vary: for about 20% of people they last less than a week; for others 1 to 2 weeks, or 2 weeks to a month.
  • If relapses get shorter after starting treatment (e.g., from a month down to a week or less), that suggests the medication is working.

Clinical Manifestations

No finding is unique to MS, but some are highly characteristic. The big takeaway: symptoms depend on which nerves are affected.

Common initial symptoms

  • Paresthesias of the face, trunk and limbs; numbness, tingling, weakness, loss of muscle function.
  • Optic neuritis and unilateral vision loss.
  • Impaired gait.
  • Urinary incontinence.
  • Some develop seizures.

Symptoms to monitor closely

  • Cognitive "cog fog": difficulty thinking clearly and concentrating, not necessarily a memory problem.
  • Vision problems.
  • Depression, related to loss of function.
  • Fatigue: needs more rest periods.
  • Pain: chronic (continuous) or acute (with exacerbations); burning or stabbing.
  • Bowel: constipation most common, possibly incontinence. Bladder: incontinence or retention.
  • Sexual dysfunction; muscle stiffness or spasms.
  • Walking and balance issues, vertigo: high fall risk; some eventually need a wheelchair.
Clients with MS are at high risk for falls. Most have walking and balance issues, and some have vertigo.

Goals of Drug Therapy

  • There is no cure. Goals: slow disability and keep the client functioning at the highest possible capacity.
  • Decrease relapse frequency, lengthen remissions, and reduce new brain lesions (which lead to more dysfunction and symptoms).
  • Three jobs: modify the disease process, treat acute exacerbations, and manage symptoms.

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

ALS: What It Is

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

  • Also called Lou Gehrig's disease (after the Yankees baseball player who had it).
  • A neurodegenerative, progressive muscle weakness. The cause is unknown.
  • It is fatal, usually from respiratory failure caused by the progressive muscle weakness. Death usually occurs about 3 years after diagnosis.
Risk FactorWhat the Lecture Says
AgeTypically diagnosed between 40 and 70; peaks around 60 to 69.
SexMore diagnoses in males.
GeneticsA familial form with a genetic mutation accounts for about 10% of diagnoses.
SmokingSome research suggests it is a risk factor.

ALS: Pathology & Cause

  • The principal feature is degeneration of the upper and lower motor neurons, so the voluntary muscles are affected.
  • Large motor neurons decrease in the spinal cord, brainstem and cerebral cortex, followed by degeneration of the remaining motor neurons.
  • Motor neuron death leads to axonal degeneration, demyelination and sclerosis (scarring). Damaged motor neurons stop sending messages, so the muscles can't function.
The excitotoxicity hypothesis (the current working hypothesis): excessive levels of glutamate, an excitatory neurotransmitter, start a series of events that eventually kill the neuron. It came from finding elevated glutamate in the CSF of people with ALS, and riluzole, which reduces glutamate, improves survival.

ALS: Clinical Manifestations

Signs vary greatly from person to person, depending on which neurons are affected. Initial symptoms relate to upper motor neuron dysfunction, lower motor neuron dysfunction, or both.

Spinal form (about half)Weakness begins in the arms and legs and progresses to muscle atrophy: spasticity, lost dexterity, impaired gait, trouble walking, tripping and falling, hand weakness.
As it progressesDifficulty chewing, swallowing and speaking; cognitive and behavioral changes in some; constipation (immobility, diet, muscle weakness); sleep problems.
EventuallyBreathing becomes the main issue: ALS paralyzes the breathing muscles, and respiratory failure is why people with ALS die.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

Guillain-Barré Syndrome: What It Is

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), also called acute ascending polyneuritis

  • A rare autoimmune disorder that causes demyelination: autoantibodies damage the myelin sheath.
  • Myelin covers the axons and lets impulses travel almost instantaneously. As it is destroyed, impulses are delayed or completely absent.
TriggerWhat the Lecture Says
Viral infectionThe usual trigger, a few days to weeks earlier, typically after a respiratory tract or GI infection.
Bacterial infectionSome cases; particularly Campylobacter jejuni, a foodborne illness.
SurgeryOccasionally, days to weeks after surgery.
Flu vaccinationRarely.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome: Pattern & Manifestations

The ascending pattern

  • Symptoms usually start in the lower extremities (weakness and tingling, pins and needles) and ascend up the body.
  • Recovery goes the opposite direction: strength returns from the top down (upper body, lungs, then the legs).
  • Severity ranges from tingling and weakness to full leg paralysis, complete quadriplegia, and respiratory insufficiency.

Timing

  • Most worsen over hours to weeks.
  • Some decline rapidly, over 24 hours; these clients may end up intubated on a ventilator.
  • Recovery is just as variable; some need a tracheostomy after weeks of ventilation.
The concern is the ascending weakness reaching the breathing muscles. Once they're affected, GBS can cause respiratory insufficiency and ultimately respiratory failure needing mechanical ventilation.

Manifestations

  • Uncoordinated movements, loss of strength, difficulty walking: a high fall risk.
  • Decreased sensation, so clients may not notice injury.
  • Loss of bowel and bladder control.
  • Blurred vision, which doesn't always fit the ascending pattern and can be an early sign.
  • Problems breathing, swallowing and chewing, so an aspiration risk.

Guillain-Barré Syndrome: Treatment & Prognosis

  • No cure. The goal is to manage severity and speed recovery.
  • Acute phase: steroids (high dose, titrated down as the client recovers) and IV immunoglobulin (IVIG), which shorten the severity and duration of symptoms; high-dose IVIG can shorten hospital stays.
  • Most people get GBS only once; some have recurrences.

Neuro Infectious Diseases

Meningitis: What It Is & What Causes It

  • The lecture defines meningitis as "an inflammation of the brain or the spinal cord." It is primarily an infection of the pia mater and subarachnoid space, the ventricular system and the CSF.
  • Infectious meningitis is caused by bacteria: from an infection elsewhere (such as pneumonia), the bloodstream, or a penetrating wound that lets bacteria reach the CSF or brain.
  • Most common pathogens in adults: Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococci) and Neisseria meningitidis (meningococci).
  • It may also be caused by viruses (enteroviruses in particular), fungi, parasites or toxins, but bacteria are the most common.
Meningeal LayerWhat It Is
Pia materThe layer closest to the brain.
Arachnoid materThe middle layer; the subarachnoid space is filled with cerebrospinal fluid.
Dura materThe outer layer: really tough and fibrous.

Who Gets Meningitis & How It Spreads

When and who

  • Typically fall or winter, when people stay inside with the windows closed.
  • Typically follows otitis or sinusitis (an ear or sinus infection), an immunocompromised state, or pneumonia.
  • Pneumococcal meningitis mostly affects young people and those older than 40 (as stated in the lecture).
  • Outbreaks in college dorms, prisons, military bases: anywhere large groups live and sleep together. It is very communicable.

How it spreads

  • Respiratory droplets, or contact with contaminated saliva or respiratory secretions.
  • Some carriers of meningococcal bacteria never develop the disease but can still pass it on.

Meningitis: Pathophysiology

StepWhat Happens
1Meningococci or pneumococci are inhaled and attach to the epithelial cells of the nasopharynx.
2They cross the mucosal barrier and enter the bloodstream.
3They travel to the brain, cross the blood-brain barrier and infect the meninges, starting in the arachnoid mater and the CSF of the subarachnoid space.
4Large numbers of neutrophils are recruited; the inflammation causes brain edema, and the exudate thickens the CSF and can impair its flow.
5Increased intracranial pressure: an adult's skull is a closed system with no room for more.

Meningitis: Clinical Manifestations

The classic triad: Fever (infection), Headache (meningeal irritation; often throbbing), and Stiff neck / nuchal rigidity (tucking the chin to the chest is painful because it stretches the inflamed meninges).

Other manifestations

  • Change in mental status.
  • Nausea and vomiting (can happen with increased ICP).
  • Severe photophobia.
  • Decreased consciousness, up to coma or death, from increased ICP.
  • Seizures in about 30% of cases.
  • Specific to meningococcus: a petechial or purpuric rash covering the skin and mucous membranes.

Bacterial vs Viral Meningitis

Bacterial

  • More common, with a higher fatality rate if treatment isn't started quickly: it can kill within hours.
  • The petechial rash is associated with this form. It is non-blanchable: press a glass over it and a petechial or purpuric rash won't fade.
  • Can cause septic emboli: tiny microemboli that lodge in capillaries and block blood flow, so adults and children can lose fingers or limbs.
  • Long-term effects: hearing loss, seizures, brain damage.

Viral (acute viral meningitis)

  • The milder form.
  • Often no elevated white cell count in the CSF on lumbar puncture.
  • Typically no long-term effects.
Bacterial meningitis: give antibiotics as soon as possible and do not hold them for any reason. It can kill within a matter of hours.

Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)

Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)

  • A type of cerebrovascular disease: a focal, acute cerebral insufficiency. Usually only one vessel gets closed off, clotted off or has a spasm, so only that area of the brain is affected.
  • Typically lasts less than 24 hours: a transient episode of neurologic dysfunction with no acute infarction. Symptoms are often gone by the time the client reaches the ED, with no residual effects.
  • A stroke, by contrast, causes neurological deficits that last longer than 24 hours.
  • Fairly common; seen more often in males.
  • After a TIA, the client is at increased risk for a stroke, especially within the first month. Monitor and teach them to go to the ED if they suspect another TIA, because it might be a full-blown stroke.
  • Presentation depends on which vessel is involved (a vessel supplying the speech area causes difficulty forming words). Recurrent TIAs usually produce the same symptoms each time. Onset is abrupt, and recovery is abrupt and rapid.

The lecture also says a CT can show blood in the brain tissue with a TIA, though "not as clear as it is with an ischemic stroke." That is kept as stated; check the slide wording.

Carotid area

  • Weakness or heaviness in the arms, legs or face.
  • Numbness.
  • Vision loss.

Vertebrobasilar area (back of the brain)

  • Blurry vision.
  • Vertigo.
  • Ataxia.
  • Motor or sensory changes.

TIA mimic: Bell's palsy

A problem with the facial nerve causes facial paralysis (drooping mouth, drooping face), so these clients are often evaluated for a stroke or TIA. Bell's palsy recovers, but it takes time and requires treatment; a TIA recovers much more quickly.

Stroke & Ischemic Stroke

Stroke (CVA): Overview & Statistics

  • Also called a cerebrovascular accident (CVA), the term used in hospitals, or a "brain attack" (a term that didn't stick).
  • The result of a problem with blood supply to the brain. Two types: ischemic, about 87% of strokes, and hemorrhagic, about 13%.
  • About 800,000 strokes a year; for over half a million people it's their first stroke. Risk rises with age, but about a third of people with a first stroke are under 65.
  • A leading cause of disability. Third leading cause of death in women, fifth in men, fourth overall. Someone has a stroke about every 40 seconds and someone dies from one every 4 minutes; about $36.5 billion in healthcare costs a year.

Signs of a Stroke & the First Test

Clinical manifestations

  • Numbness or weakness of the face, arm or leg, usually on one side (asymmetrical weakness).
  • Sudden confusion; difficulty speaking or understanding speech; slurred speech (dysarthria).
  • Vision trouble in one or both eyes.
  • Trouble walking, balance problems, dizziness, lack of coordination (ataxia).
  • A sudden, severe headache with no known cause, particularly important in hemorrhagic stroke.

CT without contrast, done very quickly

  • Part of the stroke alert protocol (with attention to airway and breathing as needed); it drives the rest of the treatment plan.
  • Blood shows up right away as white; an ischemic stroke shows as a darkened area.
  • We must know ischemic vs hemorrhagic: never anticoagulate or give a thrombolytic to a hemorrhagic stroke. It makes the bleeding worse and could kill the client.

Ischemic Stroke

  • An occlusion of a cerebral artery by a thrombus or an embolus; everything distal to the occlusion loses its blood supply.
  • Both types typically present the same way, based on which part of the brain isn't perfused, but the etiology guides treatment: atherosclerosis? a hypercoagulability problem? a thrombus or embolism elsewhere?
  • On CT, a darkened area that isn't prominent at first but becomes more noticeable if blood flow isn't restored.
  • A tiny vessel occlusion may go unnoticed (evidence of an old stroke is sometimes found on an unrelated CT). A larger vessel occlusion produces symptoms based on its location.
  • Neuro deficits start within about one minute of oxygen deprivation; without reestablished blood flow, the damage becomes irreversible.
  • Hemorrhagic conversion: after an ischemic stroke, blood from the peripheral circulation extravasates across a disrupted blood-brain barrier into the brain tissue once reperfusion is established, naturally or as a result of therapy. A hemorrhage then occurs where there was an ischemic event.

Hemorrhagic Stroke & Intracranial Bleeds

Hemorrhagic Stroke

  • A ruptured vessel with bleeding within the brain tissue (the parenchyma).
  • Leading cause: untreated hypertension; over time the pressure weakens vessels until they rupture. Clients with aneurysms are also at increased risk.
  • About 38% mortality, within minutes to hours; in the brainstem, death can occur within seconds. Mortality depends on the size of the area affected.
  • Blood is very irritating to brain tissue, so inflammation causes edema; blood plus edema in the confined skull sharply increases ICP. The brain tissue can collapse on itself (herniation), which usually results in death.
  • Usually ages 30 to 60, younger than ischemic stroke clients. High morbidity and mortality, with serious disabilities.

Prognosis depends on

  1. Age: older clients have brain atrophy and more room, so a better prognosis.
  2. Location and size: the brainstem has a very poor prognosis.
  3. How rapid the bleeding is, and whether it causes brain distortion or shift. A midline shift (a bleed pushing one side across the center line, seen on a CT report) means a very poor prognosis.

Presentation

  • The worst headache of their life.
  • A rapid change in level of consciousness.

DKA & HHS: Physiology & Labs

DKA vs HHS: What They Are & Who Gets Them

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

  • A crisis that occurs as an acute metabolic complication of diabetes, with ketosis.
  • More common in people under 65, and characteristically associated with type 1 diabetes.
  • Can occur in type 2 under extreme stress: really serious infections, sepsis, trauma, cardiovascular or other emergencies. Type 2 diabetics still make insulin, which is typically enough to prevent fat breakdown into ketones unless the situation is extreme (for example, a type 2 diabetic who requires insulin and gets a serious infection).
  • About 30% of all DKA cases occur in people with type 2 diabetes, particularly those taking SGLT2 inhibitors (the "-flozins," like dapagliflozin).

Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)

  • Also called hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome; in the past, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar non-ketotic (HHNK). HHS is the name you'll see most.
  • Another life-threatening complication of diabetes, but with no ketosis.
  • Mostly type 2 diabetes, and more common over 65.
  • Pretty rare.

Background: Insulin, Glucagon & Counter-Regulatory Hormones

  • Extracellular glucose is primarily regulated by two hormones: insulin and glucagon.
  • After a meal, glucose enters the pancreatic beta cells, which leads to insulin release. Insulin restores normal blood glucose by reducing hepatic glucose production (less glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis) and by increasing glucose uptake by skeletal muscle and adipose tissue.
  • Gluconeogenesis turns non-carbohydrate substrates (amino acids, lactate, glycerol) into glucose. Glycogenolysis breaks glycogen down into glucose, which the body can use for energy.
  • In times of stress, more insulin is needed than usual; in diabetics that insulin isn't available, and DKA or HHS can develop.
  • In both, the main problem is that insulin isn't working properly: in DKA the body isn't making enough; in HHS the insulin that is available isn't working effectively.
  • On top of that, the counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol, growth hormone) go up. They increase hepatic glucose production and impair the peripheral tissues' ability to use glucose.
  • Dehydration and electrolyte imbalances occur primarily from the osmotic diuresis caused by glycosuria (glucose in the urine).

The lecture uses the word "gluconeogenesis" twice in a row; the second definition (glycogen broken down into glucose) is glycogenolysis, which is how it is listed here.

Why DKA Makes Ketones & HHS Doesn't

  • DKA is marked by increased gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and ketogenesis, with reduced glycolysis: that is the "keto" in ketoacidosis.
  • The insulin deficiency is absolute in type 1 and relative in type 2, and the excess counter-regulatory hormones add to it.
  • Suppressing lipolysis and ketogenesis is more sensitive to insulin than suppressing gluconeogenesis. In HHS, the residual insulin is enough to minimize ketoacidosis but not enough to control the hyperglycemia.
StepKetone Production
1Insulin deficiency and resistance mean glucose can't get into the cells, which need it for energy.
2Without glucose, the body breaks down fat for energy (lipolysis), releasing free fatty acids and glycerol from peripheral fat stores.
3The free fatty acids travel to the liver, are taken up by hepatocytes and activated.
4They are converted into acetyl-CoA, enter the ketogenic pathway and form ketone bodies.

Why not in HHS? One theory: there may be enough insulin activity to minimize lipolysis (and so ketone formation), but not enough to block gluconeogenesis, promote glucose use and prevent the hyperglycemia.

Why HHS Glucose Runs Higher

DKA

  • Clients present earlier, with symptoms of ketoacidosis: shortness of breath, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting.
  • Because they seek treatment earlier, glucose is usually 350 to 450 (can be as high as 800).
  • Tend to be younger with a higher GFR, so a greater capacity to excrete glucose.

HHS

  • Clients present later, without those early ketoacidosis symptoms.
  • Glucose is typically higher: it can exceed 1,000.
  • Glycosuria first limits the rise in glucose, but the osmotic diuresis causes volume depletion and a lower GFR, which limits glucose excretion. The effect is more pronounced in HHS.

LabWhat the Lecture Says
Anion gapDKA typically presents as an elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis. The anion gap is the difference between negatively and positively charged electrolytes, calculated from sodium, chloride and bicarb (sodium minus chloride plus bicarb), and reported on the blood chemistry panel.

Potassium: High on Arrival, Depleted Underneath

Most body potassium is in the cells. Clients with DKA and HHS present with a normal to elevated serum potassium but a significant total body potassium depletion, for three reasons:

ReasonWhat Happens
AcidosisIn metabolic acidosis, hydrogen ions move into the cells and potassium moves out to balance the charge, raising serum potassium. Working kidneys can offset this by excreting the excess.
Insulin deficiencyInsulin normally moves potassium into the cells; without it, potassium stays in the extracellular fluid, raising serum levels further.
HyperosmolarityThe osmotic diuresis from high glucose causes massive urinary losses, and potassium is lost continuously in the urine, depleting the total body supply.
  • Serum potassium can be 5, 6 or beyond on arrival. Don't bring it down unless it's causing problems and the client is symptomatic: it returns toward normal as the pH is corrected.
  • Once treatment starts, insulin drives potassium back into the cells and the serum level falls quickly, which can cause severe hypokalemia. If the kidneys have already excreted potassium, there is even less left to move back in.
  • As soon as the potassium reaches a normal level, replacement typically starts, because it will keep moving into the cells and the serum still needs a normal level to prevent cardiac problems.
  • Watch the client: are they symptomatic, and are there EKG changes of hyper- or hypokalemia (including dysrhythmias)?

DKA & HHS: Presentation, Treatment & Resolution

Precipitating Factors

  • A precipitating factor can often be identified.
  • Infection is the most common: severe pneumonia or a UTI without an insulin adjustment.
  • Inflammatory events: MI, CVA, sepsis, pancreatitis, without an insulin adjustment.
  • Poor compliance with insulin, or increased insulin needs that aren't met. Compliance can be checked with a hemoglobin A1c.

Clinical Presentation

DKA: evolves rapidly, over about 24 hours

  • GI: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, from delayed gastric emptying or an ileus induced by the metabolic acidosis.
  • Neurologic: slight confusion up to obtunded, with severe acidosis.
  • Volume depletion: decreased skin turgor, decreased urine output, dry oral mucosa, tachycardia, hypotension.
  • Fruity breath (some say it smells like nail polish remover) from exhaled acetone.
  • Kussmaul respirations: really deep respirations and hyperventilation, compensating for the metabolic acidosis.

HHS: begins several days before admission

  • Marked hyperglycemia with the typical signs: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, plus weakness.
  • As it progresses: lethargy, obtunded, up to coma. Neurologic symptoms are most common in HHS because of the hyperosmolarity.
  • Extracellular volume depletion: dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes, hypotension, tachycardia.
  • Symptoms of a coexisting illness that may have precipitated it (pneumonia, UTI) are usually present.

Treatment

Hospitals usually have a protocol and algorithm; follow your hospital's protocol. The lecturer cites the University of Kentucky (UK) protocol for the potassium numbers.

DKA: look at the labs

Make sure the ketoacidosis has resolved: venous pH or the anion gap, and the level of ketoacidosis by beta-hydroxybutyrate, which falls as the acidosis is corrected.

HHS: look at the patient

Are they mentally alert and back to baseline? Also check the plasma osmolality: the goal is about 315, which the lecture calls a normal level.

HHS: Why Hydration Matters So Much

  • If adequate hydration isn't maintained, dehydration and worsening renal function follow.
  • Clients with poor fluid intake from altered thirst mechanisms or inability to access fluids (as may be seen in the elderly) are at increased risk of severe dehydration.
  • Diminished renal filtration further impairs glucose excretion, which exacerbates the hyperglycemia.

Thyroid Storm: What It Is

  • An overwhelming release of thyroid hormones that puts an intense stimulus on the body's metabolism.
  • Typically a reaction to a major stressor (a big infection, major surgery, a car wreck). The stressor releases stress hormones such as adrenaline, everything in the body is "revved up," and the body can't tolerate this for long.

Addison's Disease: The Three S's

Addison's disease is a disease of the adrenal cortex that causes hyposecretion of all three adrenocortical hormones.

SHormoneWhat It Does
SugarCortisolStimulates gluconeogenesis and raises blood sugar; mobilizes fat stores; helps break down proteins.
SaltAldosteroneAssists in fluid and electrolyte balance.
SexAndrogensMale sex hormones.
  • The most severe effects come from the lack of cortisol.
  • Long-term effects: immune suppression and bone breakdown (bone density problems).

Causes & the Feedback Loop

  • It can be idiopathic, but the most common cause is autoimmune. The adrenal gland can also be damaged or destroyed by disease or trauma.
  • In primary adrenocortical insufficiency (primary Addison's), the adrenal gland stops secreting cortisol, so there is no negative feedback. The anterior pituitary increases MSH and ACTH to try to stimulate cortisol, but despite very high levels, the adrenal cortex can't produce it, and the loop never closes.
  • Manifestations don't become evident until about 90% of the adrenal cortex is destroyed, so Addison's is usually advanced at diagnosis, or diagnosed during an Addisonian crisis.

Aldosterone Deficiency vs Cortisol Deficiency

Low aldosterone (hypoaldosteronism)

  • Aldosterone regulates sodium, and so water retention. Low aldosterone means loss of water and sodium, with potassium retention.
  • Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia and dehydration, with the signs of all three.
  • Hypotension from decreased vascular tone, cardiac output and circulating blood volume.
  • Salt craving.
  • The lecture adds that hypoaldosteronism signals more ADH, which stimulates water reabsorption and could lead to fluid volume excess in some cases.

Low cortisol (hypocortisolism)

  • Low energy: a lack of stress hormones.
  • Hypoglycemia, since cortisol normally raises blood glucose.
  • Hyperpigmentation: the pituitary increases ACTH to raise cortisol, and ACTH also stimulates melanin secretion by melanocytes, so unsuppressed ACTH darkens the skin.

Lifelong Replacement Therapy

Adrenal insufficiency requires lifelong replacement with the appropriate corticosteroids.

DrugWhat the Lecture Says
Hydrocortisone (Cortef)The drug of choice: has both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid activity. IM, IV and PO.
PrednisoneGlucocorticoid, PO.
DexamethasoneGlucocorticoid, IV or PO.
FludrocortisoneAdded when extra mineralocorticoid activity is needed; the only mineralocorticoid available. Works like aldosterone, so expect an impact on sodium and water balance. PO.
  • Dosing should mimic the body's normal pattern of corticosteroid secretion. Mineralocorticoids can be given once a day; glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid doses should approximate what the adrenal glands normally secrete.
  • Never stop therapy abruptly.

Stress Dosing & Teaching

  • Healthy adrenal glands increase glucocorticoid output in response to stress. These clients don't get that boost, so it has to be given exogenously: increase the dose in times of stress.
  • Supplemental dosing follows guidelines for specific medical conditions and surgical procedures, so it varies. Example: for a mild or febrile illness, the 3 x 3 rule (loosely, three times the usual dose for three days).
  • Keep an emergency supply: an injectable preparation plus an oral preparation.
  • Wear a medical alert bracelet so emergency personnel know about the glucocorticoid needs.
Not following the regimen (stopping abruptly, or not increasing the dose under stress) can cause an Addisonian crisis, which can be fatal.
