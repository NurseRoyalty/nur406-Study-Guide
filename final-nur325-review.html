Module 1: Foundations of Pathophysiology

Intro to patho, cellular adaptation, immunity and infection.

Intro to Pathophysiology

  • Physiology = how the body normally works; pathophysiology = the study of disease and injury and how they affect the body: the abnormal processes that disrupt normal functioning and cause signs and symptoms.
  • Disease = a disruption in homeostasis (homeo = same, stasis = balance); it can be physical (heart failure), mental (chronic stress) or social (chronic social isolation).
  • Homeostasis keeps us within a small normal window (heart rate 60–100; temperature around 98.5). Too hot: vessels dilate, sweat glands secrete. Too cold: vessels constrict, no sweating, shivering generates heat.

Intrinsic (inside the body)

Over- or underproduction of a hormone (type 1 diabetes, thyroid), autoimmune disorders, genes, immunity, age, sex.

Extrinsic (outside the body)

Toxins, pollutants, bacteria, viruses; lifestyle and behaviors (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol); stressors.

Epidemiologic triangle (epi-triad): pathogen (the causative agent), host (susceptibility: genetics, age, immune status) and environment (external conditions, e.g., some bacteria can't survive heat, which is why meat is cooked to set temperatures). Disease risk occurs when all three come together.

Investigate every sign or symptom in detail: for a cough, is it productive (color, thickness, blood), when does it occur, acute or chronic, allergens, smoking, medications, other symptoms? Weight loss: how fast, and is it intentional?

Cellular Adaptation

Cells change size or form to survive and keep functioning. This can be normal (muscles growing with exercise, uterine cells in pregnancy) or abnormal, usually after something like radiation, a medication or lack of oxygen.

More differentiated (less anaplastic) cells; grows slowly; usually encapsulated; doesn't metastasize. Usually harmless unless it compresses a sensitive area like the brain.

Malignant neoplasm

Very anaplastic, immature cells; reproduces rapidly; no capsule; metastasizes near or far, depending on closeness to lymph or blood systems.

Intro to Immunity: Inflammation

  • Inflammation happens when cells are injured, whatever the cause. At first it is protective: it destroys invading agents, limits their spread and prepares damaged tissue for repair. Left unchecked, it becomes the problem. The suffix -itis means inflammation (appendicitis, nephritis).
  • Five cardinal signs: redness, swelling, heat, pain, loss of function.
  • Causes: exogenous (surgery, trauma, falls, burns) or endogenous (internal cell damage, such as tissue ischemia).
  • Acute inflammation lasts less than 2 weeks and can be helpful. Chronic inflammation lasts longer, can form scar tissue and is no longer helpful.

Intro to Immunity: Specific Adaptive Immunity

  • The major histocompatibility complex (MHC), called HLA in humans, is a gene cluster on chromosome 6 whose proteins sit on almost every cell surface as a "self" name tag. Two classes: MHC class 1 and 2.
  • Specific adaptive immunity recognizes foreign invaders, destroys them and remembers them for a faster response next time. It depends on lymphocytes (B and T cells) that react to foreign antigens while staying tolerant to self.
  • B cells: humoral (antibody-mediated) immunity, through antibodies in body fluids. Memory B cells remember an antigen and respond rapidly on re-exposure; plasma cells are short-lived and secrete antibodies.
  • T cells: cell-mediated immunity, recognizing antigens presented on cell surfaces.
  • An antigen is the "name tag" on a foreign or harmful cell (cancer, bacterial, fungal); antibodies (immunoglobulins) are what our own body makes against it.

Intro to Infection: Microorganisms & Transmission

Infection = a host colonized or taken over by a microbial species, either localized or systemic (spread to several body regions).

BacteriaLarger, single-celled, usually one DNA strand; reproduce inside or outside cells; can adapt and suppress defenses. Strep throat, tuberculosis, UTIs.
FungiSpore-forming (yeast infections).
ProtozoaLive in water; malaria is the most common.
HelminthsParasitic worms (roundworms, hookworms).
PrionsMisfolded proteins that attach to other proteins; devastating disease such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob (mad cow) disease.

Microorganisms live in a reservoir (humans, water, animals, insects).

Direct transmission

Physical contact (kissing, sex, contaminated soil: STDs, hookworm) and droplets, short-range aerosols from sneezing or coughing (pertussis, meningitis).

Indirect transmission

Airborne (droplet nuclei stay suspended: measles); vehicle-borne (food, water, blood: hepatitis A in contaminated food); vector-borne (mosquitoes, fleas, ticks: malaria, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, Lyme disease).

  • Portals of entry: airways, GI tract, genitourinary tract (STDs, catheters), blood (transfusion, needle sticks), maternal-fetal (Zika, Listeria; across the placenta or during birth).
  • The skin, mucous membranes and endothelium are the main physical barrier (tight junctions). The skin is the biggest barrier, so a break in skin integrity is the biggest vulnerability.
  • Translocation: bacteria move across the intestinal lining (a leaky gut, into the peritoneal cavity), or an infection in the blood lodges in another organ such as bone.

Intro to Infection: Diagnosis & Healthcare-Acquired Infections

  • Colonization: a pathogen lives at a body site without signs of infection (e.g., MRSA or VRE found on admission swabs). It can become active infection if the immune system is compromised or tissue is disrupted.
  • Signs of infection: vital sign changes (fever, increased heart rate and respiratory rate from a higher metabolic rate).
  • Culture: a Gram stain first (gram-positive vs gram-negative, shape and arrangement), then sensitivity (at least 24 hours, up to 72 for full identification): the exact bacteria and which antibiotics it's sensitive or resistant to. Gram-negative is more dangerous: a capsule or slime layer hides its antigens.
  • Culture the likely source: urine (catheter), sputum (cough), blood if concerned about sepsis ("septic" = infection in the blood). Blood cultures: aerobic and anaerobic bottles, two sets from two sticks, at least one peripheral; high risk of skin contamination, so follow policy.

C. difficile (C. diff)

  • Frequent (as often as hourly), very watery diarrhea (can be orange-tinged), abdominal cramping and tenderness; can appear months after antibiotics.
  • Diagnosed by stool C. diff toxin PCR, not culture.
  • Contact isolation (gown and gloves) and soap and water: spores resist hand sanitizer.
  • Treat with PO or IV metronidazole (Flagyl) or PO vancomycin. No antidiarrheals.
  • Complication: pseudomembranous colitis, life-threatening colon dilation needing decompression or partial removal.

Candidiasis

  • Fungal (yeast) overgrowth after antibiotics kill normal flora: oral (thrush), vaginal, and moist skin folds (under the breasts, fat folds).
  • Untreated thrush can descend into the esophagus.
  • Prevention is key: nystatin (antifungal), vaginal suppositories, antifungal powders or sprays.

Module 1: Introduction to Pharmacology

Drug development, schedules, the three phases, CYP450 and drug safety.

Intro to Pharmacology

  • Pharmacology = the study of drugs (chemicals, medications) put into the body that cause a change. Pharmacokinetics = what the body does to the drug. Pharmacodynamics = what the drug does to the body (mechanism of action and effects).
  • Three names: chemical (long, used in research: acetylparaminophenol), generic (the one official name, lowercase: acetaminophen; what exams and NCLEX use), trade (brand name, capitalized: Tylenol). When the patent expires, any company can make the generic.
  • Prototype: the first drug in a class; new drugs in the class are compared to it for effectiveness and side effects (ibuprofen/Advil for NSAIDs).
  • Therapeutic effect = what we intend the drug to do. Side effects = unintended effects of a normal dose (used interchangeably with adverse effects in this class). Toxicity = harmful effects. Adverse effects = unexpected and dangerous. Allergic reaction = an immune response, from itching and hives up to anaphylaxis.
  • For every drug, know: generic name, class, mechanism of action, indications, common and serious adverse effects, contraindications, nursing considerations (e.g., check blood sugar before insulin, BP before a BP medication) and serious interactions. Acetaminophen is contraindicated in liver failure.

Pharm Principles: Routes & the Three Phases

  • Enteral = through the GI tract (PO, enteric-coated, sublingual, buccal, rectal). Parenteral = outside the GI tract, usually injection (intradermal like a TB skin test, subQ, IM, intra-arterial, intrathecal/epidural, intra-articular, IV). Topical/transdermal = applied to body surfaces.
  • Pharmaceutical phase (oral drugs only): disintegration (tablet breaks into particles) then dissolution (particles dissolve in GI fluid). Liquids are already dissolved and absorb fastest; enteric-coated tablets resist stomach acid and dissolve in the more alkaline intestine, so they absorb more slowly.
  • Pharmacokinetic phase = what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion.
  • Pharmacodynamic phase = what the drug does to the body (mechanism of action, therapeutic effect).

Pharm Principles: Absorption & Distribution

  • Cell membranes have a phospholipid (fat) bilayer: most drugs are lipid-soluble and cross easily; water-soluble drugs need a channel or transport mechanism.
  • IV is fastest (no barriers) and essentially irreversible once given.
  • Bioavailability = the percentage of a dose reaching systemic circulation in active form. IV = 100%. Oral is less because of the first-pass effect: drug absorbed from the intestine passes through the liver first, which inactivates a portion (a 40% first pass leaves 60% bioavailability). That's why oral doses are larger (morphine about 5–10 mg PO vs 1–2 mg IV). Parenteral, sublingual, buccal and rectal routes avoid first pass.
  • Distribution depends on blood flow: best to the heart, liver, kidneys and brain; slower to muscle, skin and fat. Poor circulation (peripheral vascular disease) or an abscess reduces drug delivery; draining pus improves antibiotic distribution.
  • Blood-brain barrier: tight capillary junctions; only lipid-soluble drugs or those with a transport system cross (alcohol, glucose). It protects the CNS and is not fully developed in infants.
  • Protein binding: drugs travel partly bound to plasma proteins (mainly albumin). Only free (unbound) drug acts; bound drug is a reversible store, so highly bound drugs last longer. Low albumin (malnutrition, liver disease, alcoholism) means more free drug and risk of toxicity. Warfarin (Coumadin) is 97–99% bound: low albumin raises bleeding risk. Two highly bound drugs together can displace each other and cause toxicity.

Pharm Principles: Metabolism, Excretion & Half-Life

  • Metabolism (biotransformation), mainly in the liver: active lipid-soluble drug becomes an inactive water-soluble metabolite ready for excretion. Poor liver function means toxicity risk, so monitor liver function tests.
  • Excretion, mainly by the kidneys (glomerular filtration, tubular secretion, reabsorption). Penicillin G is about 90% excreted, so it must be given often. Monitor BUN, creatinine and GFR (the best measure of kidney function); rising values mean drugs can build up.

  • Half-life (t½) = time for serum concentration to drop 50% (100 mg/L to 50 mg/L in 8 hours = 8-hour half-life). About 5 half-lives eliminate about 97% (a 1-week half-life takes about 5 weeks to clear).
  • Steady state (amount eliminated = amount absorbed each dose) takes about 4–5 half-lives; it gives maximum therapeutic benefit. Half-life guides dosing intervals (QD, QID).
  • Around-the-clock dosing (e.g., morphine 10 mg every 3 hours with a 3-hour half-life) keeps about 50% concentration on board for chronic pain, with a PRN for breakthrough pain.
  • Onset = time to reach the minimum effective concentration; peak = time to maximum effect; duration = how long it works (determines how often to dose).

Pharm Principles: Pharmacodynamics & Drug Safety

  • Drugs can increase, decrease, replace, inhibit, destroy, protect or irritate. A bronchodilator's therapeutic effect is opening the airways; tachycardia and palpitations are side effects.
  • Receptors: reactive sites (usually proteins) on or in cells; a drug-receptor complex stimulates or inhibits.
  • Agonist: binds a receptor and initiates the effect. Antagonist: blocks natural substances from binding (diphenhydramine/Benadryl is an H1 antagonist that blocks histamine).
  • Receptor-less actions: antacids neutralize acid chemically; magnesium sulfate is an osmotic laxative.
  • Therapeutic index: ratio of toxic level to therapeutic level. Narrow (small window; warfarin, digoxin): more adverse reactions, monitor closely. Wide (amoxicillin): rarely toxic.
  • Black box warning: the FDA's strongest warning for a marketed drug, prominent on labels, ads and inserts (Celebrex: MI and thrombotic events in cardiovascular disease).
  • Medication errors cause about 1.3 million ED visits and about $3.5 billion in excess costs a year; the lecture calls them the third leading cause of death (CDC 2018). Safeguards: restrict high-alert drugs, tall man lettering (heParin), barcode scanning, Pyxis, standardization, involving the patient.
  • No trailing zeros (write 1 mg, not 1.0 mg); always use a leading zero (0.25 mg, not .25 mg).
  • High-alert medications (ISMP list, 1995): most likely to cause serious harm or death; extra precautions when prescribing, dispensing and administering.

  • Older adults: slower hepatic metabolism, reduced circulation and distribution, decreased GI absorption, less complete renal excretion, so doses may need lowering or rescheduling. Common adverse reactions: depression, constipation, falls, immobility, confusion, hip fractures.

Module 2: Hyperlipidemia & Hypertension

Cholesterol, atherosclerosis, lipid drugs, RAAS and antihypertensives.

Hyperlipidemia: Cholesterol & Lipoproteins

  • We need cholesterol: it's part of every cell membrane and builds estrogen, testosterone, vitamin D, cortisol and most bile acid. It is highly insoluble, so it enters cells by binding surface receptors.
  • Only about a quarter comes from diet (exogenous); the 2015 USDA report says dietary cholesterol is not a nutrient of concern. About three quarters is made in the liver (endogenous) through the HMG-CoA reductase pathway.
  • More dietary cholesterol makes the liver produce less; more saturated fat lets the liver make more, raising cholesterol about 15–25%. So education targets unhealthy saturated and trans fats, not cholesterol itself.
  • Liver cells have LDL receptors; a negative feedback loop: when the liver isn't making cholesterol, it makes more LDL receptors and pulls more LDL from the blood.

  • Familial hypercholesterolemia: genetic defect in liver LDL receptors, so LDL stays high despite diet and exercise. Suspect it in young people with high cholesterol; inheriting it from both parents can cause heart disease and heart attacks in children.

Hyperlipidemia: Atherosclerosis

  • Atherosclerosis = cholesterol buildup in vessels; arteriosclerosis = thickening and hardening of artery walls (often used interchangeably). Arteries matter most because they carry oxygenated blood; the coronary and peripheral arteries are the main concern.
StepPlaque Formation
1. Endothelial injuryFrom smoking, chronic hemodynamic wall stress (hypertension) or hyperglycemia.
2. Increased permeabilityLDL slips under the injured lining and triggers inflammation.
3. Foam cellsMacrophages engulf the lipids, becoming foam cells that form fatty streaks.
4. PlaqueA lipid core grows into the lumen and impedes flow. A large lipid core is prone to rupture; a clot then blocks the vessel (MI in the heart, stroke in the brain). Older plaques have more collagen and a stable cap.
  • Narrowed vessels can't dilate for more flow, so exercise brings chest pain. A blockage can reach 90% without symptoms (inactive person, small artery); at 99%, angina comes even walking from bed to chair: needs immediate intervention.
  • Only an invasive dye study under fluoroscopy shows the lumen. C-reactive protein (CRP) is a nonspecific blood marker of systemic inflammation released by the liver: elevated means higher risk, not a diagnosis of atherosclerosis.
  • CHD, CAD, ischemic heart disease and ASCVD all describe plaque in the coronary arteries limiting oxygen to the heart.
  • Lower the risk: decrease LDL, increase HDL, medication, the DASH diet (fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy; limit sugar-sweetened foods and drinks, red meat, added fats), weight control, exercise, and stop smoking (strongly tied to endothelial damage; likely vaping too).

Hyperlipidemia: Drug Therapy

Try diet and activity changes for 6 months first (unless there's already a heart attack, stroke or other organ damage); then medication.

Hypertension: Pathophysiology

StepThe RAAS (Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System)
TriggerLow blood volume, a drop in BP, or decreased renal perfusion.
1The kidney releases renin.
2Renin converts angiotensinogen (from the liver; "-ogen" = precursor) to angiotensin I.
3In the lungs, ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) converts it to angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor.
4Angiotensin II makes the adrenal glands release aldosterone: the kidney reabsorbs sodium, and water follows ("salt sucks").
EndpointsSodium and water retention plus vasoconstriction = higher BP. RAAS regulates long-term BP and extracellular volume.
  • RAAS is normally appropriate, but it can be pathologic (hypersensitivity to angiotensin II, high renin secretors). Chronic stress stimulates renin and raises angiotensin II.
  • Arterial baroreceptors (carotid sinus, aorta, left ventricle) sense BP and change heart rate and vessel tone.
  • Vascular autoregulation keeps tissue perfusion steady by changing arteriole diameter (resistance) based on the mean arterial pressure (MAP), the number in parentheses on a BP reading.

Primary (essential) hypertension

  • 90–95% of cases; no known underlying cause (often idiopathic): genetics interacting with environment.
  • Mechanisms: overactive SNS (higher heart rate, vasoconstriction), overactive RAAS (vessel remodeling and permanently higher peripheral vascular resistance, the target of most BP drugs), natriuretic peptide disruption; systemic inflammation (IL-6, CRP), endothelial dysfunction, obesity hormones, insulin resistance.
  • Risk factors: smoking, excess sodium, sedentary lifestyle, hyperlipidemia, stress, family history, obesity, age (males more before 55, females more after 60), insulin resistance, high alcohol intake.

Secondary hypertension

  • A known cause: treat the underlying cause.
  • Renal disease, adrenal cortical tumors, adrenal medullary tumors (pheochromocytoma), pregnancy, hormonal therapy.
  • Drugs: oral contraceptives, corticosteroids, antihistamines, cocaine, amphetamines.
  • Usually no symptoms, the "silent killer": symptoms mean end organ damage (chest pain, headache, visual changes, extremity weakness or pain), so screen early.
  • Long-term: more left ventricle workload, left ventricular hypertrophy, higher myocardial oxygen demand; accelerated atherosclerosis; aortic aneurysm; a primary cause of end-stage renal disease; stroke, aneurysm and hemorrhage; retinopathy and blindness; gangrene and intermittent claudication.

Hypertensive urgency

BP 180 or above and/or 120 or above, no end organ damage. Treat with oral drugs, lowering BP gradually over hours to days. Causes: anxiety, pain, abrupt withdrawal of illicit drugs or certain antihypertensives.

Hypertensive emergency

Same BP plus end organ damage (headache, blurry vision, then heart dysfunction, stroke, brain hemorrhage, chest pain, ACS, unresponsiveness, seizures). IV drugs (usually labetalol) lower BP aggressively over minutes to hours: the organ damage outweighs the risk of bottoming out.

Diabetes Pathophysiology

  • Diabetes affects about 12% of the US (2023), with about 11 million undiagnosed and about 115 million with prediabetes; the 7th leading cause of death. A metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia from a problem with insulin secretion or sensitivity.
  • The liver stores glucose as glycogen (glycogenesis) and breaks it back down when glucose is low (glycogenolysis).
  • The pancreas is exocrine (amylase, lipase into the pancreatic duct) and endocrine. The islets of Langerhans are 1–2% of the pancreas but get about 20% of its blood flow. Alpha cells secrete glucagon (raises glucose via glycogenolysis); beta cells secrete insulin and amylin (lowers glucose by moving it into skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle and fat, and storing it as glycogen).
  • Counter-regulatory hormones raise glucose and defend against hypoglycemia: glucagon, epinephrine, glucocorticoids, growth hormone.
  • Insulin also inhibits fat breakdown, so insulin problems disturb both carbohydrate and fat metabolism.
Insulin Deficiency Leads ToWhat Happens
HyperglycemiaGlucose can't enter cells without insulin, so it stays in the blood and the cells starve.
Lipolysis (fat breakdown)Free fatty acids for energy; the excess becomes cholesterol and phospholipids (atherosclerosis risk). Fatty acids → acetyl-CoA → acetoacetic acid in the liver → ketone bodies → ketosis, which can progress to severe metabolic acidosis and coma.
Proteolysis (protein breakdown)Protein synthesis stops; amino acids are used for energy: muscle wasting, amino acidemia, increased BUN, organ dysfunction. More typical of type 1.
Osmotic diuresis & dehydrationHigh glucose raises serum osmolality, pulling water out of cells (intracellular dehydration). Above the renal threshold (about 200 mg/dL) glucose spills into urine (glycosuria), dragging water and electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, phosphorus) with it; GFR falls and glucose accumulates further: a self-feeding loop.

The three P's: polyphagia (hunger from cellular starvation and fat/protein breakdown), polydipsia (thirst from dehydration and high osmolality), polyuria (osmotic diuresis).

Type 1

  • Most common pediatric chronic disease; diagnosed around age 12 (adult onset is often misdiagnosed as type 2).
  • Usually autoimmune (genetics plus environment): T cells destroy beta cells, which can't regenerate. 80–90% must be destroyed before insulin secretion falls.
  • Absolute insulin deficiency: no endogenous insulin. 5–10% of cases (formerly insulin-dependent or juvenile-onset).
  • Long subclinical period; then the three P's, more often weight loss.

Type 2

  • Genetic and environmental. Risk: age, obesity, high BP, poor diet, inactivity, family history, high cholesterol.
  • Insulin resistance (receptors lose sensitivity) plus some decreased secretion from exhausted beta cells; linked to long-term obesity.
  • Vague symptoms: recurrent infections, visual changes, slow wound healing, fatigue; the three P's are less common.
  • Compensatory hyperinsulinemia stimulates fat creation and blocks fat breakdown; the liver releases more glucose.
  • ADA: screen all adults 35 or older; earlier with risk factors.

Somogyi effect

Too much insulin at bedtime causes nocturnal hypoglycemia; counter-regulatory hormones and the liver's stored glucose cause rebound morning hyperglycemia (can reach ketosis). "I wake up at 320 and haven't eaten." Review the night dose, basal dose and bedtime corrections.

Dawn phenomenon

Morning hyperglycemia from the natural early-morning release of cortisol and growth hormone; usually milder than Somogyi.

Obesity & Metabolic Syndrome

  • 74% of US adults (20+) are overweight or obese; 42% obese, 10% severely obese; highest prevalence at ages 40–59. Obesity is the 5th leading cause of death in the US and a risk factor for type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, hypertension, reflux, arthritis, infection and sleep apnea.
  • Adipose tissue insulates, supports, stores energy as triglycerides in adipocytes (which can increase in number and hypertrophy) and acts as an endocrine organ secreting adipokines.

Subcutaneous (peripheral) fat

Just under the skin (pinchable); less inflammatory, the healthier storage.

Visceral fat

Around abdominal organs (e.g., the omentum); more hormonally active: impaired lipid and glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, cancer and cardiovascular risk.

  • Android (apple) obesity, more common in males, means more visceral fat: higher risk of CAD, hypertension, stroke and diabetes. Gynoid (pear), more common in females, carries less of that risk.
  • Waist circumference is a cardiovascular risk factor: women over 35 inches, men over 40 inches.

Delirium & Dementia

Delirium (acute confusional state, organic brain syndrome) is a transient disorder of cognition, consciousness or perception, with sudden or gradual onset, usually tied to another condition: drug or alcohol intoxication or withdrawal, hypoglycemia and metabolic problems (liver, kidney: metabolite buildup), brain trauma or surgery, post-anesthesia, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance.

Hyperactive delirium

  • Develops over 2–3 days; ICU, post-surgery, withdrawal, hospitalized elderly; autonomic nervous system overactivity.
  • Risk: benzodiazepines, narcotics, infection or sepsis, surgery, hypoxia, electrolyte or metabolic dysfunction, insomnia.
  • Restless, irritable, poor concentration, insomnia, shaking, poor appetite; then hallucinations, inattention, altered perception; dilated pupils, high heart rate, diaphoresis, fever.
  • Unresolved, it can become excited delirium syndrome (combative, aggressive, rapid breathing): seizures and death.
  • Usually resolves in 2–3 days; can persist weeks if risk factors remain.

Hypoactive delirium

  • Linked to right-sided frontal basal ganglia disruption (coordinated movement and alertness).
  • More common with metabolic disorders (liver or kidney failure: metabolite buildup).
  • Decreased alertness and attention, can't perceive or interpret surroundings, forgetful, apathetic, slow speech, falls asleep often.
  • Treatment: identify and remove the cause and risk factors (lowest opioid dose for the shortest time, especially in the elderly; dialysis for kidney failure; stop offending drugs). Prevent it: avoid CNS-altering drugs, let patients sleep (dark at night), keep normal routines.
  • Antipsychotics (haloperidol/Haldol, quetiapine/Seroquel; IV or PO) only short term, when there's danger to the patient or staff. Symptoms that persist after the acute situation resolves need evaluation for another condition.
  • People with dementia are at higher risk of delirium, but they're different conditions: delirium is acute and transient; dementia is chronic and irreversible. Routines are crucial for dementia patients.

Dementia: acquired, progressive failure of cerebral function (memory, language, judgment, orientation, decision-making). Patients usually aren't aware; family notices. Patients show agitation, wandering, frustration, aggression. Causes: neuron degeneration, compression, cerebral atherosclerosis, trauma, infection (HIV, Creutzfeldt-Jakob), inflammation, genetics. Slow onset, irreversible, no cure: treatment optimizes remaining function. Often mixed.

Pain Pathophysiology

  • Pain is whatever the patient says it is: shaped by physical, cognitive, spiritual, emotional, environmental factors, past experiences and genetics.
  • Acute pain is protective (pulling away from a hot stove; resting a hurt ankle). Chronic pain lasts months to years and isn't protective.
  • Neuroanatomy: afferent pathways (peripheral nerves to the spinal cord and up to the cortex), interpretive centers (brainstem, midbrain, cerebral cortex recognize "pain"), efferent pathways (messages back down that produce the response). The whole process is nociception.
  • Nociceptors = free nerve endings (pain receptors) activated above a threshold by mechanical, thermal (heat, cold) or chemical (acids, inflammatory chemicals) stimuli. Many in skin, dental pulp, meninges and some organs; few in the brain, alveoli and deep tissue.
  • Neurotransmitters are excitatory (enhance pain, sensitize nociceptors) or inhibitory (GABA is the primary inhibitor). Endorphins ("endogenous morphine") are natural opioids that inhibit pain; morphine mimics them.
Phase of NociceptionWhat Happens
1. TransductionA painful stimulus becomes an action potential at the receptor. Injury releases chemical mediators; prostaglandins lower the pain threshold.
2. TransmissionThe impulse travels along A-delta and C fibers to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and up to the thalamus.
3. PerceptionThe somatosensory cortex interprets location and intensity; conscious awareness in the limbic and reticular systems, shaped by attention, distraction, anxiety, fear, fatigue, past experience, genetics, age, culture.
4. ModulationTransmission is amplified or dampened before, during and after perception; endorphins bind opioid receptors and close spinal cord "gates".

A-delta fibers

Small (not the smallest); sharp, well-localized, "fast" pain (stinging, cutting, pinching); can trigger a spinal withdrawal reflex before pain is perceived (curling iron burn, needle stick).

C fibers

Smallest and most numerous (muscles, tendons, organs, skin); dull, aching, burning, poorly localized, constant pain.

  • Pain threshold = the lowest intensity recognized as pain. Perceptual dominance: intense pain in one spot raises the threshold elsewhere (an acute knee masks back pain). Stress, exertion, acupuncture and sexual activity raise the threshold.
  • Pain tolerance = the most pain a person can handle; it varies. Decreased by repeated pain, fatigue, anger, boredom, apprehension, sleep deprivation; increased by alcohol, persistent opioid use, hypnosis, distraction, strong faith.
  • Opioid tolerance: receptor changes after ongoing opioid exposure; doesn't necessarily mean addiction.
  • Gate control theory: stimulating large A-alpha and A-beta fibers (touch, massage, distraction, activity) closes the gate to small-fiber pain signals (why you grab a stubbed toe).
  • Inflammation: injury activates histamine and arachidonic acid metabolites (prostaglandins, leukotrienes). Prostaglandins promote inflammation, pain and fever, but also protect the stomach lining, activate platelets (clotting) and dilate renal vessels (kidney function). COX-1 and COX-2 produce prostanoids that become prostaglandins: the target of NSAIDs.

Opioids

  • Pain ladder: non-opioid ± adjuvant → add an opioid as pain persists → opioid ± non-opioid ± adjuvant for moderate to severe pain.
  • All opioids are high-alert drugs. Originally from the opium poppy; now mostly synthetic. Three chemical classes: morphine-like, meperidine-like, methadone-like: an allergy to one class may not apply to another.
  • Assess LOC, BP, pulse and respirations before and periodically after every dose.
Respiratory depression is the biggest concern (the cause of overdose death). If RR is below 10, assess sedation and use physical stimulation if needed; if the patient is depressed or needs significant stimulation to rouse, reduce the next dose by 25–50%. Initial drowsiness lessens with continued use.

Seizures

  • Seizure = brief abnormal electrical activity in brain neurons (motor, sensory or cognitive). Convulsion = a more severe seizure with involuntary spasmodic muscle contractions. Epilepsy = chronic recurrent seizures. Myoclonic = brief shock-like jerks, often several in a row, mistaken for tics or clumsiness.
  • The seizure focus is a group of neurons firing abnormally and autonomously (often with scar tissue, gliosis); it can recruit nearby neurons so the seizure spreads locally or globally.
  • Primary (idiopathic, about 50%, genetic predisposition) = epilepsy. Secondary = a known cause: blood sugar and chemical imbalances, drugs, fever (febrile seizures in children), TBI, stroke, meningitis, tumors.
  • Epilepsy is a disease of recurrent seizures with no reversible metabolic cause, requiring more than one seizure ("an electrical storm"). EEG shows abnormal activity (nurses needn't read it).
  • Seizure threshold = likelihood of seizing; higher is safer. Raised by sleep and taking AEDs as prescribed. Lowered by bupropion, some antibiotics, tramadol, some anesthetic gases, alcohol, menstruation, missed medication, stress, illness.

Antiepileptic Drugs (AEDs)

  • Goal: control or prevent seizures while maintaining quality of life (risk vs benefit). 70% become seizure-free on one drug; 30% need several.
  • Usually lifelong; may be tapered only under medical supervision after 1–2 seizure-free years. Never stop abruptly: it causes seizures.
  • Not usually started after one seizure (about a 40% chance it won't recur). Chosen by efficacy, side effects, interactions, cost and access (social determinants: stopping because of cost causes seizures).
  • Monotherapy first, slowly titrated; switch drugs rather than pile them on. Almost all need therapeutic drug monitoring (serum levels guide dosing) and frank conversations about taking them every day.
  • Traditional: barbiturates, hydantoins, iminostilbenes, valproic acid. Second and third generation: fewer side effects and interactions (good for older adults on many drugs), but many neurologists still use traditional drugs.
  • AEDs raise the seizure threshold, limit spread and slow nerve impulses; many raise GABA (low GABA is associated with seizures).
  • Black box for all AEDs: suicidal ideation, worsening depression, unusual mood changes: educate and give follow-up steps. AEDs are teratogenic: pregnant patients need close OB and neurology care. Common: dizziness, drowsiness (CNS depression, worse with multiple drugs), GI upset.

Fluids

  • Body fluid (water with glucose, electrolytes, proteins) transports nutrients and wastes, dissolves electrolytes, and supports temperature, digestion, elimination, acid-base balance and lubrication. About 50–60% of adult body weight is water: 70% intracellular, 30% extracellular (interstitial fluid between cells, and intravascular plasma).
  • Osmosis: water moves across a semipermeable membrane from low to high solute concentration until balanced (or hydrostatic pressure opposes it). Diffusion: solute molecules move from high to low concentration; volume stays the same.
  • Colloids (mainly albumin, also globulin, fibrinogen) create oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure, pulling fluid from the interstitial space into the blood. Total protein measures it (not protein nutrition); it falls with age and malnutrition.
  • Hydrostatic pressure (from blood pressure, at the capillary) pushes fluid out of vessels into tissue, delivering nutrients. Oncotic pressure (from plasma proteins) holds fluid in and pulls water back, removing wastes.

Electrolytes

  • Electrolytes are charged in solution; they affect fluid and acid-base balance, nerve impulses, muscle contraction and heart rhythm, and they influence each other (electroneutrality). Levels depend on intake, absorption, distribution and excretion (kidneys).
  • Intracellular: potassium, magnesium, phosphorus. Extracellular: sodium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium. Cations (+): sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium. Anions (−): phosphorus, chloride, bicarbonate. Replace losses with similar fluids and electrolytes.

Skin Disorders: Fungal Infections

  • Fungi are spore producers that live everywhere. They cause problems through implantation (into an injury), inhalation, or after antibiotics wipe out normal flora. More common in the elderly, the immunocompromised (transplant, chemo) and patients with indwelling vascular catheters (central lines).
  • Superficial infections affect only surface layers. Dermatophytes cause tinea; Candida albicans (yeast) causes candidiasis: the two most common. Dermatophytes love keratinized tissue (hair, skin, nails) and warm, dark, moist areas; their enzymes cause scaling, nail breakdown and broken hair. "Ringworm" refers to the ring-shaped rash (no worm). Treat with topical antifungals; extensive or resistant cases need PO or IV antifungals.

  • A diff gives the total WBC (absolute number per mm³) plus the percentage of each of the five types; the percentages always add up to 100.

Coagulation Cascade

What blood is made of

  • Plasma (55%), about 92% water, plus plasma proteins, clotting factors and electrolytes (Na, K, Mg, Ca).
  • Albumin (about 57% of plasma proteins): carrier molecule; regulates water and solutes across capillaries; drives oncotic pressure.
  • Globulins: HDL, prothrombin (a clotting factor), hormone-transport proteins.
  • Fibrinogen: the most plentiful clotting factor.
  • Most plasma proteins are made in the liver, so liver failure leads to clotting problems.
  • Serum = plasma without clotting factors (serum + clotting factors = plasma).
  • Formed elements (45%): RBCs, WBCs, platelets. Blood is only about 8% of body fluid.

Platelets

  • Not true cells: anuclear cytoplasmic fragments with granules that release adhesive proteins, coagulation factors and growth factors when they sense vessel injury.
  • Made in the bone marrow; extras are stored in the spleen.
  • Normal 150,000–400,000. Below about 100,000 = thrombocytopenia, a high bleeding risk (varies by facility).

Platelet activation, step by step

  1. Healthy state: smooth, unactivated platelets circulate freely in the plasma.
  2. A platelet senses vessel damage, so activation begins and adhesion to the injured wall increases.
  3. Its shape and biochemistry change: smooth becomes the spiky dendritic form, which is sticky.
  4. Aggregation: platelets clump with each other and on the vessel wall at the injury.
  5. That activates the clotting system, forming an immobilizing meshwork of platelets + fibrin = a clot.

The clotting cascade

  • Also called the clotting system, coagulation cascade or coagulation system: groups of proteins activate to form a clot.
  • Activated by tissue injury (think: cutting your hand while cooking) or infection; platelets are the primary initiators.
  • End goal: a meshwork of fibrin strands + platelets (often trapping RBCs) that plugs the vessel. Bleeding stopped = hemostasis.
  • Initiated through the intrinsic or extrinsic pathway; thrombin activates fibrinogen into fibrin and is a major drug target.

Stopping the clot

  • Antithrombin III (AT-III): circulating inhibitor of thrombin, so fibrinogen is no longer turned into fibrin.
  • Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI): made by platelets and endothelial cells; inhibits factor Xa ("ten a").
  • Both also inhibit other clotting factors. Deficiencies in these mechanisms lead to bleeding disorders.

The voiceover says "antithrombin 383" and "TFP". These mean antithrombin III and TFPI.

Removing the clot: fibrinolysis

  • The clot forms within minutes; about 20–60 min later the process starts to stop.
  • tPA (tissue plasminogen activator) and uPA (urokinase-like plasminogen activator) turn circulating plasminogen into plasmin.
  • Plasmin breaks down fibrin, releasing the trapped old platelets and RBCs to be degraded.
  • Hear plasmin or tPA, think clot busting.

Coagulation Modifiers

Goals: prevent clot formation (the focus of this course), break apart existing clots (not covered), improve circulation and perfusion, decrease pain, prevent tissue damage. Common indications: atrial fibrillation, heart disease, heart failure, history of DVT, prolonged immobility (e.g. hip or knee surgery), history of stroke.

Anticoagulants

  • Inhibit the action or formation of clotting factors.
  • Prevent clots and the extension of an established clot. They have no effect on an existing clot.

Antiplatelets

  • Prevent the platelet plug by inhibiting aggregation: platelets stop being sticky.
  • Best for preventing heart attack and stroke. Not anticoagulants.
  • Both classes are antithrombotic.

DVT (deep vein thrombosis)A thrombus in a deep vein, usually the lower extremities, most commonly the iliac and femoral veins.
Virchow's triad: three factors that promote venous thrombosis.
  • Venous stasis (slow venous flow): older age, immobility, hospitalized and post-op patients, heart failure.
  • Venous endothelial damage: trauma, certain IV medications, surgery.
  • Hypercoagulable states: inherited clotting disorders, malignancy, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, HRT, recent surgery; genetic mutations affecting prothrombin or antithrombin (deficiencies).

Pathophysiology & signs

  • The thrombus usually forms near a venous valve: inflammation there promotes inappropriate platelet aggregation, and the thrombus grows.
  • Pain, redness, warmth (inflammation), usually in the lower extremity; may be asymptomatic.
  • Significant obstruction raises venous pressure, causing edema in the affected limb.
  • Assess: ask about symptoms and onset, and compare limbs side to side.

Risk factors

  • Clotting disorders; immobility and surgery, especially hip fracture, hip or knee replacement; ortho, spinal cord injury, OB/GYN.
  • Pregnancy and up to 6 weeks postpartum; oral contraceptives; HRT.
  • Overweight or obese, smoking, some cancers and chemo, heart failure, IBD.
  • Personal or family history of DVT; age over 60; varicose (spider) veins.

  • Aggressive mobilization in the hospital and post-op.
  • Monitor bleeding times (PT/INR) for at-risk patients.

Stress & Adaptation

TermMeaning

What shapes coping and adaptive ability

  • Genetics (a more or less efficient relaxation response; a small part).
  • Culture and environment (a child of a parent who panics learns to panic).
  • Prior experiences (a bad dentist visit leads to anticipatory stress).
  • Pre-existing health status (acute or chronic disease already taxing the body).
  • Ability to manage stress: support system, coping skills, logical thinking, personality.

Coping mechanisms

  • Strategies directed at the stressor; effective if they resolve the situation and the feelings about it.
  • Healthy: meditation, exercise, social support, dinner with friends, calling a parent.
  • Unhealthy: drug or alcohol use.
  • Manage stress by avoiding it, changing your reaction, or using healthy coping skills.

General adaptation syndrome: the same response to any stressor

StageWhat happens
1. Alarm ("fight or flight")Initial arousal: a surge of energy to evade or confront a stressor that may be physical, emotional, real, perceived, positive or negative.
  • SNS / adrenergic: the hypothalamus secretes CRH, which activates the SNS; the adrenal medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine.
  • HPA axis: CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release ACTH, so the adrenal cortex releases glucocorticoids (cortisol). This is a negative-feedback loop.
  • SNS effects: pupils dilate, dry mouth, ↑ HR, bronchodilation, ↓ peristalsis and gastric secretions, liver makes glucose, bladder contraction inhibited.
2. ResistanceOngoing stress: the body tries to cope and adjust, but this takes a lot of energy and supplies run out (glucose, epi, norepi, cortisol). Ideally the stressor is removed and the PNS takes over (cholinergic, acetylcholine): the relaxation response lowers HR and BP and improves immunity, sleep and digestion. Hormones take time to fall, so you may feel jittery afterward (a near-miss car accident).
3. ExhaustionThe body can't return to homeostasis. The adrenal cortex hypertrophies (constant stimulation) while lymph tissue atrophies, so infections rise. No energy stores are left, adaptation is lost and disease takes over. Can end in death (the adrenal cortex stops making cortisol and the medulla stops making catecholamines).

PNS = rest and digest, the opposite of SNS: saliva, bronchoconstriction, peristalsis resumes, bile released, bladder contracts.

Stress and memory: glucocorticoids promote epinephrine synthesis, and catecholamines plus glucocorticoids form memories in the brain. That helps us avoid a stressor (the rabbit learns to avoid the tiger) but can create traumatic memories, so PTSD (e.g. after a long ICU stay). In overload, memory is impaired, contributing to major depressive disorder.

Chronic stress and allostatic overload
  • Examples: living in a war zone, daily racism and discrimination, poverty, caregiving for a sick family member.
  • Immunosuppression (cortisol): thymus atrophy, ↓ T cells, infections, autoimmune disorders, poor wound healing. Flares of shingles, cold sores and colds after stress.
  • Physical symptoms: hair loss, stiff neck, tension headaches, asthma triggers, palpitations, tics, GI upset (nausea, diarrhea, constipation, anorexia), irritable bladder, acne, irregular menses or impotence, sleep disturbances.
  • Linked diseases: CAD, HTN, stroke, tension headaches, rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune diseases, IBS, ulcers, sexual disorders, type 2 diabetes.
  • Stress does not cause bleeding ulcers (an old belief), but it can worsen an existing one.

Prolonged cortisol

  • Hyperglycemia: ↑ glucose (opposite of insulin), gluconeogenesis, ↓ peripheral glucose use and muscle/fat uptake; also promotes glycogen storage.
  • Muscle wasting, fragile skin that tears easily, bone breakdown (fragile bones).
  • Fat redistribution from the extremities to the face and trunk: moon face, buffalo hump.

Sleep deprivation

  • Increases stress levels; very common in the hospital (noise, frequent waking, lost privacy) and among night-shift nurses.
  • Irritability, apathy, lack of interest, poor memory and judgment, hallucinations, changes in mentation.

Steroids Pharmacology

Systemic steroids (PO or IV) act body-wide. Inhaled steroids give a localized lung effect with little or no systemic effect.

Why we give steroids

  • Replacement: the adrenal gland isn't working, or can't keep up after prolonged stress.
  • Anti-inflammatory: systemic inflammatory response, COPD exacerbation (IV, then PO), chronic IBD, rheumatic disorders.
  • Immunosuppression: allergies; post-transplant to prevent rejection (weaned off if there's no rejection).
  • Asthma: usually inhaled.

Replacement dosing: mimic the adrenal gland

  • Cortisol rises with stress, so steroid doses must increase during illness or extra stress: the patient's adrenal gland won't make more.
  • Methods: alternate-day therapy, multiple small doses, or ⅔ of the daily dose in the AM and ⅓ in the PM (mimics the dawn burst).
  • Mineralocorticoids: usually once daily, and often not replaced.

  • Fluid retention, high BP (caution in heart failure).

  • Hyperglycemia: diabetes worsens or develops; expect ↑ glucose with IV steroids.

Basic GI Pharmacology

Constipation

  • Small, infrequent or difficult BMs, generally fewer than 3 per week. Know the patient's own normal pattern.
  • Causes: low-fiber diet (fiber adds bulk), lack of exercise (exercise stimulates peristalsis), slowed peristalsis in the elderly, obstruction, diverticulitis.
  • Unrelieved, it leads to fecal impaction: a firm, immovable mass blocks the lower GI tract. Liquid stool can ooze around it, so ask whether it's true diarrhea.
  • Impaction signs: anorexia, N/V, abdominal distention, cramping, rectal pain.

Diarrhea

  • ↑ frequency and fluidity of BMs; often the first sign of a bigger GI disorder.
  • Acute: viral or bacterial infection, emotional stress, drugs (antibiotics, antacids, chemo), impaction.
  • Chronic: lasts more than 4 weeks: chronic GI infection, motility or integrity changes, malabsorption, endocrine disorders.
  • Episodic: food allergies, irritants (caffeine, tube feedings).
  • Ask in a sensitive, nonjudgmental way.
  • Concerns: skin breakdown, fluid and electrolyte loss, nutrition, especially in the very young, very old and debilitated.

Hypersensitivities

Hypersensitivity = a normal immune response that is inappropriately triggered, excessive (out of proportion; e.g. sepsis, where the response and its mediators do more damage than the infection), or produces damaging effects. It is triggered by a specific antigen–antibody or antibody–lymphocyte interaction, is specific to one antigen, and usually doesn't happen on first exposure (the second bee sting). Types I–III are mediated by antibodies from B cells (B cells mature into plasma cells, the "antibody factories"); Type IV is mediated by T cells and is delayed. Memory aid: B cells = antibodies; T cells = long-term immunity.

Type I mediators and symptoms

  • Vasodilation: stuffy nose, welts, drop in BP.
  • ↑ vascular permeability: fluid leaks out, adding to hypotension and runny nose.
  • Bronchial smooth-muscle constriction: wheezing, trouble breathing.
  • Irritant receptors stimulated: pruritus; urticaria (hives), swelling, redness.
  • Allergy shots build tolerance (↓ sensitivity); allergy medications (antihistamines) block the response.

Local vs systemic Type I

  • Local (atopic): allergic rhinitis. About 50% of people in the US react to one of the 10 most common allergens. Triggers: pollen, dust, mold, dander, peanuts, penicillin.
  • Systemic = anaphylaxis, a medical emergency: laryngeal edema (can't breathe) plus vascular collapse with severe hypotension. Most common triggers: medications, bee stings, foods; tiny doses can trigger it within minutes. Itching and hives progress fast to bronchoconstriction and swelling.
  • Treat with antihistamines, glucocorticoids, epinephrine. Teach: MedicAlert bracelet; carry EpiPens.
Transfusion reaction (Type II): stop the transfusion immediately at any sign. Stay with the patient for the first 15 min; take temperature before and 15 min after starting. Fever, chills or flushing are often first; watch HR and BP changes, chest or back pain (clumped cells block small vessels), restlessness, anxiety, headache.
Type II vs Type III: both form antigen–antibody complexes. In Type II the complex attacks the cell directly. In Type III the complexes deposit in tissue and the inflammation causes the damage.

Epinephrine: giving it safely

  • Side effects: dysrhythmias, angina or chest pain, hypertension, tachycardia, stroke risk; nervousness, restlessness, tremor.
  • Routes: every route except PO: IM, IV, subQ (often preferred), even down an ET tube.
  • Anaphylaxis: lowest dose; adult 0.2–1 mg; peds weight-based 0.01 mg/kg.
  • 1:1,000 (1 mg/1 mL) for anaphylaxis vs 1:10,000 for cardiac arrest. IV solutions are less concentrated: concentrated epi IV can be fatal (severe dysrhythmias or HTN). An IM dose given IV could kill.

EpiPen teaching

  • Teach the patient and family how to use it; use exactly as directed.
  • Use only when symptoms truly occur (it's a dangerous drug).
  • Outer thigh, through clothing; hold 10 seconds. One dose per pen; often a 2-pack plus a trainer.
  • Seek medical attention after every use: short half-life, so symptoms can return.
  • Carry it at all times (e.g. hiking with a bee allergy); wear a MedicAlert ID.

  • Acidic urine (low pH) and urea.
  • Males: prostatic secretions (more protective). Females: urethral gland secretions.

Overactive bladder & incontinence

The bladder is smooth muscle (the detrusor) that holds about 300–500 mL before signaling the need to void; pelvic floor muscles underneath help control the bladder and bowel.

Overactive bladder

  • Bladder muscles contract involuntarily even at low volume, causing urgency.
  • Causes: neurologic disorders (stroke), diabetes, UTIs, menopause hormone changes, bladder abnormalities (tumors, stones), outflow obstruction.
  • Signs: sudden urge, urgency incontinence, frequency (8 or more times in 24 h), nocturia (waking more than 2 times a night).

Mirabegron

  • Bladder antispasmodic: selectively stimulates beta-3 adrenergic receptors, relaxing bladder smooth muscle.
  • PO, 25–50 mg/day.
  • Most serious AE: hypertension. Monitor BP; a poor choice for patients with HTN.
  • Also urinary retention, UTIs, headache.

Incontinence = involuntary loss of urine. It is not a normal part of aging, though age-related changes can contribute.

Depression

Facts

  • More than 17 million people in the US; women 2× (men under-report). More common in white people and those never married.
  • Leading cause of disability worldwide; high suicide risk.
  • A risk factor for cardiovascular disease: 64% greater risk of CAD.
  • Etiology: a combination of genetic (family history), biological, environmental and psychological factors; neurochemical, situational, chronic or acute; or a medication side effect.
  • Types: major depressive disorder, persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia), premenstrual dysphoric disorder, substance- or medication-induced, due to another medical condition, postpartum.

Diagnosis, symptoms, treatment

  • Requires multiple symptoms intense enough to cause distress and persistently impair psychosocial functioning (not one bad exam).
  • Symptoms: loss of interest or pleasure, fatigue or restlessness and irritability, poor concentration, low self-esteem, negative thinking, sleep and appetite disturbances (either direction).
  • Treatment: medication (takes 4–8 weeks for full effect, so keep taking it), psychotherapy (CBT), psychoeducation, support groups, brain stimulation for refractory cases. Combinations tailored to the patient work best.

Depression & Anxiety Pharmacology

Rules for every antidepressant
  • Combination therapy works best (medication plus CBT or EMDR). Most "antidepressants" treat both depression and anxiety.
  • SSRIs and SNRIs are first-line because they're the safest.
  • All carry a suicide risk and risk of mental imbalance (they alter neurotransmitters): assess before starting, during adjustment and over time.
  • Not effective right away: evaluate efficacy over 4–8 weeks. If it isn't working, increase the dose, switch within the class, change class, or add a second drug.
  • Withdrawal syndrome if stopped abruptly: dizziness, headache, sensory disturbances (hands or feet "on fire"), shaking or tremor, anxiety, dysphoria. Patients must talk with the provider before stopping (cost, access, dislike).
  • No common suffix in any class: memorize which drug belongs where.

  • Schedule IV, moderate abuse risk; teratogenic (not in pregnancy).

Dangers & antidote

  • CNS depression, ↓ LOC, respiratory depression (more with IV); addictive, with withdrawal; memory loss.
  • Don't combine with other CNS depressants: Benadryl, alcohol, opioids, barbiturates (can stop breathing).
  • CYP450: avoid grapefruit and fatty foods (affect absorption).
  • Antidote: flumazenil.

Substance Use Disorder & Withdrawal

Addiction is a disease

  • Substance use disorder (SUD) is a disease of brain and behavior: a complex interaction of environmental, biological, genetic and brain-chemistry factors. It is not a personal failure or weakness.
  • Dopamine (reward): substances cause a big, rapid dopamine surge far beyond normal pleasure. Over time normal pathways downregulate, so more and more frequent use is needed for the same surge.
  • Also linked to prefrontal cortex changes.
  • Brain scans: dopamine-transporter activity is low after 1 month without meth and returns by 14 months, so brain changes are reversible.
  • Use person-first language ("a person using meth").

Risk vs protective factors

  • Risk: aggressive behavior in childhood, lack of parental supervision, poor social skills, drug experimentation, drug availability at school, community poverty.
  • Protective: good self-control, parental monitoring and support, positive relationships, good grades, school anti-drug policies, neighborhood resources.
  • Think upstream: these are proxies for social determinants of health. Addiction also depends on the drug itself (type, route, effect, age at first use, availability, cost).

Treatment principles

  • No single treatment fits everyone: individualize and treat the whole person.
  • Therapy is the crux: CBT, contingency management and positive reinforcement, motivational enhancement (readiness to change), family therapy, 12-step programs. Medication goes hand in hand with these.
  • Medications differ by substance: opioids (methadone, buprenorphine, naltrexone, lofexidine), nicotine (buprenorphine, nicotine replacement patches and gum), alcohol (naltrexone, disulfiram). The full chart isn't required.

Medically assisted detox and screening

  • Medically assisted detox: withdrawal in a structured, medically supervised setting; the safe way to manage acute, possibly life-threatening withdrawal. Not every rehab is medically assisted.
  • Screen for HIV/AIDS, hepatitis B and C, TB, endocarditis: no access to clean needles and equipment spreads bloodborne pathogens. Endocarditis is common with long-term IV drug use.

Opioid withdrawal

  • Severe flu-like symptoms: N/V, stomach cramps, diarrhea, goosebumps, depression, cravings, anxiety, sweating, muscle aches, fever.
  • Supportive treatment: treat each symptom (antiemetics, antipyretics) plus medication-assisted therapy.

Benzo & alcohol withdrawal (both act on GABA, treated the same)

  • Manageable: sleep disturbance, dry retching, irritability, nausea, tension, weight loss, anxiety, palpitations, panic attacks, headache, muscle aches, sweating, poor concentration, perceptual changes.
  • Dangerous: seizures, hallucinations, delirium tremens (DTs), Wernicke's encephalopathy.
  • Treat with a benzodiazepine taper (prevents seizures, lessens DTs), plus 12-step or therapy.
  • Alcohol: vitamin supplementation, especially thiamine (chronic stomach inflammation impairs absorption).

Wernicke's encephalopathy triad

  1. Profound disorientation, indifference, inattention.
  2. Oculomotor dysfunction: nystagmus, lateral rectus palsy, conjugate gaze palsy.
  3. Gait ataxia.

Delirium tremens (DTs)

  • Hallucinations, disorientation.
  • Tachycardia, hypertension, fever.
  • Agitation, diaphoresis.

Sign vs symptom?
Sign = objective (what you see, hear, touch). Symptom = subjective (what the patient tells you).
A virus stays dormant and later reactivates as shingles. What stage is that?
Latent (the chickenpox virus lying in wait).
Five steps of the inflammatory response?
Mediators released, vasodilation and permeability, leukocyte migration (chemotaxis), phagocytosis, inflammatory exudate.
Frequent, very watery, sometimes orange-tinged diarrhea after antibiotics. What's the concern?
C. difficile: antibiotics killed the normal flora. Diagnosed by stool toxin.
What does a CYP450 inducer do?
It's the 'gas pedal': it increases enzyme activity (an inhibitor decreases it).
Narrow therapeutic index: meaning and an example?
A small window between therapeutic and toxic levels (warfarin, digoxin): monitor closely.
Schedule II drugs: refills?
No refills: a new prescription each time (opioids, amphetamines).
What is displacement?
A second drug pushes the first off albumin, increasing the free drug's effect.
Most common reason to switch off an ACE inhibitor?
A dry, persistent, nonproductive cough. Also watch for angioedema and hyperkalemia; not in pregnancy.
Statin adverse effects to ask about?
Myopathy (muscle weakness), rhabdomyolysis (can cause AKI), hepatotoxicity (AST/ALT).
RAAS in one line?
Kidney renin turns angiotensinogen into angiotensin I; ACE in the lungs makes angiotensin II (vasoconstriction plus aldosterone, sodium and water retention), so BP rises.
LDL goal for a high-risk patient?
Below 70 (usually below 130, often below 100).
Rapid-acting lispro: when do you give it?
With meals, when the tray is in front of them. Given before a meal they don't eat, it causes hypoglycemia.
The only IV insulin?
Regular (short-acting): IV bolus or infusion (DKA), and the insulin in pumps.
Hypoglycemic and unable to swallow?
Glucagon IM or IV dextrose.
Leptin in obesity?
Rises with fat but causes leptin resistance, so satiety signaling fails and overeating continues.
Donepezil vs memantine?
Donepezil = cholinesterase inhibitor (more ACh), for mild to moderate Alzheimer's. Memantine = NMDA blocker (blocks excess glutamate), for moderate to severe.
Daily acetaminophen limit?
4 g/24 h (2 g with liver disease or chronic alcohol use), counting Norco, Lortab, Percocet.
Opioid overdose reversal?
Naloxone (Narcan): an opioid antagonist at mu receptors.
Phenytoin: therapeutic level and a classic teaching point?
10–20 mcg/mL. Gingival hyperplasia, so very good dental care.
CBC with diff: WBC 13,000, segs and bands up. Interpretation?
Bands present = shift to the left: an acute bacterial infection.
Eosinophils up?
'Worms, wheezes and weird diseases': parasites, allergic reactions or asthma.
Neutropenic precautions?
ANC below 1,000: pristine hand hygiene, no sick contacts, no raw fruits, vegetables or grains, no fresh flowers, door shut.
Fluconazole: key toxicity and interactions?
Liver (azoLe = Liver): monitor AST/ALT. CYP450: more bleeding with warfarin, hypoglycemia with sulfonylureas, higher Haldol and Dilantin levels.
Heparin antidote and how to give it?
Protamine sulfate, IV slowly (a fast push causes profound hypotension).
Platelets drop below 100,000 on heparin, with new thrombi in the toes. What now?
HIT: stop heparin immediately and use argatroban IV.
Warfarin teaching about vitamin K foods?
A consistent, balanced intake, not total avoidance. Therapeutic INR is about 2–3.5.
Virchow's triad?
Venous stasis, endothelial damage, hypercoagulable state.
A patient on long-term steroids gets sick. What happens to the dose?
It needs to increase: the adrenal gland can't make more. Never stop abruptly; taper.
Which laxative is OK long term, and what's the key teaching?
Bulk-forming (psyllium): take it with plenty of water.
Type I vs Type IV hypersensitivity: timing and cells?
Type I: IgE and mast cells, about 15–20 min. Type IV: T cells, no antibodies, 48–72 h (TB skin test).
Benzo and alcohol withdrawal: treatment and supplement?
A benzodiazepine taper (prevents seizures, lessens DTs), plus thiamine for alcohol.
