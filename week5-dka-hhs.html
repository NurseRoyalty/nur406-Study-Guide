DKA & HHS

This page follows the DKA and HHS voiceover, which compares and contrasts the two throughout: what each is and who gets it, the insulin and counter-regulatory hormone background, why DKA makes ketones and HHS doesn't, the anion gap, plasma osmolality and sodium, potassium, precipitating factors, how each presents, treatment, and how you know each has resolved.

DKA vs HHS: What They Are & Who Gets Them

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

  • A crisis that occurs as an acute metabolic complication of diabetes, with ketosis.
  • More common in people under 65, and characteristically associated with type 1 diabetes.
  • Can occur in type 2 under extreme stress: really serious infections, sepsis, trauma, cardiovascular or other emergencies. Type 2 diabetics still make insulin, which is typically enough to prevent fat breakdown into ketones unless the situation is extreme (for example, a type 2 diabetic who requires insulin and gets a serious infection).
  • About 30% of all DKA cases occur in people with type 2 diabetes, particularly those taking SGLT2 inhibitors (the "-flozins," like dapagliflozin).

Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)

  • Also called hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome; in the past, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar non-ketotic (HHNK). HHS is the name you'll see most.
  • Another life-threatening complication of diabetes, but with no ketosis.
  • Mostly type 2 diabetes, and more common over 65.
  • Pretty rare.

Background: Insulin, Glucagon & Counter-Regulatory Hormones

  • Extracellular glucose is primarily regulated by two hormones: insulin and glucagon.
  • After a meal, glucose enters the pancreatic beta cells, which leads to insulin release. Insulin restores normal blood glucose by reducing hepatic glucose production (less glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis) and by increasing glucose uptake by skeletal muscle and adipose tissue.
  • Gluconeogenesis turns non-carbohydrate substrates (amino acids, lactate, glycerol) into glucose. Glycogenolysis breaks glycogen down into glucose, which the body can use for energy.
  • In times of stress, more insulin is needed than usual; in diabetics that insulin isn't available, and DKA or HHS can develop.
  • In both, the main problem is that insulin isn't working properly: in DKA the body isn't making enough; in HHS the insulin that is available isn't working effectively.
  • On top of that, the counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol, growth hormone) go up. They increase hepatic glucose production and impair the peripheral tissues' ability to use glucose.
  • Dehydration and electrolyte imbalances occur primarily from the osmotic diuresis caused by glycosuria (glucose in the urine).

The lecture uses the word "gluconeogenesis" twice in a row; the second definition (glycogen broken down into glucose) is glycogenolysis, which is how it is listed here.

Why DKA Makes Ketones & HHS Doesn't

  • DKA is marked by increased gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and ketogenesis, with reduced glycolysis: that is the "keto" in ketoacidosis.
  • The insulin deficiency is absolute in type 1 and relative in type 2, and the excess counter-regulatory hormones add to it.
  • Suppressing lipolysis and ketogenesis is more sensitive to insulin than suppressing gluconeogenesis. In HHS, the residual insulin is enough to minimize ketoacidosis but not enough to control the hyperglycemia.
StepKetone Production
1Insulin deficiency and resistance mean glucose can't get into the cells, which need it for energy.
2Without glucose, the body breaks down fat for energy (lipolysis), releasing free fatty acids and glycerol from peripheral fat stores.
3The free fatty acids travel to the liver, are taken up by hepatocytes and activated.
4They are converted into acetyl-CoA, enter the ketogenic pathway and form ketone bodies.

Why not in HHS? One theory: there may be enough insulin activity to minimize lipolysis (and so ketone formation), but not enough to block gluconeogenesis, promote glucose use and prevent the hyperglycemia.

Why HHS Glucose Runs Higher

DKA

  • Clients present earlier, with symptoms of ketoacidosis: shortness of breath, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting.
  • Because they seek treatment earlier, glucose is usually 350 to 450 (can be as high as 800).
  • Tend to be younger with a higher GFR, so a greater capacity to excrete glucose.

HHS

  • Clients present later, without those early ketoacidosis symptoms.
  • Glucose is typically higher: it can exceed 1,000.
  • Glycosuria first limits the rise in glucose, but the osmotic diuresis causes volume depletion and a lower GFR, which limits glucose excretion. The effect is more pronounced in HHS.

Anion Gap, Plasma Osmolality & Sodium

LabWhat the Lecture Says
Anion gapDKA typically presents as an elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis. The anion gap is the difference between negatively and positively charged electrolytes, calculated from sodium, chloride and bicarb (sodium minus chloride plus bicarb), and reported on the blood chemistry panel.
Beta-hydroxybutyrateKetones are no longer really measured directly; in DKA, beta-hydroxybutyrate (one of the main metabolic products in ketoacidosis) is measured instead.
Closing the gapKetoacid anion excretion depends on volume status and GFR. Isotonic IV fluids correct the hypovolemia, increase renal excretion of ketoacid anions and reduce the anion gap.
Plasma osmolalityIncreased in both, in proportion to the glucose; usually not as high in DKA because the glucose isn't as high.
SodiumHyperglycemia pulls water out of the cells and expands the extracellular fluid, so plasma sodium falls: a dilutional hyponatremia. It isn't treated directly, because it corrects as the hyperglycemia is corrected.

Potassium: High on Arrival, Depleted Underneath

Most body potassium is in the cells. Clients with DKA and HHS present with a normal to elevated serum potassium but a significant total body potassium depletion, for three reasons:

ReasonWhat Happens
AcidosisIn metabolic acidosis, hydrogen ions move into the cells and potassium moves out to balance the charge, raising serum potassium. Working kidneys can offset this by excreting the excess.
Insulin deficiencyInsulin normally moves potassium into the cells; without it, potassium stays in the extracellular fluid, raising serum levels further.
HyperosmolarityThe osmotic diuresis from high glucose causes massive urinary losses, and potassium is lost continuously in the urine, depleting the total body supply.
  • Serum potassium can be 5, 6 or beyond on arrival. Don't bring it down unless it's causing problems and the client is symptomatic: it returns toward normal as the pH is corrected.
  • Once treatment starts, insulin drives potassium back into the cells and the serum level falls quickly, which can cause severe hypokalemia. If the kidneys have already excreted potassium, there is even less left to move back in.
  • As soon as the potassium reaches a normal level, replacement typically starts, because it will keep moving into the cells and the serum still needs a normal level to prevent cardiac problems.
  • Watch the client: are they symptomatic, and are there EKG changes of hyper- or hypokalemia (including dysrhythmias)?

The hydrogen-potassium shift was first covered in Acid-Base Balance (Week 2)

Precipitating Factors

  • A precipitating factor can often be identified.
  • Infection is the most common: severe pneumonia or a UTI without an insulin adjustment.
  • Inflammatory events: MI, CVA, sepsis, pancreatitis, without an insulin adjustment.
  • Poor compliance with insulin, or increased insulin needs that aren't met. Compliance can be checked with a hemoglobin A1c.

Clinical Presentation

DKA: evolves rapidly, over about 24 hours

  • GI: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, from delayed gastric emptying or an ileus induced by the metabolic acidosis.
  • Neurologic: slight confusion up to obtunded, with severe acidosis.
  • Volume depletion: decreased skin turgor, decreased urine output, dry oral mucosa, tachycardia, hypotension.
  • Fruity breath (some say it smells like nail polish remover) from exhaled acetone.
  • Kussmaul respirations: really deep respirations and hyperventilation, compensating for the metabolic acidosis.

HHS: begins several days before admission

  • Marked hyperglycemia with the typical signs: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, plus weakness.
  • As it progresses: lethargy, obtunded, up to coma. Neurologic symptoms are most common in HHS because of the hyperosmolarity.
  • Extracellular volume depletion: dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes, hypotension, tachycardia.
  • Symptoms of a coexisting illness that may have precipitated it (pneumonia, UTI) are usually present.

Treatment

Hospitals usually have a protocol and algorithm; follow your hospital's protocol. The lecturer cites the University of Kentucky (UK) protocol for the potassium numbers.

How You Know It's Resolved

DKA: look at the labs

Make sure the ketoacidosis has resolved: venous pH or the anion gap, and the level of ketoacidosis by beta-hydroxybutyrate, which falls as the acidosis is corrected.

HHS: look at the patient

Are they mentally alert and back to baseline? Also check the plasma osmolality: the goal is about 315, which the lecture calls a normal level.

Once the client can eat regularly, IV insulin is changed to subQ insulin.

HHS: Why Hydration Matters So Much

  • If adequate hydration isn't maintained, dehydration and worsening renal function follow.
  • Clients with poor fluid intake from altered thirst mechanisms or inability to access fluids (as may be seen in the elderly) are at increased risk of severe dehydration.
  • Diminished renal filtration further impairs glucose excretion, which exacerbates the hyperglycemia.
