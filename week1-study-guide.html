Part 1 — Myocarditis, Pericarditis & Cardiac Tamponade

Topic 1, the first lecture of NUR 406: myocarditis, then the pericardium, how inflammation leads to pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade, the bedside signs, pulsus paradoxus, and treatment.

Myocarditis

Think back to normal A&P: the myocardium is the muscle layer of the heart, the muscle cells. Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease of the myocardium.

FeatureWhat the Lecture Says
Range of severityA wide variety of symptoms: it can be a pretty mild disorder with little to no discomfort, all the way up to heart failure and a lethal condition.
What happens to the cellsThe inflammation comes with degeneration and necrosis of the cardiac myocytes. The inflammation causes direct injury to the myocytes, and over time it can create fibrosis and scarring that lead to structural changes as well.
ConductionWe often see a disruption in conduction through the heart. The tissue changes, inflammatory cytokines and T cell activation in the myocardium can result in abnormal electrical conduction.
CauseTypically viruses. In the United States and Europe, viruses are the leading cause of myocarditis. The Coxsackie virus A and B are the majority of cases.
TreatmentDepends on how severe the symptoms are. Treated by giving antivirals; anti-inflammatories can also be used.

The Pericardium & Pericardial Fluid

The pericardium is a sac-like membrane that surrounds the heart and the roots of the major blood vessels, and it helps the heart function properly. Between its inner and outer layers is the pericardial space.

StageFluid in the Pericardial SpaceWhat It Does to the Heart
NormalA small amount, about 15 to 50 mL.Acts like a lubricant between the inner and outer layers, for normal function of the heart.
Pericardial effusionWhen the pericardium is inflamed, fluid accumulates in excess of what is normal. Effusion is that excess fluid accumulation.Can compress the heart chambers and limit cardiac filling.
Cardiac tamponadeAbout 200 mL.The fluid compresses the heart. The chambers are restricted and can't stretch and fill with blood as they normally would.
Cardiac tamponade is a medical emergency. The fluid has to be removed quickly, because the heart chambers can't stretch and fill with blood.

Acute Pericarditis vs Cardiac Tamponade

The lecture describes what acute pericarditis looks like, then what changes once it progresses to cardiac tamponade and cardiac output falls.

Acute pericarditis

  • Chest pain: a sharp pain that worsens with deep breathing.
  • Fever, because of the inflammatory process.
  • Dyspnea (the lecture glosses it as "some painful breathing").
  • Pericardial friction rub on auscultation: a scratching sound, almost like rubbing on a balloon.
  • Muffled heart sounds, because you are listening to the heart pump through a layer of fluid.
  • ECG changes: elevations and also depressions.
  • Pulsus paradoxus is also named as a sign of acute pericarditis.

Cardiac tamponade

  • Signs of decreased cardiac output: hypotension, and the usual signs of a patient who is not being perfused appropriately, such as confusion and dizziness.
  • Jugular vein distension, from blood backing up in the system because of the decreased cardiac output.
  • Muffled heart sounds, from listening through a layer of fluid.
  • Pulsus paradoxus occurs in about 70 to 80 percent of patients experiencing cardiac tamponade.

Pulsus Paradoxus

QuestionWhat the Lecture Says
What it isA decrease in the systolic blood pressure when the patient breathes in (with inspiration).
Why it's a "paradox"Typically your blood pressure should increase a little bit when you breathe in. Seeing a drop instead is the paradox.
What it indicatesThe heart is not pumping properly, and something is impacting cardiac filling.
How you see itMost clearly with an arterial line (an IV catheter in an artery), which gives real-time blood pressure: you can watch the pressure change as the patient breathes in.
How oftenAbout 70 to 80 percent of patients experiencing cardiac tamponade.

The recording garbles the exact size of the systolic drop ("a decrease in the systolic blood pressure of more and more"), so no number is given here. If the slide shows a value, it can be added.

Pericarditis on the ECG

The ECG in pericarditis shows elements of ST depression as well as ST elevation. In some cases it looks similar to a patient having an MI, but the clinical symptoms will be different.

Treating Pericarditis

TreatmentWhat the Lecture Says
NSAIDs (anti-inflammatories)Anti-inflammatories are used. Aspirin may be seen occasionally.
Corticosteroids"Give or take": sometimes they work really well, sometimes not so much.
ColchicineTypically a gout medication, but recently found to work well for pericarditis that is not responding to NSAIDs.
PericardiocentesisFor cardiac tamponade caused by the pericarditis. A needle is put directly into the pericardium and the fluid is drawn out. Because tamponade is a medical emergency, this happens quickly.

Part 2A — Acute Coronary Syndrome

Topic 2, first half: what ACS is, the spectrum from Prinzmetal's angina to STEMI, what makes plaque unstable and why it ruptures, unstable angina, and how ACS differs from stable angina.

What ACS Is

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)

  • An umbrella term for situations in which the blood supply to the heart muscle is suddenly blocked or compromised.
  • It is certainly a medical emergency.
  • The blockage can come and go, or it can be sudden and complete. Either way, cardiac tissue is dying.
  • ACS covers three conditions: unstable angina, non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).
  • The three have a similar pathophysiology, so the signs and symptoms can look similar. The ECG is what helps determine the next step: send the patient to the cath lab for reperfusion, or treat medically?
"Time is tissue." The instructor says it several times: treat the blockage as quickly as possible to save as much tissue as possible.

The ACS Spectrum

The slide shows a progression from a normal vessel to complete occlusion. Stable angina has been covered before; ACS is the part of the picture where stable angina has become unstable.

What Makes Plaque Unstable

Usually, when plaque becomes unstable, people develop ACS. These are people who already have atherosclerotic plaque in their coronary vessels.

Stable plaque

  • A pretty thick fibrous cap on top of the fat, which contributes to its stability.

Unstable plaque

  • The lipid core (the fat inside the vessel) gets bigger.
  • As the core grows, the fibrous cap gets thinner.
  • There is a component of inflammation; C-reactive protein (CRP) is a marker of inflammation in the body.
  • Smooth muscle cells proliferate into the intima (the lecture describes the intima as the middle layer of the blood vessel). These cells create the fibrous cap and bring in inflammatory components, increasing the risk of plaque rupture.

Assessing Unstable Plaque

  • A study that puts dye into the vessel shows how much narrowing is happening, so we can roughly estimate whether the lipid core is larger or smaller.
  • CRP levels show how much inflammation is going on.

Why Plaque Ruptures

Rupture risk is higher with unstable plaque: a large lipid core, a thin fibrous cap, and ongoing inflammation. The slide walks through the theory step by step.

StepWhat Happens
1. TriggerPsychological stress, exercise, or circadian rhythms stimulate the sympathetic nervous system.
2. Sympathetic responseEpinephrine and norepinephrine are released: blood pressure and heart rate rise, and the heart works harder with stronger contractions.
3. Force on the vesselsPushing out more blood with stronger contractions puts more force on the vessels, including the coronary vessels themselves.
4. RuptureThat force can lead to rupture of the plaque.
5. PlateletsPlatelets come to the site and adhere to the ruptured plaque. They release substances that attract more platelets and can contribute to vasospasm.
6. ThrombusA thrombus forms in that same area.

Unstable Angina

What counts as unstable angina

  • Chest pain occurring for the first time. Consider it unstable immediately, until proven otherwise; it is a medical emergency because no pattern or history has been established.
  • In a patient who normally has stable angina, pain that is more severe than normal: for example, they have taken their first nitro and five minutes later the pain hasn't stopped or is getting worse.
  • Pain that is different: usually in the chest, but this time down one arm, down both arms, or radiating into the jaw.
  • It may happen at rest; the person may be awakened in the middle of the night.

What's happening in the vessel

  • The plaque ruptures and a thrombus forms. That rupture and clot formation is the real worry.
  • The size of the clot and of the blockage determine how much blood gets past to perfuse the tissue on the other side. A 30% occluded artery may still allow some flow around a clot; a 90% blockage leaves little room.
  • If there is no infarction, the blockage wasn't complete or the clot dissolved, and some tissue was still perfused.
  • The ECG changes and then goes back to normal, and we typically don't see troponin changes.
Unstable angina is a medical emergency. Teach patients to recognize the signs, recognize it as an emergency, and call 911 right now. They should most definitely not drive themselves to the ER.

Stable Angina vs ACS

Teach patients the difference so they can identify it and seek care. The big difference is the pain: its severity and its duration.

Stable angina

  • Pain should last about 5 minutes, and be gone by maybe about 15 minutes.
  • May feel like a little bit of chest tightness.
  • Flares up with strenuous activity.

ACS

  • Chest pain lasts a lot longer.
  • No relief with nitroglycerin.
  • More severe: the sensation of an elephant sitting on your chest.
  • Other symptoms: diaphoresis, shortness of air, nausea and vomiting.
  • An impending sense of doom: they know something is not right but can't pinpoint it. Take them very, very seriously.

Signs in Men and Women

Research over roughly the last 50 years shows different signs and symptoms of ACS between men and women. These symptoms aren't limited to one or the other.

Typically associated with men

  • Chest pain.
  • Tingling or discomfort in the back, arms, neck or jaw.

Other symptoms (often reported by women)

  • Dizziness, nausea, vomiting, tiredness.
  • Heartburn: a lot of women with ACS say they took some Tums that morning because they thought it was heartburn.

Some of these can feel similar to stable angina. The particular concern is when the experience changes or gets worse.

Part 2B — Myocardial Infarction

Topic 2, second half: the signs of MI, what an acute MI is, what happens to heart tissue over time, the zones of damage, STEMI vs NSTEMI, and the LAD.

Signs & Symptoms of Myocardial Infarction

What the lecture lists

  • Diaphoresis and dyspnea.
  • Extreme anxiety; restless, can't sit still, moving around.
  • Impending sense of doom ("I know something's wrong") without being able to pinpoint it.
  • Levine sign: the fist to the chest, the "Hollywood" heart attack sign.
  • Pallor and weak pulses, from perfusion issues because the heart isn't working right.
  • Retrosternal (behind the sternum) crushing chest pain that can radiate to several parts of the body; the elephant on the chest.

Acute Myocardial Infarction

What an acute MI is

  • Ruptured plaque plus thrombus that severely reduces or completely blocks blood flow to the heart muscle.
  • Think of ACS as a continuum; MI is at the end of that continuum.
  • It is ACS with prolonged ischemia that results in the death of myocardial tissue: an irreversible ischemic necrosis. These cells are never going to function properly again.
  • Infarction happens either because blood flow disruption goes on over a very long period of time, or because of a complete and total blockage.
  • How much tissue is lost depends on: where the infarction is (smaller or larger vessel, left or right side of the heart), how long blood supply has been affected, and how complete the blockage is. Partial flow over a few days can damage tissue, and so can a more immediate, complete occlusion.
  • MIs are classified by ECG findings: STEMI and NSTEMI.
FindingUnstable AnginaMyocardial Infarction
ECGChanges, then eventually goes back to normal.Changes that stay; a repeat ECG won't go back to normal.
TroponinTypically no change in cardiac enzymes.Increases. Serial troponins trend up during the infarction, then trend down after the occlusion has been rectified.
TissueNo infarction: the blockage wasn't complete or the clot dissolved.Death of myocardial tissue, which is irreversible.

Ischemia, Infarction & ATP

Ischemia

  • When the oxygen supply does not meet the heart's metabolic demands.

Infarction

  • Tissue death that results from that ischemia.

Why ATP Matters

  • ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a molecule that stores and transfers energy in cells; it is the main energy source for cellular function.
  • Myocardial cells cannot store ATP. They must be continuously fed ATP, so they need a continuous supply of oxygen to make it.
  • When oxygen goes down, ATP supply goes down, but the heart can't stop beating while ATP isn't being produced.

What Happens to Heart Tissue Over Time

TimeWhat Happens
Acute ischemiaATP depletes instantly.
1 to 2 minutesDecreased ability of the heart to contract.
30 minutes up to 4 hoursIrreversible injury to cardiac tissue. By 4 hours, necrosis has begun and the tissue begins to die.
1 to 2 weeksThe necrotic tissue is cleared away; the myocardium is very weak and susceptible to rupture.
About 6 weeksThe necrotic tissue has been replaced by tough scar tissue.
Reperfusion: as quickly as possible, at least within 4 hours. Most guidelines state reperfusion therapy should be done within four hours. Within 30 minutes would be ideal, since irreversible injury can occur that quickly.

Scar tissue is thickened and tougher, so electrical impulses have a harder time traveling through it. That is why ECGs after an MI can show permanent changes.

Zones of Damage

ZoneWhat's HappeningCan It Recover?
InfarctionNecrosis; dead cells, where the MI happened.No. The goal is to stop it from increasing.
InjuryNot dead yet, but the tissue has suffered injury.Some recovery is still possible; it can be perfused and restored to be viable.
IschemiaThe outer zone.Full recovery is possible if it is perfused again.

The goal is to not extend the size of the infarct: increase oxygen and decrease the demand on the heart. The instructor compares this with an ischemic stroke: the same concept of tissue dying from lack of oxygen, where time is of the essence.

Three Factors That Decide the Extent of Damage

  • The location or level of the occlusion in the coronary artery.
  • The length of time it has been occluded.
  • The availability of collateral circulation. In people with atherosclerotic plaque the heart can start to develop collateral circulation. The lecture calls it "typically a bad thing," but here it creates ways to bypass the blockage and still get some oxygen and blood to the tissue, which can help limit the damage.

STEMI vs NSTEMI

The main difference is the ST segment on the ECG.

FeatureSTEMINSTEMI
ST segmentElevated.Normal, or a slight ST depression.
QRS complexUsually a wide QRS that develops over hours.Normal.
T wavePeaked, then over time less peaked, then inverted.Inverted the entire time.
TroponinElevated; still elevated after 2 hours, with a sharper increase than in NSTEMI.Elevated.
Infarct sizeTypically larger; more blockage, more tissue dying.Smaller.
OutcomesWorse.Better than STEMI.
The "tombstone." Nurses describe the ST elevation of a STEMI as looking like a tombstone, because the prognosis is not good if it isn't treated very quickly. Seeing it on an ECG should throw up red flags: investigate, and do it quickly.

On the ECG images, an NSTEMI can show ST depression or T wave inversion; a STEMI shows ST elevation.

The LAD: The "Widow-Maker"

Coronary blood supply named in the lecture

  • The right coronary artery.
  • The left main artery, which branches into the left circumflex artery and the left anterior descending (LAD) artery.
  • The LAD supplies almost the entire front side of the heart, much of the sidewall, and most of the left ventricle.
The LAD is the vessel most commonly involved in MI, and one of the most damaging. It is a really large vessel feeding most of the left ventricle, so a blockage there is particularly detrimental and often very lethal if not treated very quickly. An LAD MI is called a widow-maker heart attack because of its very poor prognosis if no one acts quickly.

Part 3 — Acute MI Pharmacology

Topic 3, the second part of the same ACS PowerPoint: the initial drugs, fibrinolytic therapy with alteplase, nitroglycerin safety, reperfusion injury, and the key takeaways. Diagnostics and surgical reperfusion are covered in med-surg, not here.

Initial Drug Treatment

The lecture goes through these drugs twice, once briefly and once as a recap with more detail. Both passes are combined here.

DrugWhy It's GivenWatch For and Notes
OxygenIncreases oxygen delivery to the ischemic myocardium.New research shows that if the patient is not hypoxic, oxygen may not have that benefit. Whether it is ordered depends on the provider.
MorphineDecreases pain. Some research suggests it dilates blood vessels to help blood flow, and there is some thought that it helps preserve ischemic tissue.Side effects: respiratory depression and hypotension, so check vital signs and make sure the patient can tolerate it. Newer data say the benefits may not be what we once thought, and it may inhibit antiplatelet medications and worsen outcomes. Which Drugs Affect Mortality

DrugMortality
AspirinDecreases mortality in STEMI.
Beta blockersCan help with mortality.
NitroglycerinDoes not reduce mortality (it limits infarct size).

Fibrinolytic Therapy: Alteplase (tPA)

When it's used

  • Often used in a STEMI.
  • PCI (percutaneous coronary intervention) is the first choice for treating a STEMI. If a cath lab is not available, fibrinolytic therapy is effective.
  • These are the "clot busters."

Alteplase (tPA)

  • The prototype and the drug of choice; it is the most effective.
  • Dissolves the clot by converting plasminogen into plasmin; plasmin is what actually dissolves the clot.
  • Works best when given within 30 to 70 minutes of the onset of symptoms (when the chest pain started).

Drugs Given Alongside

  • In a STEMI where fibrinolytic therapy is anticipated, clopidogrel is also given at presentation, and heparin might be started.
  • Anyone who has had fibrinolytic therapy is given heparin and antiplatelet therapy (like clopidogrel) to help prevent new clots from forming. Alteplase dissolves the current clot; the others keep new ones from forming.
The main adverse effect is bleeding. We are dissolving a clot, so watch for bleeding.

The lecture gives two time windows: thrombolytics "ideally" within about 4 to 6 hours of chest pain starting, and alteplase working best within 30 to 70 minutes of symptom onset. Both are recorded here exactly as stated.

Nitroglycerin Safety

ConcernWhat the Lecture Says
Sildenafil (Viagra)Do not give nitroglycerin with it. Both cause vasodilation of the veins and can reduce preload, leading to really profound hypotension.
Other nitratesSevere hypotension, especially when taken with other nitrates.
Common effectsHeadache and flushing.

Reperfusion Injury

What the lecture says

  • When myocardial tissue that has been deprived of oxygen suddenly receives oxygenated blood, it can become stunned, and that can cause heart failure.
  • Why: white blood cells come to the infarcted, damaged tissue (that's their job), and they release oxidized free radicals that cause the reperfusion injury.
  • These patients need to be monitored closely.
  • Also monitor for reperfusion dysrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia (VTach) and ventricular fibrillation.

Key Takeaways

Acute MI by the numbers

  • Acute MI is a leading cause of death in the United States and throughout the world.
  • A big reason for that mortality is hesitating or delaying care ("it might pass," "it's not serious").
  • With treatment, survival is great: about 90 to 95% for treated patients in the US. Teach patients: if you suspect an MI, seek treatment. Better safe than sorry.
  • About half of people with MI are younger than 65.
  • People older than 65 and people with diabetes can have an MI and not know it: a silent or asymptomatic MI, found later, with no signs or symptoms at the time.

Part 4 — Aneurysms & Aortic Dissection

Topic 4: what an aneurysm is and why it forms, the types by location and shape, assessing a AAA, and the two big complications, rupture and aortic dissection.

What an Aneurysm Is

Definition and cause

  • A localized dilation or outpouching of a vessel wall.
  • A true aneurysm involves all three layers of the arterial wall. There are also false aneurysms (below).
  • Aneurysms are primarily a result of hypertension. Prolonged, uncontrolled high blood pressure puts constant pressure on the arterial wall: the vessel dilates, the wall weakens, a pouch or bulge forms, and eventually the pouch can burst.
  • Aneurysms cause turbulent blood flow, which makes them susceptible to rupture.
  • Most commonly seen in the aorta and the cerebral arteries, but they can occur elsewhere.
Listen for a bruit. Because of the turbulent flow, a stethoscope over an aneurysm may pick up a bruit: a whooshing-type sound.

Risk Factors

Think of anything that damages blood vessels. The primary drivers are atherosclerosis and hypertension.

Named in the lecture

  • Atherosclerosis
  • Hypertension
  • Diseases of the blood vessels
  • Trauma
  • Tobacco use
  • Older adult age (over 65)
  • A hereditary component

Types of Aneurysms

By location

  • Aortic aneurysms, the most common type: abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA, "triple A"), thoracic aortic aneurysm, and dissecting aortic aneurysm.
  • Cerebral aneurysms: anywhere in the brain.
  • False or pseudoaneurysms.

By shape

  • Saccular
  • Berry (a subtype of saccular)
  • Fusiform

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

AssessmentWhat You May Find
AuscultationListen with the stethoscope over the abdominal aorta. A bruit there suggests a AAA may be present.

The aorta is the largest vessel in the body and supplies a lot of really important places, so the clinical manifestations of an aortic aneurysm depend on where it is located. An aneurysm above the kidneys looks different from one below the kidneys, where the kidneys are getting normal blood flow.

Complications: Rupture & Aortic Dissection

Rupture

  • Especially in the aorta, where the pressure in the vessel is very high.
  • If a AAA ruptures, the patient will bleed out very, very quickly, with a lot of blood involved.

Aortic dissection (dissecting aneurysm)

  • A tear in the inner layer of the artery; blood enters between the layers of the artery wall.
  • The turbulent blood flow tears away at the layers, separating them.
  • A medical emergency. These patients can also rupture, and without quick intervention it can be fatal.
  • An aneurysm increases the risk for dissection: a weakened wall can keep weakening, eventually tear, and turn into a dissection.

Aneurysm vs Aortic Dissection

Aneurysm

  • Can be completely asymptomatic.

Aortic dissection

  • Sudden, severe tearing pain: the most common manifestation.
  • Most likely felt in the chest, but it can also be in the neck, jaw, back or abdomen.

Clinical Manifestations of Aortic Dissection

Saccular (one side, wide neck), berry (small neck, circle of Willis; drain, clip or coil), fusiform (whole circumference, monitored), false (inner tear, a hematoma after procedures). Suspected AAA: light palpation only. Aneurysms can be asymptomatic; dissection is sudden severe tearing pain, BP high early and unattainable later. Dissection is a surgical emergency; beta blockers slow further tearing.

At rest (sleeping or minimal activity).

C-reactive protein (CRP).

How soon after acute ischemia does the heart's ability to contract decrease?
Within 1 to 2 minutes.
When after an MI is the myocardium very weak and susceptible to rupture?
At 1 to 2 weeks.
What three factors decide the extent of damage in an MI?
Location of the occlusion, how long it has been occluded, and collateral circulation.
How does the T wave change over time in a STEMI?
Peaked, then less peaked, then inverted.
What is the prototype fibrinolytic drug?
Alteplase (tPA).
Why are heparin and clopidogrel given after fibrinolytic therapy?
To prevent new clots from forming.
What causes reperfusion injury?
Oxidized free radicals released by white blood cells responding to the damaged tissue.
What is the survival rate for treated MI patients in the US?
About 90 to 95 percent.
Which aneurysm type is often monitored until it reaches a certain size?
Fusiform.
What are the surgical options for a berry aneurysm?
Drain, clip or coil.
What happens to blood pressure in the early stages of aortic dissection?
It is elevated, as the body compensates for blood loss.
How is aortic dissection diagnosed?
CT scan or MRA (MRI with angiogram).
