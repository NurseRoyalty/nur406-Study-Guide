Week 1: Antimicrobials & HIV

Antibiotic principles, cell wall and intracellular antibiotics, antivirals, HIV and AIDS.

Mechanisms (how the classes are grouped): inhibit cell wall synthesis; increase cell wall permeability; inhibit protein synthesis (kills the cell, or only stops function/reproduction); inhibit DNA synthesis or alter DNA function; disrupt a specific metabolic or biochemical reaction.

Choosing an antimicrobial

  • Community-acquired (easier to treat, more often viral) vs hospital-acquired (more often bacterial, more often resistant).
  • Susceptibility patterns and the suspected organism (the number one way to limit resistance).
  • Site: can the drug penetrate it (bone, lung, kidney; some concentrate in urine, good for UTIs)?
  • Patient: allergies, age extremes (lower doses), renal and liver function.
  • Dose and length depend on host defenses, site (an abscess takes longer than a UTI), organism, local vs systemic (sepsis).
  • Give it early; some drugs need peak and trough levels (e.g. vancomycin).

Cultures before antimicrobials

  • Goal: get cultures before starting, since the drug can keep the organism from growing in the culture.
  • Exceptions: critically ill or severe sepsis, and meningitis (start a drug that crosses the blood-brain barrier).
  • A culture order is stat: delegate if you're busy.
  • Sputum, urine or blood. Blood: two sets, aerobic and anaerobic bottles, preferably a peripheral stick (lines and ports carry surface bacteria); clean the skin and bottles well, since nurses often contaminate cultures.
  • Disk diffusion: no growth around the disk = effective. MIC = lowest concentration that inhibits growth; MBC = lowest that kills 99.9%. Reports usually say only "susceptible" or "resistant".

Allergy vs adverse effect

  • Nausea and vomiting are usually adverse effects (intolerance), not allergy.
  • True allergy: rash, welts, angioedema, anaphylaxis.
  • Cross-reactivity: penicillin allergy may mean cephalosporin allergy, especially if anaphylactic.
  • Penicillin and sulfa allergies are common; allergies can develop at any age.

Resistance & hospital infections

  • Driven by over-prescription. MRSA (methicillin resistant), CRE (whole carbapenem class), MDRO (multiple classes).
  • Mechanisms: innate, shared genetic material, mutation from exposure, not taking the full course ("survival of the fittest").
  • Hospital risk: illness, surgery, invasive devices (CLABSI, CAUTI, G-tubes), skin breakdown. Post-op: atelectasis raises pneumonia risk; wound dehiscence or evisceration.
  • Upper respiratory infections are mostly viral: about a 1 in 4,000 chance an antibiotic helps vs 1 in 1,000 chance of harm.
  • Environment: C. diff spores survive 5+ months on surfaces; VRE contaminates cushions; gowns 37–65% contaminated after care; white coats 69%.
  • Treatment failure: wrong drug, started too late, dose too low, too short, can't reach the site (blood-brain barrier).

Antibiotics: Cell Wall Inhibitors

Penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems and vancomycin all inhibit or damage cell wall synthesis: the weakened wall lets extracellular fluid rush in, so the cell swells and bursts (lysis and death). Learn them as classes and put each generation on its own row of your drug matrix.

Beta-lactamase and its inhibitors: penicillins, cephalosporins and carbapenems share a beta-lactam ring. Some bacteria secrete beta-lactamase, an enzyme that breaks that ring so the antibiotic doesn't work. Beta-lactamase inhibitors (sulbactam, clavulanic acid, tazobactam, avibactam) are never given alone; they are paired with the antibiotic so it works against those bacteria. Examples: ampicillin-sulbactam, Augmentin (amoxicillin + clavulanic acid), Zosyn (piperacillin + tazobactam). You need to know why the combination is used, not each inhibitor.

The voiceover calls these "beta-lactam antibiotics" and calls the enzyme "beta-lactam". It means beta-lactamase inhibitors and beta-lactamase.

Penicillins

Inhibit transpeptidase, so the wall becomes fragile and the bacteria autolyze; bacteria must be growing and dividing. Low toxicity, good for outpatients. Uses: UTIs, STIs, pneumonia, peritonitis, sepsis, meningitis; mostly gram-positive (strep, enterococcus, staph) unless extended spectrum. AEs: urticaria, pruritus, rash, angioedema; severe reactions more likely IV. Ampicillin rash in 5–10% of kids. Allergic to one penicillin = allergic to all. Interactions: NSAIDs, oral contraceptives, warfarin: get a good drug history.

Carbapenems ("-penem")

  • Broadest spectrum of all antibiotics: saved as a last resort to avoid resistance.
  • Unique AE: seizures. Use caution with a seizure history, the elderly, and other seizure-inducing drugs. All are IV, infused over 60 minutes.
  • Imipenem-cilastatin: very broad, resists beta-lactamase, crosses the BBB. Cilastatin inhibits renal dihydropeptidase, which would break imipenem down too fast.
  • Meropenem: a bit less coverage, doesn't degrade in the kidney (no cilastatin needed), less seizure activity, so used more often; some GI distress.
  • CRE (carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae): a deadly public health emergency, killing about 50%; a major infection-prevention concern, with very few drug options left.

Vancomycin (glycopeptide)

  • Immediate cell wall inhibition (not by autolysis). Gram-positive, MDR staph (MRSA), penicillin-resistant organisms. Not for meningitis/CNS.
  • PO vancomycin treats C. diff and pseudomembranous colitis only (it stays in the gut).
  • Nephrotoxic (IV): monitor renal labs, especially with IV contrast or other nephrotoxic drugs. Also ototoxic at high levels (reversible), immune thrombocytopenia (monitor platelets), caution with neuromuscular blockade.
  • Red man syndrome: from infusing too fast: rapid flushing, itchy rash, possibly tachycardia and hypotension. Slow the infusion; premedicate with Benadryl next time; tell the pharmacist or provider.
Peak and trough: they keep the drug in its therapeutic range. Peak = highest level, drawn about 30 min after the IV infusion is finished (infusion done at 1:00 PM, so draw about 1:30). Trough = lowest level, drawn about 30 min before the next dose (dose due at 8:00 PM, so draw at 7:30), not 30 minutes after a dose.

Antivirals (Non-HIV)

Few antivirals exist: viruses replicate only inside host cells (no cell wall of their own), so a drug that kills the virus can kill healthy cells too. Most antivirals work only during the replication phase, which is often over by the time symptoms appear. They inhibit replication so the immune system can destroy the virus. Targets covered: HSV, herpes zoster, influenza A and B, RSV, hepatitis (in the hepatitis lecture), CMV.

HIV & AIDS

Definitions & epidemiology

  • HIV (human immunodeficiency virus): a retrovirus that destroys CD4 T cells. HIV-1 is the most prevalent and the one seen in the US; HIV-2 is less pathogenic and mostly confined to West Africa.
  • AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome) is caused by HIV: not everyone with HIV has AIDS, but everyone with AIDS has HIV.
  • About 1.1 million infected in the US. 76% of adults and adolescents with HIV are men. Black men who have sex with men are the highest-risk group; new cases are rising among women. Use "men who have sex with men" (less stigmatizing).

How HIV takes over

  • Binds cells with CD4 receptors (mainly T lymphocytes; also monocytes and macrophages), fuses and enters.
  • Reverse transcriptase makes a DNA copy of its RNA; integrase inserts it into the cell's DNA; protease helps assemble new virus, which buds out to infect more cells. These enzymes are the drug targets.
  • Infected for life. About 10 billion particles are made daily. Anti-HIV antibodies form but are ineffective: they are only a marker of infection.
  • Losing CD4 cells means losing cell-mediated (memory) immunity, then opportunistic infections and cancers.
StageWhat happens
1. Early (acute) infectionRapid replication; 10–60% notice no symptoms, or nonspecific flu-like ones. Infectious from day one. Seroconversion: antibodies become detectable, the person is highly infectious, and flu-like symptoms can last weeks.
2. Clinical latency (chronic)Viral levels stabilize; asymptomatic or mild. Untreated, 3–12 years; with treatment it can last for decades. Eventually CD4 drops and viral load rises: they move inversely.
3. Symptomatic HIV, then AIDSFever, sore throat, swollen lymph nodes, rash, muscle aches, night sweats, mouth ulcers, chills, fatigue. Testing is driven by risk factors, since the symptoms are nonspecific.
AIDS is diagnosed by either: (1) CD4 count below 200, or (2) any AIDS-defining condition regardless of labs. Ones to memorize: Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP), TB (Mycobacterium), Kaposi sarcoma, invasive cervical cancer, pervasive (invasive) candidiasis, wasting syndrome, HIV-associated (AIDS) dementia / encephalopathy.

Oral health & dementia

  • Oral manifestations (fungal, viral, bacterial, cancerous; oral hairy leukoplakia on the tongue; periodontal disease) appear as CD4 drops, a sign of progression toward AIDS. Teach oral care and dental visits; consider masks in public.
  • HIV-associated dementia (AIDS dementia complex): HIV crosses the blood-brain barrier; once 40–60% of patients, now much less with treatment. AIDS-defining.

Transmission

  • Blood and body fluids: anal or vaginal sex without a condom (high risk; oral sex low to none); perinatal (25–30% untreated vs under 2% treated); shared needles; transfusions or transplants (rare now).
  • Not from toilet seats, hugging, saliva or kissing.
  • Risk rises with frequency and duration of contact and with viral load (high early, often before diagnosis), plus host immune status and genetics.
  • Health care exposure: splashes are near zero risk; a needlestick is still under 1%. Always report it; PEP may be needed.

Antiretroviral therapy (ART)

Know the principles, not individual drug names. Typical regimen: two NRTIs plus a third agent from another class. This has dramatically reduced morbidity and mortality, keeping patients in clinical latency for decades.

Monitoring & goals

  • Start as soon as possible after diagnosis.
  • Goal: an undetectable viral load (effectively no transmission, but still HIV positive).
  • Monitor CD4 count (want it high; treated patients ideally about 500–600) and viral load (want it low). Low CD4 with high viral load is bad.

PrEP vs PEP

  • Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP): daily antiretroviral (a 2-in-1 pill) for someone in a stable relationship with an HIV-positive partner; cuts transmission by more than 90%; not given to just anyone (resistance).
  • Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP): ART for 28 days after an exposure, then HIV tests at 6 and 12 weeks. Also used after high-risk health care exposures.

Histamine

  • Stored in mast cells (skin, soft tissue) and basophils (blood).
  • Causes hives and itching, vasodilation (redness, low BP if systemic), bronchoconstriction, sleep–wake effects, ↑ gastric acid.
  • Responsible for most allergic reaction symptoms (drugs, foods, contact).
  • H1 receptors: smooth muscle contraction and capillary dilation (the target of antihistamines). H2: heart rate and gastric acid (H2 blockers for GERD).

Bronchospasm

  • Sudden constriction of bronchial muscles.
  • Triggers: asthma history, a URI, especially before anesthesia (procedures may be delayed); ventilated and PACU patients.
  • Sudden trouble breathing, dropping sats, wheezing.
  • Treat with an inhaled bronchodilator; an inhaled steroid and anticholinergic longer term.

Pneumonia

Pneumonia = any infection of the lower respiratory tract after the upper airway defenses (cilia, mucus, cough) fail. It can be viral, bacterial, fungal, protozoal or parasitic. Inflammation fills the alveoli with purulent material, fibrin and dead cells, so gas exchange fails. Spread by droplets. About 3 million cases and more than 48,600 deaths a year before COVID; more common in winter (flu) and in males.

Risk factors

  • Age extremes: children under 5, adults over 70–80.
  • Compromised immunity: long-term steroids, transplants, HIV.
  • Underlying lung disease (COPD, pulmonary hypertension); smoking impairs ciliary clearance.
  • Aspiration risk: alcoholism, altered LOC, impaired swallowing.
  • Nursing home residents, immobility, hospitalization, thoracic or abdominal surgery, anesthesia, intubation.
  • Influenza: the most common cause.

CAP vs HAP

  • CAP (community-acquired): a leading reason for hospitalization; usually easier to treat. HCAP (health care–associated: nursing homes, dialysis, chemo) falls under CAP.
  • HAP (hospital-acquired): develops 48+ hours after admission; worse outcomes, ICU-associated, more virulent and deadly. VAP (ventilator-associated) in intubated patients.
  • Aspiration of oropharyngeal secretions is the most common route; droplets are the other.

Patho: the pathogen reaches the lower airway, triggering an inflammatory reaction; lung vessels dilate; goblet cells secrete mucus that accumulates around the alveoli and pulmonary capillaries; alveoli can't open and close properly, so gas exchange worsens.

Presentation

  • Usually preceded by a URI. Fever, chills, cough, malaise, pleuritic pain; severe cases bring dyspnea and hemoptysis.
  • Bacterial (usually hospital-acquired): productive, purulent sputum: green, rusty, or "currant jelly".
  • Viral (usually community-acquired): nonproductive, scant cough.
  • Respiratory distress: still oxygenating, but through more work (accessory muscles, frequent breaks, ↑ HR, can't talk well). Respiratory failure: can no longer compensate, so expect arrest and CPR.

Diagnosis

Tuberculosis

Patho

  • Any infection by Mycobacterium; M. tuberculosis is most common (M. leprae = leprosy).
  • Aerobic rod-shaped bacillus that needs lots of oxygen, so it favors the lungs (can also affect bone and brain).
  • Granulomas (tubercles): walled-off clusters of macrophages and lymphocytes around cheesy caseous tissue breakdown.
  • Airborne droplets from coughing and sneezing (mainly human-to-human); spreads to the lungs through blood and lymph.
  • Slow-growing, which makes it hard to treat.

Latent vs active

  • Normal immunity usually contains it, with no disease.
  • Latent TB: bacilli dormant in granulomas, no signs or symptoms. It can reactivate when immunity drops: HIV, immunosuppressants (RA, autoimmune disease), poor nutrition, renal failure.
  • Active TB: gradual, often unnoticed until advanced: fatigue, weight loss, lethargy, anorexia, low-grade afternoon fever, slowly worsening productive cough over months, night sweats (most suggestive), anxiety. Later: dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis.
  • Extrapulmonary TB: neurologic signs or meningitis (brain), bone pain, urinary problems.

Screening: high-risk people get the interferon-gamma release assay (blood draw); the non-high-risk public (including students) gets the TB skin test. A positive test is not a diagnosis: next a chest X-ray (granulomas), then a sputum stain and culture. Contagious until the sputum stops growing mycobacteria.

Drug therapy principles: first-line drugs (the 5 below) are used unless resistance is suspected; second-line drugs are reserved for resistant cases and are less effective (names not needed). Start a broad 4-drug first-line regimen, then narrow it by susceptibility results. Always at least 2 drugs when possible (high-risk lifestyles can make multiple drugs hard to sustain).

Asthma triggers & the late response

  • Most common trigger: exercise (people with asthma can still exercise, with awareness). Also secondhand smoke, climate, dust mites, pet dander, pollen.
  • Early response: mediators cause vasodilation, capillary permeability, edema, bronchoconstriction and mucus.
  • Late response: recruited eosinophils, neutrophils and lymphocytes release mediators again hours later. Teach: after an attack, keep meds close and limit the trigger.
  • Patients often underestimate their severity: education matters.

Status asthmaticus

  • An unrelenting attack: silent chest (no air movement); blood CO₂ above 70.
  • A life-threatening emergency; often needs IV epinephrine.
  • Mainstays for acute attacks: bronchodilators, corticosteroids, oxygen.

Anemia

Anemia = too few RBCs, or poor quality or quantity of hemoglobin, from impaired production, blood loss, RBC destruction, or a combination. Whatever the cause, less oxygen reaches the tissues. Hemoglobin (heme with iron at its center) reversibly binds O₂ and CO₂. The bus analogy: RBC = bus, hemoglobin = seats, iron = what the seats are made of, oxygen = passengers.

  • Hemoglobin is the best indicator of RBCs.
  • Hematocrit is much more affected by fluid volume.

Renal: Pyelonephritis, Obstruction & Kidney Stones

Review your A&P and the NUR 325 lower UTI content: per the lecture, it is testable.

Pyelonephritis (upper UTI / kidney infection)

  • Inflammation of one or both kidneys; usually ascending from a lower UTI (or from a bloodstream infection). E. coli is most common.
  • Kidneys swell with infectious exudate; if severe, the renal artery is compressed: necrosis, abscess. Chronic or recurrent infection can cause CKD and sepsis.
  • Risk: pregnancy, recurrent lower UTIs, antibiotic-resistant strains.
  • Sudden onset: looks systemically sick, with fever and chills, CVA (costovertebral angle) tenderness, plus lower UTI symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency, hematuria), N/V, anorexia.
  • Treatment: antibiotics (community-acquired: Bactrim, ciprofloxacin, or nitrofurantoin), short course, or 7–14 days if recurrent; IV inpatient if severe. Urinalysis and culture for sensitivity; start broad, then narrow.
  • Complication: urosepsis (high mortality), especially in the elderly. Pregnancy complicates drug choice (usually not Bactrim or cipro).

Urinary obstruction

  • Anywhere in the tract: renal pelvis (mostly stones), ureter (stones, pregnancy, tumors), bladder or urethra (bladder cancer, neurogenic bladder, prostate, stricture).
  • Complications: urinary stasis leading to infection (recurrent UTIs or pyelonephritis); back-up pressure causing hydroureter / hydronephrosis and post-renal AKI. Fix the blockage (often surgery; may be an emergency).
  • Presentation depends on site, size, internal vs external cause, and speed of onset (the main factor in severity): a stone in the roomy renal pelvis may not hurt; once it enters the narrow ureter, pain is severe and comes in waves. A slowly enlarging prostate is better tolerated.

Nephrolithiasis (renal calculi / kidney stones)

The most common cause of urinary obstruction. Crystals form in the nephron (kidney level) when urine is supersaturated with a solute; size ranges from tiny to golf ball, smooth or jagged. Promoters: pH changes (frequent UTIs), high urinary salt concentration, dehydration, bone disease, gout, renal disease, urinary stasis (immobility). Risk: men slightly more, 20s–30s, Caucasians, family history, congenital urinary defects, hot weather (concentrated urine), obesity, sedentary life.

Renal: Urologic Cancers & Glomerular Disease

Kidney cancer (renal cell carcinoma)

  • 85% of kidney cancers; kidney cancer is under 5% of all cancers.
  • Risk: smoking (#1), obesity, older age, men, genetics.
  • No early signs: usually found after metastasis (sometimes by chance on a CT), so 5-year survival is about 10% once metastasized.
  • Late signs: CVA tenderness, cola-colored hematuria, palpable abdominal mass. Metastasizes to bone and lung (dyspnea, cough, bone pain).
  • Usually chemo-resistant. Not metastasized: remove the kidney (the other compensates). Metastasized: chemo and radiation.

Bladder cancer (urothelial carcinoma)

  • The 4th most common cancer in men.
  • Risk: smoking (#1), male, occupational toxins (rubber, paint), low fluid intake (toxins sit concentrated in the bladder; more fluid flushes them).
  • Early: pink or red hematuria (may be microscopic, found on UA). Later: UTI-like frequency, urgency, dysuria.
  • Stage 1: intravesical therapy; advanced: systemic chemo.
BCG (intravesical, for early bladder cancer): a live vaccine instilled into the bladder (empty the bladder, in-and-out catheter) that triggers a local inflammatory response so the immune system attacks cancer cells. Weekly for 6–12 weeks. Keep it in for 2 hours, changing position every 15 min so it coats the whole bladder. AEs: bladder irritation and UTI-type symptoms; systemic infection if immunocompromised (avoid in HIV/AIDS). Disinfect (bleach) the urine for 6 hours after treatment.

Glomerular disease

The glomerulus: arterioles in Bowman's capsule; the afferent arteriole brings blood in, the efferent takes it away; the pressure gradient pushes wastes into the urine. Filtration takes a lot of oxygen (the kidneys get about 20% of cardiac output) and needs adequate BP. The capillary membrane has 3 layers: endothelium, glomerular basement membrane (GBM) (where most damage occurs), and podocytes (epithelial cells that start urine formation).

Acute Kidney Injury & Chronic Kidney Disease

What the kidneys do

  • Maintain fluid and electrolyte homeostasis (BP, third spacing).
  • Excrete water-soluble wastes and drugs in urine.
  • Endocrine: make erythropoietin (RBCs), activate vitamin D (calcium, bone), make renin (RAAS, BP).
  • Very "needy": about 1 L/min of blood (about 20% of cardiac output) for 0.5% of body mass, so injury can happen within hours of poor perfusion; often reversible.
  • GFR is the indicator of kidney function: normal above 90; below 60 suggests a problem; about 25% of normal (about 25–30) = renal insufficiency. Prolonged insufficiency progresses to chronic disease.

AKI classifications

  • Pre-renal (most common): volume loss before the kidney: blood loss, dehydration, third spacing (sepsis).
  • Intrarenal: direct kidney damage: acute tubular necrosis (ATN) from toxins, overdose or nephrotoxic drugs; acute glomerulonephritis; vascular disease (diabetes, HTN).
  • Post-renal (least common): obstruction (stone, tumor, blockage).
  • The injury also triggers an inflammatory response that can cause more cell death.

The voiceover says insufficiency is "25 to 30 mL per hour" when describing GFR (GFR is per minute), and first says oliguria is "less than 400 mL an hour" before correcting to 400 mL in 24 hours.

AKI presentation

  • Oliguria: under 400 mL/24 h (under 200 mL a shift, about 30 mL/h), beginning about 1 day after a hypotensive event and lasting 1–3 weeks.
  • Severe: fluid volume excess, metabolic acidosis, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, azotemia / uremia (wastes build up), neurologic changes.
  • Treatment: fix the cause (replace volume; antidote or flush for toxins), treat electrolytes, avoid nephrotoxic drugs, monitor labs.

CKD causes & staging

  • Staged by GFR: stages 1–2 usually asymptomatic; moderate brings HTN; severe brings the other manifestations; stage 5 = uremic ("urine in the blood").
  • ESKD causes: diabetes about 50%, hypertension about 30%, other about 20% (glomerulonephritis, AKI). Best treatment is prevention: control glucose and BP.
  • Risk: family history, CAD, hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis, over 60, men, Black Americans, smoking, obesity.
  • Cycle of worsening: proteinuria (leaky GBM; proteins injure tubules and drive inflammation) and ↑ angiotensin II (vasoconstriction, HTN, hyperfiltration, more proteinuria). Watch BP and urine.

Week 4: Liver, Gallbladder & Pancreas

Jaundice, hepatitis, cirrhosis and liver failure, gallbladder disease, pancreatitis.

Liver Function, Jaundice & Viral Hepatitis

The liver

  • Largest organ (about 3 lb), right upper (epigastric) area, 5–7% of cardiac output. Lobules of hepatocytes around a central vein; hepatocytes can regenerate.
  • Kupffer cells line the sinusoids and remove bacteria and toxins.
  • Functions: metabolizes and stores fat, carbohydrate, protein, vitamins and minerals; blood volume reservoir; blood filter (removes bilirubin); makes clotting factors (prothrombin, fibrinogen); drug metabolism and detoxification. Liver failure loses all of these.
  • Portal circulation: the portal vein brings nutrient-rich deoxygenated blood from the stomach, intestines, spleen and pancreas, which is the basis of the first-pass effect (oral doses are higher than IV).

Liver labs in liver disease

  • LFTs = liver enzymes AST, ALT, alk phos: rise (often not concerning until over about 150), but they're not a good measure of severity.
  • Bilirubin (total, direct, indirect): rises.
  • Ammonia: rises (the liver breaks it down).
  • Total protein and albumin: fall (the liver makes them).
  • PT: prolonged (no prothrombin), so bleeding risk.

Jaundice (icterus)

Yellowing from ↑ bilirubin (a heme breakdown product), usually visible at about 2–2.5 (normal about 0.6–1). Look at the sclera (best in darker skin), palms, soles and mucous membranes. Also dark urine, ↑ liver enzymes, normal or clay-colored stools (bile salts give stool its brown color), and pruritus (bilirubin build-up; palms, soles or generalized).

Cirrhosis, Acute Liver Failure & Liver Pharmacology

Hepatitis (inflammation) can be reversible; cirrhosis is irreversible inflammatory fibrotic scarring (death rate about 26%). Leukocytes release mediators that drive fibrosis; chaotic fibrosis blocks bile channels and blood flow (jaundice, portal hypertension); hypoxia disrupts regeneration, so hepatocytes regrow in a disorganized way, form scar, then necrosis, atrophy and failure. It develops over years; the rate depends on the cause and toxin amount. Removing the toxin (stopping drinking) slows or stops progression but can't undo it.

Causes

  • Hepatitis B and C.
  • Excessive alcohol: alcoholic cirrhosis is the most common single type (still only about 25%).
  • NAFLD / NASH (nonalcoholic fatty liver / steatohepatitis).
  • Autoimmune, hereditary metabolic, idiopathic.

Alcohol & the liver

  • Alcohol becomes acetaldehyde, which alters hepatocytes and activates stellate cells (fibrosis); blocks protein export, causing vitamin and mineral problems and malnutrition. Kupffer cells recruit neutrophils; gut bacteria translocate; immunity is suppressed.
  • Alcoholic fatty liver: mild, asymptomatic, reversible if drinking stops.
  • Alcoholic steatohepatitis: fat, inflammation and hepatocyte degeneration; irreversible fibrosis begins; anorexia, nausea, jaundice, edema.
  • Cirrhosis: necrosis, fibrosis, scarring.

Gallbladder: Cholelithiasis & Cholecystitis

Cholelithiasis (gallstones)

  • Impaired metabolism of cholesterol, bilirubin and bile acids: crystals saturate and aggregate in the gallbladder.
  • Cholesterol stones about 70%; pigmented (black, hard) and mixed are less common.
  • Risk: developed countries, obesity, female, around 40, oral contraceptives, rapid weight loss, Native Americans (60–70%) vs 10–15% of white Americans, genetics, pre-existing pancreatic or liver disease. The old mnemonic: "fair, fat and forty."
  • Stones sitting in the gallbladder are asymptomatic. Pain comes when a stone lodges in the cystic or common duct during a contraction.
  • Biliary colic: severe epigastric / RUQ pain radiating to the back, about 30 min to a few hours after a fatty meal; intermittent or steady. Also fatty-food intolerance (and cabbage), heartburn, flatulence.

Obstructed bile flow (stone in the bile duct)

  • Obstructive jaundice (bile can't reach the duodenum).
  • Dark amber urine that foams when shaken (water-soluble conjugated bilirubin).
  • Clay-colored stools (bilirubin can't reach the gut to become urobilinogen).
  • Steatorrhea (no bile salts, so no fat digestion); fatty-food intolerance.
  • Pruritus (bile salts deposit in the skin).
  • Bleeding tendency (no bile, no vitamin K absorption, fewer clotting factors).
Cholecystitis (inflamed gallbladder): usually a stone lodged in the cystic duct; acute or chronic; the gallbladder becomes edematous, hyperemic, distended, and loses function over time. Pain is like biliary colic, but pressure on the wall cuts blood flow, risking ischemia, necrosis, perforation (taken seriously in the ED). Inflammatory signs point to cholecystitis rather than stones alone: fever, leukocytosis, rebound tenderness, guarding; ↑ bilirubin and ↑ alk phos. Chronic: scarring and degeneration. Treatment: remove the gallbladder (cholecystectomy).

Pancreatitis

Diabetes is the endocrine pancreas; pancreatitis is exocrine dysfunction.

Acute pancreatitis

  • Most cases are mild and resolve on their own; about 20% become severe and need hospitalization.
  • Causes: obstruction of enzyme outflow, usually a gallstone in the common bile duct (shared with the pancreas); also alcohol, some drugs, viral infections.
  • Trapped enzymes autodigest the pancreas: vascular damage, coagulopathy, necrosis, fat necrosis.

Trapped enzymes and their damage

  • Trypsin: edema, necrosis, hemorrhage.
  • Elastase: hemorrhage.
  • Phospholipase A: fat necrosis.
  • Kallikrein: edema, ↑ vascular permeability (fluid leaks into the abdomen), smooth muscle contraction (pain), shock.
  • Lipase: fat necrosis (breaks down pancreatic cells). Amylase and lipase are the enzymes most often discussed.

Presentation & labs

  • Hallmark: sudden, severe epigastric / mid-abdominal pain, often radiating to the back; tenderness and guarding (from edema distending the ducts and capsule, plus peritoneal irritation).
  • N/V, abdominal distention, fever, hypoactive bowel sounds; severe cases: hypotension and tachycardia (fluid loss); jaundice if the bile duct is blocked.
  • Labs: ↑ pancreatic enzymes (amylase, lipase), glucose up or down (monitor closely), ↑ WBC.
  • Hemorrhagic pancreatitis (severe, rare): Grey Turner sign = flank bruising; Cullen sign = bruising around the umbilicus (the lecturer's memory trick: vampires don't have belly buttons, think of the Cullens from Twilight).

Complications

  • Pulmonary: shallow breathing from pain, pleural effusion, diaphragm irritation.
  • Hypovolemic shock.
  • Hypocalcemia leading to tetany: fat necrosis makes free fatty acids that bind calcium.
  • Pseudocyst: a fluid sac outside the pancreas (necrotic products and secretions) causing more inflammation and scarring; may be a palpable epigastric mass; rupture causes peritonitis.
  • Abscess: fluid cavity inside the pancreas from extensive necrosis; infection and perforation; very high fever, leukocytosis, mass.

Chronic pancreatitis

  • Progressive fibrotic disease. Most common cause: alcohol (acinar cells make a toxic metabolite and cytokines, destroying the pancreas including the islets of Langerhans). Also genetics, chronic gallstone obstruction, smoking, autoimmune, tumor.
  • Repeated acute attacks plus ongoing dysfunction; constant, hard-to-treat pain (a chronic pain condition).
  • Weight loss and fat malabsorption (no enzymes); diabetes (islet loss); pseudocysts.

Treatment

  • Pain control first: opioids (hydromorphone, morphine).
  • Dicyclomine (anticholinergic antispasmodic): ↓ secretions, relaxes ductal smooth muscle.
  • Antacids and H2 blockers: ↓ hydrochloric acid, which ↓ pancreatic enzyme secretion.
  • Insulin or glucagon as blood sugar requires.
  • Pancrelipase (chronic): replaces the missing pancreatic enzymes; essentially no adverse effects; take with every meal and snack (often 3–4 capsules).

Week 5: GI & Endocrine

Upper and lower GI disorders, GI drugs, adrenal, pituitary and thyroid disorders.

Upper GI Disorders

Upper GI = esophagus, stomach and the start of the small intestine; lower GI = the rest of the small intestine, colon, rectum and anus. Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing, solids first, then liquids): mechanical causes (stenosis, strictures, diverticula, tumors) or neuromuscular causes (stroke, achalasia, where the LES can't open; common after intubation or tracheostomy). LES = lower esophageal sphincter.

GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease)

  • The LES doesn't close properly, so acidic stomach contents flow back into the esophagus.
  • Causes: weak LES closure or ↑ abdominal pressure: fatty, spicy, tomato-based, citrus foods, caffeine, heavy alcohol, smoking, sleep position, obesity, pregnancy, drugs.
  • Signs: heartburn (pyrosis), dyspepsia, regurgitation, chest pain, dysphagia, pulmonary symptoms; tooth decay, gingivitis, ear problems, hoarseness, chronic sore throat, throat clearing, laryngitis.
  • Complications: esophageal ulcers, scarring, strictures, and Barrett's esophagus: premalignant metaplasia with 3× the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma (very low survival).
  • Treat the GERD (avoid triggers, drugs) to prevent complications.

Hiatal hernia

  • The diaphragm opening for the esophagus is too large, so part of the stomach moves into the thorax.
  • Sliding (less severe; peritoneum intact). Paraesophageal / rolling (part of the stomach stays above the diaphragm permanently; thin peritoneum forms a true sac). Types III and IV are mixed or complex.
  • Risk: older age, diaphragm injury, repeated abdominal pressure (severe coughing, vomiting, constipation and straining), obesity, smoking.
  • Often no symptoms; otherwise belching, dysphagia, chest or epigastric pain: looks like GERD (they often coexist).
  • Conservative care: small frequent meals, don't lie down after eating, avoid tight clothing or abdominal binders, weight control, GERD drugs; surgery if needed.

Lower GI Disorders

Appendicitis

  • An obstructed appendix becomes inflamed. The danger is the complications: rupture, gangrene, abscess, peritonitis.
  • Peak age 10–12. Dull, steady pain that worsens over 4–6 hours, periumbilical, then RLQ; fever, nausea, anorexia.
  • Rebound tenderness in the RLQ (pain when pressure is released).
  • Sudden pain relief is a bad sign: possible rupture, so high peritonitis risk.
  • Diagnosis: signs, ultrasound, sometimes exploratory laparotomy.

Peritonitis

  • Inflamed peritoneum (the serous membrane lining the abdomen), usually after something ruptures or perforates: gastric or duodenal ulcer, gallbladder, pancreatitis, spleen, bladder, appendix.
  • Causes third spacing, leading to hypovolemic shock and sepsis; ↓ peristalsis leads to paralytic ileus and obstruction.
  • Sudden, severe pain (can't move, talk or breathe easily), tenderness, a rigid, board-like abdomen (not the same as distended), N/V, fever, ↑ WBC, ↑ HR, ↓ BP (pain and fluid shifts).
  • Treatment: anti-inflammatories; fix the cause.

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS): chronic altered bowel pattern from changes in motility: IBS-C (constipation) or IBS-D (diarrhea). Endocrine: Addison's Disease & Adrenal Crises

Addison's disease = adrenal cortex disease with hyposecretion of all three cortical hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, androgens); the most severe effects come from the lack of cortisol. Cause: idiopathic, autoimmune (most common), or a damaged or destroyed gland. In primary insufficiency the adrenal stops secreting cortisol, so there's no negative feedback: the anterior pituitary pours out ACTH and MSH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone), but the cortex can't respond. Signs appear only after about 90% of the cortex is destroyed, so it is usually advanced at diagnosis; onset is slow (anorexia, weakness, malaise, electrolyte problems). (President Kennedy's tan was from Addison's.)

Low aldosterone

  • Loses sodium and water, keeps potassium: hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, dehydration.
  • Low BP: ↓ vascular tone, cardiac output and circulating volume.
  • Salt craving.
  • The lecture adds that low aldosterone signals more ADH, which could lead to fluid volume excess.

Low cortisol

  • Low energy, hypoglycemia, weakness, fatigue.
  • High ACTH.
  • Hyperpigmentation ("tan") from MSH stimulating melanocytes.

Replacement therapy teaching (review the NUR 325 steroid drug matrix)
  • Lifelong, regardless of cause. Correct dosing should make the patient look like a normal, functioning person; over- or underdosing causes problems.
  • Mimic the normal pattern: some prescribers give the whole dose at bedtime; others give ⅔ in the morning and ⅓ in the afternoon. Be consistent every day; use the smallest effective dose (much smaller than for non-endocrine uses).
  • Never stop abruptly (and never run out): this can cause Addisonian crisis.
  • Increase the dose during any stress (infection, surgery, trauma): failing to can be fatal. The 3 × 3 rule: about 3 times the usual dose for 3 days, with the provider.
  • Always carry an injectable plus an oral supply; wear medical ID.

Severe Cushing syndrome (emergency)

  • Massively high random serum cortisol, or 24-h urine free cortisol more than 4× the upper limit; severe hypokalemia.
  • Linked to sepsis, opportunistic infection, uncontrolled HTN, heart failure; can be deadly.
  • Treat the metabolic and electrolyte problems; find the cause.

Addisonian crisis (acute adrenal insufficiency)

  • Insufficient serum corticosteroids: sudden loss of the adrenal glands, sudden stress in chronic disease, or sudden stopping of corticosteroids.
  • The body "crashes": a medical emergency (more in the practice-readiness course).
  • Prevented by adequate dosing and teaching.

Endocrine: Pheochromocytoma, SIADH & Diabetes Insipidus

Pheochromocytoma

  • A rare tumor of the adrenal medulla overproducing epinephrine and norepinephrine; 90% benign; young and middle-aged adults.
  • Epinephrine stimulates alpha and beta receptors; norepinephrine stimulates almost entirely alpha. Alpha activation is the main cause of the hypertension.
  • Hallmark: hypertension (intermittent or persistent, can be extreme) with the classic triad: headache, diaphoresis, tachycardia.
  • Episodes are triggered by anything that stimulates the SNS: stress, excitement, exercise, smoking. Keep a low-stimulus, relaxed environment.
  • Danger: stroke and cardiac events. Treatment: surgical removal; alpha blockers before surgery (tumor handling can release a massive catecholamine surge) or long term if inoperable.

Phenoxybenzamine

  • An old alpha blocker, used now only for pheochromocytoma.
  • Noncompetitive, irreversible alpha blockade: long-lasting BP lowering (wanted here).
  • Given 10–14 days before surgery and briefly after.
  • AEs: orthostatic hypotension, reflex tachycardia, nasal congestion, sexual side effects in men.

ADH (antidiuretic hormone), from the posterior pituitary, is released for high serum osmolality or hypotension; it makes the kidneys retain water. "ADH stops the pee."

Thyroid & Parathyroid Disorders

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland in the neck, the body's metabolism "volume knob". It makes T4 (thyroxine), which is mostly inactive and bound and acts as the regulator, and T3, the active form converted from T4 in the body. Iodine is needed to make both. Feedback loop: hypothalamus TRH → anterior pituitary TSH → thyroid makes T4 → converted to T3 → enough T3 tells the hypothalamus to turn down. Measuring TSH, T3 and T4 shows whether the problem is above the gland or at the gland. Primary (gland-level) disorders are most common and much more common in women.

Goiter = thyroid enlargement, with or without dysfunction ("nontoxic" if no symptoms). Causes: excess pituitary TSH; low iodine (low hormone, so TSH climbs and the gland enlarges); goitrogens (foods or drugs, e.g. lithium).

Coronary Artery Disease & Stable Angina

Every heart problem is electrical (conduction), plumbing (blocked or spasming arteries, valves), or pump (dysfunctional muscle). CAD = atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries (which branch off the aorta to feed the heart): plaque narrows the lumen and heart cells are starved of oxygen. The LAD ("widow maker") feeds the left ventricle, the powerhouse. 1 in 7 US deaths. CAD raises MI risk, but not everyone with CAD has had an MI. "Heart disease" is vague: dig for what the patient actually has.

Non-modifiable risk

  • Advanced age.
  • Family history (genes and shared environment); gene polymorphisms.
  • Males earlier; women's risk equals men's after menopause (estrogen protects).
  • Black, Hispanic and Indigenous Americans more than white or Asian/Pacific Islander; a death disparity for Black Americans (structural racism, access).

Modifiable risk

  • Hypertension (2–3× risk).
  • Smoking and secondhand smoke: damage the endothelium; nicotine raises catecholamines, lipids and BP.
  • Diabetes (insulin resistance damages the endothelium, adds inflammation).
  • Obesity (abdominal / android) and inactivity: inflammation, adipokines.
  • Diet (excess fat, trans fat, carbs; DASH diet protects).
  • Hyperlipidemia (high TG and LDL, low HDL): a key drug target (statins).
  • Depression, stress, anxiety (inflammation).

Endothelial dysfunction: diseased coronary arteries constrict when they should dilate (driven by diabetes, hyperlipidemia, HTN, smoking). Combined with plaque, the heart gets less blood exactly when it needs more.

Angina

  • The main symptom of CAD (some patients are asymptomatic). Chest pain plus heartburn, palpitations, weakness, dizziness, anxiety, nausea, cold sweats, burning in the chest, shoulder or abdomen.
  • Stable angina (the focus here): a supply–demand imbalance through a partially open artery. Brought on by exertion (shoveling snow, stairs, a big meal) and relieved by rest; lasts 2–5 minutes; can be severe; often mistaken for indigestion.
  • Complete occlusion = MI.

Cardiac vs non-cardiac chest pain

  • Always rule out the heart first for pain in the chest, left arm, jaw or left shoulder, diaphoresis, pallor.
  • Cardiac: diffuse pressure or tightness ("an elephant on my chest"), hard to pinpoint.
  • Non-cardiac: focal, well localized.
  • Women often have hot or burning discomfort or tenderness, not always in the chest, plus indigestion, heartburn, nausea, fatigue.
Chest pain suggesting MI (not stable angina): occurs at rest (at a desk, waking from sleep), may radiate, is not relieved by rest, with N/V, shortness of breath, diaphoresis. Teach: if you sit down and the pain isn't better in about 5 minutes, call 911. Stable angina care: rest and relaxation (↓ O₂ demand), nitrates, and treating atherosclerosis (lipids, smoking, BP). Patients with stable angina are at high risk of unstable angina and MI.

Heart failure is a pump problem: a chronic, progressive condition in which the heart can't pump enough blood to meet the body's needs. The most common cause of hospitalization (dyspnea or volume overload), especially over 65. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume (HR helps from about 60 to 120, then falls off). Stroke volume depends on preload (volume in the LV before it squeezes: "preload = fluid"), afterload (pressure the heart pumps against: "afterload = BP"), and contractility (the pump). In HF, contractility falls while preload and afterload rise (vessels tighten), so CO falls; there is volume overload, impaired filling (diastole) and weak ejection (systole).

  • COPD (right ventricular failure).

  • From high end-diastolic volume (fluid left in the ventricle) and ↑ pressure.
  • Abnormal over age 40: indicates heart failure.
  • Best prevention: control HTN, glucose, lipids, obesity.

Conduction & Dysrhythmias

Cardiac cell properties

  • Automaticity: generates an impulse.
  • Excitability: responds to an outside impulse.
  • Conductivity: receives and passes an impulse on.
  • Contractility: shortens ("squeezes") in response.

The EKG

  • Depolarization ("light switch on") is contraction (systole); repolarization is recharging. Driven by the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump.
  • P wave = atrial depolarization (from the SA node). QRS = ventricular depolarization (AV node, then bundle of His and Purkinje fibers); atrial repolarization is hidden in the QRS. T wave = ventricular repolarization.
  • Flat baseline = isoelectric line. Each small box = 0.04 sec; count the QRSs on a 6-second strip × 10 for the rate.
Normal sinus rhythm: rate 60–100, regular (equal R-to-R), upright rounded P before every QRS, PR 0.12–0.20 sec, narrow QRS under 0.12 sec. Sinus arrhythmia (also normal): the same, but the rate varies with breathing or autonomic changes; common in young people; no effect on cardiac output. "Sinus" = it starts at the SA node.

Why dysrhythmias matter: they change rate (and sometimes filling or contractility), so they change cardiac output. Mechanisms: inappropriate automaticity (a rogue cell fires: MI, electrolyte imbalance, especially potassium, also calcium and magnesium); triggered activity (an extra impulse during or after repolarization: digoxin toxicity, SNS overstimulation, genetics); re-entry (an impulse keeps re-firing: ischemia, electrolytes). For any non-lethal rhythm, assess for symptoms before treating.

Peripheral Vascular Disease

PVD is the umbrella term for peripheral circulatory disease: PAD (arterial only), venous disease, or both. Mostly atherosclerosis of the extremity vessels (usually the legs): thick, hard vessels and plaques cause ischemia, then pain, mobility loss and poor function.

Risk factors

  • Smoking (biggest): over 80% with PAD are current or former smokers; about 4× risk, earlier onset, faster progression, 2× amputation, poorer survival.
  • Men and women are affected equally (sex isn't a risk factor).
  • Uncontrolled diabetes, high cholesterol, existing heart disease or stroke, age over 50.

Other causes

  • Thrombus (clots in the legs).
  • Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger's disease): autoimmune inflammation of peripheral arteries, mostly in smokers; permanent occlusion.
  • Vasospasm: Raynaud's (temperature sensitivity causes peripheral vasoconstriction; linked to scleroderma, pulmonary HTN, genetics; cause unknown); other autoimmune conditions.

Valve Disease & Infective Endocarditis

Blood flows through the valves in order: tricuspid → pulmonic → mitral → aortic ("TPMA"; "try before you buy": tricycle before bicycle). Tricuspid = right atrium to right ventricle; pulmonic = RV to pulmonary artery; mitral = left atrium to LV (two leaflets; the others have three); aortic = LV to aorta. The tricuspid and mitral are the AV valves. Valve tissue is delicate. Damage: wear and tear, calcium deposits, ring (annulus) problems, endocarditis (infection, often requiring replacement), clots, papillary muscle rupture (AV valves; rare).

The voiceover says "the tricuspid and aortic valves open during systole". The valves that open during ventricular systole are the pulmonic and aortic (the outflow valves). Check the slide.

Infective endocarditis

What happens

  • Infection on a valve forms vegetations (infected masses seen on echo) that keep the valve from opening and closing.
  • Septic emboli: vegetations stick to damaged endothelium and attract WBCs and platelets; the coagulation cascade and fibrin enlarge them; pieces break off with each contraction.
  • Tricuspid valve most common (the first valve bloodstream bacteria reach), so emboli go to the lungs (PE). Mitral or aortic: emboli go to the body (brain, coronaries). Any valve can be affected.

Who is at risk

  • Prosthetic valves attract bacteria: prophylactic antibiotics before dental work.
  • Pacemaker wires.
  • IV drug use (Staph aureus, Staph epidermidis via contaminated needles or skin, entering through the tricuspid first).
  • Any bloodstream infection (e.g. stepping on glass). Not every patient uses drugs; treat everyone the same.

Cell cycle (chemo targets phases)

  • G0: resting (most normal cells); cancer cells don't rest, and cells in G0 aren't affected by cycle-specific drugs.
  • G1: enters the cycle; DNA-synthesis enzymes made.
  • S: synthesis (DNA copied; the lecture describes the 46 chromosomes reorganizing into two sets of 23 pairs).
  • G2: prepares to divide (RNA, specialized proteins).
  • M: mitosis makes two daughter cells.
  • Generation time = time through one cycle (hours to days). Cancer cells cycle constantly with no restriction point, so no DNA repair and constant mutation.

Grading & staging

  • Grade (microscope): 1 = well, 2 = moderately, 3 = poorly differentiated / anaplastic.
  • TNM (international, biopsy-based): T = primary tumor size and involvement (T0 none, Tis = in situ, only in the layer it began; T1–T4 larger or more invasive); N = lymph nodes (N0 none, N1 closest few, N2 distant or numerous); M = metastasis (M0 no, M1 yes).
  • Stages: I confined to the organ of origin (most treatable), II locally invasive, III regional spread, IV distant sites.

Metastasis

Malignant cells invade, then travel by lymph or blood and need their own blood vessels (angiogenesis) to grow. Primary tumor = site of origin; secondary tumors look like the primary tissue under the microscope (breast cells in bone). Seeding: tumors shed into body cavities (e.g. intraperitoneal from colon). Implantation: direct growth into adjoining tissue (prostate into bladder). Lymphatic spread (common first stop): trapped cells die, go dormant, or proliferate and move to the next nodes. Vascular spread: into draining veins; the first stop is often the liver (portal vein).

Lung, Breast, Cervical & Colorectal Cancer

Historically the four highest-prevalence cancers in Kentucky (the most common overall is non-melanoma skin cancer). The lecture mentions racial, ethnic and rural disparities in diagnosis and survival; you don't need the statistics for the exam.

Lung cancer

  • Leading cause of cancer death in the US and Kentucky. Most are diagnosed late (advanced or metastatic) because early stages have few symptoms. Stage I five-year survival is over 60%; stage IV is below 10%.
  • Screening: low-dose CT. Average age at diagnosis is about 70 (often over 65).
  • Etiology: cigarette smoking (80–90% of cases; both a carcinogen and a promoter). Risk rises with pack-years (packs per day × years smoked; 2 packs a day from age 20 to 60 = 80 pack-years). Passive smoke (+20–30%), COPD (chronic inflammation), asbestos, radon (common in Kentucky; compounds with smoking), arsenic, genetics, prior chest radiation. Vaping data are still unclear.
  • Pathogenesis: smoke and toxins paralyze the cilia, so carcinogens build up along the airway. Cancer starts in an area of dysplasia (thickened bronchial mucosa), erodes the epithelium, and grows into the lumen or forms a mass.
  • Types: non-small cell (85–90%, slower growing) vs small cell (faster, metastasizes sooner, responds to initial treatment but relapses faster).
  • Signs and symptoms (overlap with smoking): a cough that won't go away or gets worse, hemoptysis or rust-colored sputum, chest pain worse with deep breathing, coughing or laughing, hoarseness (tumor pressing the laryngeal nerve), loss of appetite, unexplained weight loss, dyspnea, fatigue, recurrent bronchitis or pneumonia, new wheezing.

Breast cancer

  • Most common cancer in the US (outside non-melanoma skin cancer); about 1 in 8 women. Men can get it too.
  • Non-modifiable risks: age over 50; first period before 12, menopause after 55 (longer hormone exposure); dense breast tissue; family history of breast or ovarian cancer; prior chest radiation; mother took DES (given 1940–1971 to prevent miscarriage); BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations.
  • Modifiable risks: overweight or obesity after menopause (fat stores estrogen); hormone replacement therapy (estrogen plus progesterone for more than 5 years); some hormonal oral contraceptives; first pregnancy after about 30, not breastfeeding, never having a full-term pregnancy; alcohol (even one drink); night shift work; smoking.
  • BRCA mutations also raise ovarian, colon and pancreatic (and, in males, prostate) cancer risk. Genetic counseling; some choose a preventive mastectomy or oophorectomy.

Cervical cancer

  • Much less common now thanks to Pap smears and the HPV vaccine. Almost 100% of cervical cancer patients test HPV-positive.
  • Other risks: smoking; sexual history and STIs (chlamydia or gonorrhea compound HPV risk); immunosuppression (HIV/AIDS); genetics; DES.
  • HPV vaccine: 97% effective at preventing cervical cancer; 100% effective against genital warts; safe in decades-long studies. Best at age 11–12, can be given up to 45.

Colorectal cancer

  • Fourth most common cancer and fourth leading cause of cancer death; higher incidence and death in males.
  • Risks: age; inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis); family history of colorectal cancer or polyps; genetic syndromes (FAP, Lynch syndrome/HNPCC); sedentary lifestyle; diet low in fruits, vegetables and fiber, high in fat and processed meats; obesity; alcohol; tobacco.
  • Polyps: non-cancerous growths that can become cancer; usually asymptomatic and found on screening. Adenomatous = most common and higher cancer potential; hyperplastic = usually don't become cancer.
  • Symptoms (nonspecific): change in bowel habits, blood in stool, diarrhea or constipation, feeling the bowel doesn't empty (more rectal), abdominal pain or cramping, unexplained weight loss.
  • Screening starts at 45 (earlier with IBD, family history or genetic conditions). Flexible sigmoidoscopy sees about a third of the colon; colonoscopy usually every 10 years if normal.

Chemotherapy Principles

Antineoplastic drugs inhibit growth and spread of malignant cells. Cytotoxic chemotherapy kills cells, especially rapidly dividing ones. Chemo is one of three treatments, alongside surgery (removes the tumor) and radiation (high-energy radiation damages cancer cells); they're often combined after shared decision-making.

Cell-cycle specific vs nonspecific

  • Phase-specific: work only in one phase (antimetabolites in S, mitotic inhibitors in M). They can't touch G0 cells, so they need to stay in the blood a long time (prolonged infusion or frequent small doses).
  • Phase-nonspecific: toxic in any phase, including G0, but less selective for proliferating cells.
  • Usually combined: one specific plus one nonspecific.

What affects success

  • Works best on fast-growing tumors with a high growth fraction (short doubling time).
  • Cancer type, correct drug, dose and schedule.
  • Drug resistance (can explain relapse).
  • The patient's general health.

General principles

  • No single drug works on all cancers. Combination therapy usually beats a single drug, avoiding overlapping toxicity (e.g. not two cardiotoxic drugs).
  • Low therapeutic index: a fine line between therapeutic and toxic.
  • Rapidly dividing normal cells get hit: hair follicles (alopecia starts 7–14 days after chemo, reversible, regrows 1–2 months after therapy ends), GI (nausea and vomiting, which can occur several days after chemo; stomatitis), bone marrow (neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia). Fatigue is extremely common. Some effects are dose-limiting.
  • Most chemo drugs don't need routine therapeutic drug monitoring.
  • Toxicities, adverse effects and even allergies are usually not contraindications (premedication or desensitization). Reasons to delay or adjust: very low WBC/neutrophils, active infection, poor nutrition or hydration, liver or kidney dysfunction.
  • Fertility: any chemo can impair it. Discuss sperm banking or egg freezing before treatment. Most chemo drugs are pregnancy category D or X.

Myelosuppression

  • Bone marrow suppression (chemo or radiation): fewer WBCs, RBCs and platelets. Biggest risk is infection from neutropenia.
  • Nadir (lowest counts) about 7–14 days after the dose: highest risk of infection, anemia and bleeding. A key teaching point.
  • Prophylactic antibiotic (levofloxacin) for high-risk prolonged neutropenia: ANC below 500 for more than 7 days.

Stomatitis & extravasation

  • Stomatitis = inflammation of the oral mucosa. Magic mouthwash (prescription): viscous lidocaine, Mylanta, diphenhydramine, nystatin, prednisolone, distilled water. Swish, gargle, spit (swallow if the esophagus is involved). Treats symptoms; doesn't cure.
  • Extravasation = the drug leaks into tissue during IV administration, causing severe tissue damage. Established protocols; chemo is usually given through a port or tunneled line.

Osteoporosis & Its Drugs

Women have lower peak bone density and rapid bone loss in the early post-menopausal years, so they hit the fracture threshold earlier. From about age 30, osteoclast resorption exceeds osteoblast formation. Lifetime loss in women is about 35% of cortical and 50% of trabecular bone; men lose about a third less. Bone remodels throughout life (about 10% replaced each year). Osteoclasts chew bone; osteoblasts build. In osteoporosis, osteoclast activity rises and osteoblast activity falls.

Risk factors

  • Age; female (post-menopausal); Caucasian or Asian.
  • Adult fracture history; first-degree relative with a fragility fracture; genetics.
  • Low body weight, thin small frame; no weight-bearing exercise.
  • Smoking, alcohol, excess caffeine.
  • Long-term corticosteroids (inhibit osteoblasts).
  • Low calcium or vitamin D; eating disorders; gastric bypass (malnutrition).
  • Low estrogen or testosterone.

Manifestations & hip fracture

  • Early: none (screen high-risk patients with DEXA). Later: fractures, pain, loss of height, stooped posture (kyphosis).
  • Hip fracture (most common in women over 65; proximal third of the femur): mortality rises 2.5–4 times, from immobility complications (pneumonia, clots, skin breakdown, sepsis), not the fracture itself. Independent living is often lost.
  • Presentation: sudden hip pain, can't walk, severe groin pain, the leg is externally rotated and shortened, little bruising (encapsulated). A fragility fracture may present as vague knee, groin or thigh pain with no trauma.

Drug goal = reduce fractures. Prevention: calcium and vitamin D (vitamin D increases calcium absorption). Treatment mostly decreases bone resorption. Hormone replacement therapy is not recommended for osteoporosis (clots, breast and other cancers).

Fractures & Complications

A fracture is a break in bone continuity when stress exceeds what the bone can absorb. Causes: traumatic (fall), indirect (massive muscle contraction, rare), fatigue/stress (repeated stress, marathon runners), pathologic (osteoporosis, bone tumor, infection; highest risk in the elderly).

Types to know

  • Open (compound): bone breaks through the skin. Closed (simple): doesn't.
  • Transverse: straight line at 90°; common with traumatic falls.
  • Spiral: twisting injury.
  • Comminuted: more than one fracture line and more than two fragments (e.g. landing on the feet from a height).
  • Greenstick: incomplete; the bone bends. Most common in children.
  • Stress: repeated use.

Presentation & healing

  • PED: Pain, Edema (a natural splint), Deformity, plus loss of function and abnormal mobility. Splint in the position of comfort and go to the ED.
  • Healing phases: inflammatory (hematoma), reparative (fibrocartilage callus, ossification), remodeling. Complications come from this process going wrong.

Osteoarthritis & Degenerative Disc Disease

Synovial joints: outer fibrous capsule, inner synovial membrane, articular cartilage (prevents friction) and synovial fluid (lubricates). Arthropathy = joint disorder; with inflammation of one or more joints it's arthritis.

OA basics

  • Degeneration from aging and wear and tear; inflammation is local, not systemic. Most common arthritis and the leading cause of chronic disability in the US.
  • Sites: cervical and lumbosacral spine, hip, knee, hands, big toe. Usually spared: wrists, elbows, ankles.
  • More common in men before 45, in women after 45.
  • Risks: age (cartilage loss after 40), obesity (10 lb overweight adds 30–60 lb of force on the knee per step), long sports participation, repetitive heavy occupational work, prior trauma, misalignment (wider female hips, leg-length difference, compensating for a painful joint).

Pathophysiology

  • Pressure wears away cartilage, exposing subchondral bone; cysts form and erode the remaining cartilage.
  • Chondrocytes make proteoglycans to repair, but the excess fluid causes swelling.
  • Osteoblasts build osteophytes (bone spurs), the hallmark; they can break off into the joint.
  • Synovial fluid thickens; joint space narrows: bone on bone.

Manifestations: deep aching pain with exertion, relieved by rest; worse in cold weather; stiffness that improves once moving; crepitus; altered gait; hard swelling; limited ROM; deformity. Hands: Heberden's nodes (distal joints) and Bouchard's nodes (proximal joints).

Treatment: no drug stops or reverses OA, so manage pain, keep mobility, minimize disability (physical therapy, canes and walkers, weight loss, joint replacement). Mild to moderate: acetaminophen first, then OTC NSAIDs; topical capsaicin (from chili peppers). Moderate to severe: prescription NSAIDs, tramadol, eventually opioids or intra-articular steroid injections. Review NSAID risks from your NUR 325 pain lecture.

Degenerative disc disease

The same process in the spine: lumbar (L4, L5, S1) or cervical. Lifting and twisting (nurses!). Back pain is the second most common reason for doctor visits and the top cause of work disability under 45. Discs compress with age, so nerves are impinged. Lumbar: low back pain radiating down the back of the leg or buttock (sciatica), worse sitting, bending, lifting, twisting, better walking, changing position or lying down; numbness, tingling, weakness, foot drop. Cervical: pain down the shoulders and arms, arm or hand numbness and weakness. Can lead to herniated or ruptured discs.

Rheumatoid Arthritis

RA is a systemic autoimmune disease (a type III hypersensitivity): immune complexes deposit in the synovium and destroy the joint. Cause unknown: genetic predisposition plus a trigger (infection, injury, hormonal or reproductive factors).

Risk & patho

  • Diagnosed mostly at 40–60; women 2.5 times more than men; tobacco; strong family history.
  • B cells and macrophages release cytokines; they make rheumatoid factor (RF), an antibody against your own IgG. Diagnosis: RF plus symptoms and exam; CRP and sed rate for inflammation.
  • Lysosomal enzymes destroy cartilage; very vascular, inflammatory scar tissue called pannus forms and keeps destroying the joint.

Manifestations

  • Early: vague systemic signs (fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, generalized stiffness).
  • Joints affected symmetrically: heat, swelling, tenderness, morning stiffness lasting more than an hour; swelling is soft, spongy and warm (OA swelling is hard).
  • Advanced: subluxation (partial loss of contact, can't be fixed), swan-neck deformity (no Heberden's or Bouchard's nodes).
  • Systemic: fatigue and malaise, pericarditis or myocarditis, lung nodules, Sjögren's syndrome (dry eyes and mouth), rheumatoid nodules (firm subcutaneous granulomas near joints; about 25%).

Gout

A disorder of uric acid metabolism: urate crystals deposit in and around joints (gouty arthritis, acute and very painful). Purines break down to uric acid, which the kidneys excrete. High-purine foods: alcohol (beer, red wine), organ meats, red meat, seafood (scallops). Hyperuricemia comes from overproduction and underexcretion. "Disease of kings."

Risk & phases

  • Mostly men; obesity; HTN, diabetes, renal disease, sickle cell; alcohol use; high-purine diet; diuretics; Black Americans.
  • Phases: 1) asymptomatic hyperuricemia; 2) acute flare; 3) clinically inactive (still hyperuricemic); flares come closer together until chronic gouty arthritis.

Manifestations

  • Excruciating pain, classically the big toe (first MTP joint, about 50%): can't tolerate a sheet or sock.
  • Burning, redness, swelling, warmth, fever; lasts days to weeks.
  • Tophi: hard nodules of urate crystals under the skin around joints; may drain chalky material.
  • High risk of kidney stones.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

A complex autoimmune disease: autoantibodies, mainly ANA (antinuclear antibody), against the body's own DNA form immune complexes that can land in any organ and destroy it. The most common target is the kidney (glomeruli). In RA, complexes deposit only in the synovium.

Who & triggers

  • Genetic; females; ages 20–40; most common in Black Americans.
  • Sun exposure (triggers flares); hormones (estrogen-containing birth control raises risk 50%; menarche before 10 doubles it); smoking; antibiotic allergies.
  • Course: exacerbations and remissions. Flare warning signs: more fatigue, pain, headache. Triggers: sunlight, infection, abruptly stopping a medicine, stress.

Manifestations

  • Fatigue (most common), photosensitivity, butterfly (malar) rash (hallmark), edema (legs, around the eyes), fever, hair loss.
  • Raynaud's: small distal capillaries constrict with cold (painful); calcium channel blockers.
  • Any organ: brain (headache, seizures, stroke), lungs (pleuritis, effusion), heart (myocarditis, endocarditis), kidneys (about half; may need dialysis or transplant), vasculitis, anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, arthritis.
  • Multi-organ disease = poor prognosis; most have mild disease and do well.

Treatment depends on the systems involved: NSAIDs (headache, musculoskeletal pain, pleuritis, pericarditis); steroids (high dose for severe kidney or CNS disease, low dose for arthritis); hydroxychloroquine (skin and joints, and helps prevent kidney and CNS damage); biologics and immunosuppressives for multi-organ disease. RA vs SLE: both systemic autoimmune; RA targets mainly joints (plus fatigue and malaise), SLE can hit any organ.
