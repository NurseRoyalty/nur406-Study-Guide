Week 4

Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new. It's a map of Week 4's three topics, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. The Comprehensive Study Guide is separate; it pulls the whole week together on its own page. Week 4 is all neurologic, and each topic is a different way the nervous system gets damaged: degeneration and autoimmune attack on the nerves (ALS and GBS), infection and inflammation in and around the brain (meningitis, encephalitis, brain abscess), and lost or leaking blood supply (TIA and stroke). No flashcards or quiz questions here, just the map.

The non-contrast CT decides which, and that decides everything: clot busters for a clot, never for a bleed.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, follow the lecture order. Read neuro infections before the hemorrhagic stroke sections (the meningeal signs and meningeal layers are taught there first), and the TIA sections before stroke.

The Three Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1ALS & Guillain-Barré SyndromeALS: progressive, fatal upper and lower motor neuron degeneration, linked to excess glutamate; death from respiratory failure about 3 years after diagnosis; riluzole (hepatotoxic) buys about 6 months to a year. GBS: autoimmune peripheral demyelination after a viral (or Campylobacter) infection; weakness ascends, recovery descends; steroids and IVIG; most survive and return to baseline.
2Neuro Infectious DiseasesMeningitis: S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis by droplets; fever, headache, nuchal rigidity, photophobia, Kernig and Brudzinski, a non-blanching rash with meningococcus; bacterial kills in hours, so IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone, vancomycin) plus acyclovir and steroids right away; vaccines prevent it. Encephalitis: viral brain inflammation, HSV-1 the severe one (acyclovir). Brain abscess: pus from ear, tooth, mastoid or sinus; drain plus antibiotics.
3TIA, Stroke & HemorrhageTIA: under 24 hours, no infarction, but a warning (highest stroke risk in the first month). Stroke: 87% ischemic (thrombotic or embolic; save the penumbra within 3 hours with alteplase or thrombectomy) and 13% hemorrhagic (untreated HTN; reverse anticoagulation, control BP and ICP). Epidural, subdural and subarachnoid bleeds by location. BE FAST and last known normal; afterward, swallow study, contractures, hemianopia, aphasia and depression.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

The danger callouts and safety statements from across the three pages, condensed to a phrase, one row per topic. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation. Click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
ALS & Guillain-Barré SyndromeBoth: watch the breathing muscles. Riluzole is hepatotoxic: watch liver function. GBS: rapid decline over 24 hours can mean a ventilator; high fall risk; decreased sensation hides injuries; aspiration risk.
Neuro Infectious DiseasesBacterial meningitis: antibiotics as soon as possible, never held (it kills within hours). A non-blanching petechial rash means meningococcus. Septic emboli can cost fingers or limbs. Very communicable by droplets. Watch for increased ICP and seizures.
TIA, Stroke & HemorrhageAfter a TIA, go to the ED for any new episode. Non-contrast CT before any anticoagulant or thrombolytic: never give them to a hemorrhagic stroke. Treat ischemic stroke within 3 hours; get the last known normal. GCS below 8: intubate. Check blood sugar. Dysphagia: NPO until the swallow study. Alteplase: monitor BP, HR and rhythm; intracranial bleeding risk; aminocaproic acid only for life-threatening bleeding. SAH: keep BP within the ordered parameters.

Patterns You'll Notice Across These Rules

"The skull is a closed box." Infection swelling, an abscess and a bleed all fight for the same fixed space, so ICP and herniation are the shared danger, and the older, atrophied brain tolerates it best.

"Time decides the outcome." Bacterial meningitis kills in hours, the penumbra dies without reperfusion within 3 hours, and rapid GBS can need a ventilator within a day.

"Rule out the bleed first." The non-contrast CT comes before alteplase, anticoagulants and the rest of the stroke plan, because the right treatment for a clot can kill a client who is bleeding.

"Watch the airway, the breathing and the swallowing." ALS and GBS weaken the breathing and swallowing muscles; stroke clients may lose their airway or their swallow, so they're intubated below GCS 8 and kept NPO until the swallow study.

"Cover the worst cause until it's ruled out." Acyclovir is given with antibiotics until the CSF rules out a viral cause, and every suspected stroke is treated as an emergency even though some turn out to be TIAs or Bell's palsy.
