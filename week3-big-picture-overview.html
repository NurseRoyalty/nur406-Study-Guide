Week 3

Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new. It's a map of Week 3's four topics, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. The Comprehensive Study Guide is separate; it pulls the whole week together on its own page. Week 3 is all neurologic, and three of the four topics come down to communication: between neurotransmitters (Parkinson's), between nerve and muscle (myasthenia gravis), and along the nerve itself (multiple sclerosis). Migraines sit alongside them as the episodic one, driven by neurotransmitters and blood vessel tone. No flashcards or quiz questions here, just the map.

The Lectures' Own Order, Applied to the Four Topics

Five voiceovers, four pages: the Parkinson's lecture and the Parkinson's pharmacology lecture share one page.

StepWhere It LivesThe Common Thread
The Episodic HeadacheTopic 1, Migraines (all of it)A neurologic disorder that comes and goes in four phases. Neurotransmitter release triggers vasodilation, so the treatment logic follows: triptans constrict the vessels, which is also why they're off-limits in ischemic stroke, heart disease and angina.
The Neurotransmitter ImbalanceTopic 2, Parkinson's Disease & Parkinson's Pharmacology (the disease sections first, then the drug sections)The substantia nigra stops making dopamine, leaving acetylcholine unopposed, so movement loses its brakes: tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia. Every drug either adds dopamine (Sinemet, Duopa, the agonists) or blocks acetylcholine, and every drug loses ground as the disease progresses.
The Nerve–Muscle ConnectionTopic 3, Myasthenia Gravis (all of it)Acetylcholine again, but this time the problem is the receptor: antibodies destroy the nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction. The fix is more acetylcholine (cholinesterase inhibitors), and too much of that fix causes its own crisis.
The Nerve ItselfTopic 4, Multiple Sclerosis (all of it)Autoimmune again, but now the target is CNS myelin. Inflammation slows the signal; axon death blocks it permanently. The drugs mostly keep T cells from crossing the blood-brain barrier, and relapses are hit with high-dose steroids.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, follow the lecture order. Read Parkinson's before myasthenia gravis (MG builds on the acetylcholine review from Parkinson's), and the Parkinson's disease sections before the drug sections.

The Four Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1MigrainesA 4- to 72-hour headache meeting the diagnostic criteria (at least two pain features plus at least one of nausea and vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia), moving through premonitory, aura, headache and recovery phases. Treat early: analgesics, then triptans (not with ischemic stroke, heart disease or angina), rimegepant when triptans can't be used, antiemetics for vomiting. Prevent with propranolol, amitriptyline, anti-epileptics or estrogen when attacks are frequent or long.
2Parkinson's Disease & Parkinson's PharmacologyDopamine loss with relative acetylcholine excess gives the triad: rest tremor, cogwheel rigidity, and bradykinesia (the major cause of disability), plus shuffling gait and postural instability. Sinemet is the gold standard but takes months, loses effect (on-off, wearing off), and is blunted by protein. Duopa infuses it continuously; pramipexole is early monotherapy (watch for compulsions); apomorphine rescues "off" episodes.
3Myasthenia GravisAntibodies destroy nicotinic ACh receptors, causing fluctuating weakness that improves with rest, starting in the eyes and face and threatening swallowing and breathing. Steroids and neostigmine treat it. A myasthenic crisis is too little ACh; a cholinergic crisis is too much (SLUDGE, treated with atropine). Edrophonium tells them apart.
4Multiple SclerosisA T-cell and antibody attack on CNS myelin, ending in axon death and permanent loss. Mostly relapsing-remitting; symptoms depend on which nerves are hit (paresthesias, optic neuritis, cog fog, fatigue, bladder problems, falls). Disease-modifying drugs block T cells (interferon, glatiramer, fingolimod, natalizumab with its PML risk); relapses get pulse-dose IV steroids.

Respiratory failureMyasthenia Gravis: "Myasthenic Crisis" and "Cholinergic Crisis"Both crises end in respiratory failure for opposite reasons, and both may need mechanical ventilation.
Orthostatic hypotensionParkinson's: Sinemet, Duopa, and pramipexole with levodopa ("profound")The same teaching point every time: change positions slowly.
DepressionParkinson's: a risk factor and a complicationMigraines: the recovery phase. Multiple Sclerosis: common with loss of function, and an interferon side effect.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

The danger callouts and safety statements from across the four pages, condensed to a phrase, one row per topic. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation. Click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
MigrainesNo triptans with ischemic stroke, heart disease or angina (they constrict blood vessels). Rimegepant is a CYP substrate: caution with inhibitors and inducers.
Parkinson's Disease & Parkinson's PharmacologyAspiration, UTI, skin breakdown and fall risk as mobility declines. Sinemet: skin assessment before starting (melanoma), avoid high-protein meals, change positions slowly, monitor cardiac function at higher doses, watch for psychosis; MAOIs and anticholinergics can cause levodopa toxicity. Duopa: no MAOI within 2 weeks; falling asleep without warning (no truck driving or piloting). Pramipexole: compulsive behaviors; with levodopa, profound orthostatic hypotension and double the hallucination risk.
Myasthenia GravisMyasthenic crisis threatens the respiratory muscles (risk of respiratory arrest). Neostigmine causes bronchoconstriction: caution in asthma or lung disease. Cholinergic crisis means respiratory arrest risk and possible mechanical ventilation; the antidote is atropine. Medical alert bracelet at all times. Edrophonium distinguishes the two crises.
Multiple SclerosisHigh fall risk. Natalizumab: PML (possibly fatal), TOUCH program only, monotherapy only, higher risk with steroids; monitor LFTs and watch for allergic reactions. Glatiramer: monitor severe laryngeal constriction after injection. Interferon: monitor blood counts and liver. Pulse-dose steroids: monitor glucose; not for long-term use.

Patterns You'll Notice Across These Rules

"Balance is the whole story." Parkinson's is too little dopamine for the acetylcholine; myasthenia gravis is too few receptors for the acetylcholine; a cholinergic crisis is too much acetylcholine. Treatment pushes the balance back, and pushing too far causes a new problem.

"The fix becomes the problem." Levodopa loses effect and brings dyskinesias and psychosis; neostigmine overstimulates into cholinergic crisis; triptans constrict vessels a heart patient can't afford to lose; natalizumab suppresses the immune system enough to allow PML; pulse steroids spike glucose.

"Autoimmune means steroids, and steroids have limits." Myasthenia gravis and MS are both autoimmune with unknown triggers; both use steroids to calm the immune response, and the MS page spells out why they're kept short.

"Watch the breathing and the swallowing." Myasthenia gravis weakens the respiratory and swallowing muscles, both crises end in respiratory failure, and Parkinson's leads to aspiration pneumonia. Airway and nutrition run through all of them.

"Insidious onset, easy to brush off." MG starts as "a little weakness," MS clients look back and realize the weakness started long ago, and Parkinson's takes a while to diagnose because its onset is insidious.
