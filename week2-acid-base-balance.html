What pH Is

pH, acids and bases

  • pH is the potential, or the power, of a hydrogen ion. It is a scale that identifies whether a solution is more acidic or more alkaline, based on the hydrogen ions present.
  • The bottom of the scale is more acidic, the middle is neutral, and the higher the number, the more basic (alkaline).
  • pH is inversely related to hydrogen ion concentration: when hydrogen ions go up, pH goes down (more acidic); when hydrogen ions go down, pH goes up (more basic).
  • The scale is logarithmic, not linear: each number is 10 times more acidic or alkaline than the one before it. A pH of 5 is 10 times more acidic than a pH of 6; going from 7 to 6 is a tenfold increase in hydrogen ions.
  • An acid can donate (release) hydrogen ions. A base can accept hydrogen ions; bases have fewer hydrogen ions and give up a hydroxide ion (OH⁻).
  • A strong acid completely dissociates in solution (gives up all its hydrogen ions). A weak acid only partially dissociates.

Where Common Things Fall on the Scale

Acidic side (lower numbers)

  • Milk, coffee, tomato, lemon
  • Stomach acid (hydrochloric acid)
  • Battery acid

Neutral and alkaline side

  • Water: 7, neutral
  • Blood: on the alkaline side; the slide shows it at 8, but it is really a little lower than 8
  • Baking soda, stomach tablets (like Alka-Seltzer), soap
  • Drain cleaner: 14

Why Hydrogen Matters

What hydrogen does in the body

  • Helps maintain cell membranes.
  • Helps with enzymatic activity.
  • Is a component of water and keeps the body hydrated.
  • Helps with energy production.
  • Is a component of the sugars, proteins, starches and fats we consume.

The body works to stay in homeostasis and functions best within certain ranges, like a heart rate of 60 to 100 or sodium of 135 to 145. pH is one of those ranges.

Normal pH & How It Varies in the Body

Normal serum (blood) pH: 7.35 to 7.45. Most of the body's enzymes function within that very narrow range.

pH isn't the same everywhere. The stomach is the most acidic place in the body, with hydrochloric acid at a pH more like 1. The stomach is uniquely designed for it: mucous cells secrete mucus and bicarbonate at the cell surface to protect the epithelial cells. Outside the stomach, acid that strong causes damage: if the lower esophageal sphincter lets stomach acid into the esophagus, it can cause ulcers and erosion, and acid can further damage an already-damaged stomach lining (a stomach ulcer).

What pH Affects

Acid-base balance affects

  • Electrolytes: sodium, potassium in particular, and chloride.
  • Hormones.
  • Oxygen transport and delivery, which is greatly affected.

Acidic environment

  • Hemoglobin gives up oxygen more readily at the tissue.

Alkalotic environment

  • Hemoglobin holds on to oxygen and doesn't give it to the tissue, which can result in inadequate tissue oxygenation.

The slide shows this on the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve: the relationship between oxygen saturation of the blood and oxygen partial pressure, which shifts as the blood becomes acidotic or alkalotic.

Above 7.45Alkalosis.
Below 6.8 or above 7.8Incompatible with life, because of pH's profound effects on cellular metabolism, enzyme activity and oxygen delivery.

The body can function outside the normal range for a short time, but because pH affects so much (neurologic, respiratory, GI and cardiovascular function), the goal is to get back to normal as quickly as possible.

Volatile vs Non-Volatile Acids

Volatile acids

  • Can be eliminated as carbon dioxide (CO2) gas, by the lungs (pulmonary ventilation).
  • The only volatile acid in the body is carbonic acid (H2CO3).
  • Carbonic acid is a weak acid: it does not easily release its hydrogen ion.
  • In the presence of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, it readily dissociates into CO2 and water.

Non-volatile acids

  • Can be eliminated only by the kidneys.
  • Most are strong acids: they readily release their hydrogen ions.
  • Examples: lactic, phosphoric, sulfuric, acetoacetic and beta-hydroxybutyric acid (table below).
Non-Volatile AcidWhat the Lecture Says
Lactic acidA byproduct of anaerobic metabolism (metabolism without oxygen). A little different: it is metabolized by the liver and kidneys, and once oxygen is reestablished to the tissue it can be reconverted and used for energy again.
Phosphoric acidMade from phosphorus; works with calcium to help with strong bones, and supports kidney function.
Sulfuric acidResults from protein metabolism.
Acetoacetic acidProduced by the liver.
Beta-hydroxybutyric acidIncreases with exercise, calorie restriction and fasting. Its measurement is used as a diagnostic for people in DKA (ketoacidosis).

Why the Body Leans Acidic

  • At baseline, the body produces more acids than bases: from the food we eat and the drinks we consume, from the metabolism of lipids and proteins, and from cellular metabolism, whose waste product is CO2.
  • We want to avoid alkalosis, especially if a person is ill, because hemoglobin holds on to oxygen and tissue oxygenation suffers. An alkaline pH is also really difficult to correct.

The Three Lines of Defense

Line of DefenseHow FastWhat It Does
1. Chemical buffer systemsAlmost instantaneously (seconds)Primarily intracellular. Chemicals combine with acid or base to resist a change in pH. Makes only small changes; a relatively short, not sustainable fix.
2. Respiratory systemSeveral minutes to hoursChanges the rate or depth of breathing to blow off or retain CO2. The lungs are the primary controller of the carbonic acid supply.
3. Renal (kidney) controlSeveral hours to daysThe long-term regulator. Excretes hydrogen ions and retains or excretes bicarb. The kidneys are the primary controller of bicarb (the base).

The lungs and kidneys work together. The lungs respond relatively quickly; the kidneys need more time, but their mechanisms are more long-term.

Buffer Systems

Buffers are chemicals that combine with an acid or base to change the pH and bring it back to normal. They can release or accept a hydrogen ion, and they work almost instantaneously, but they make small, short-term corrections only.

BufferWhereHow It Works
Bicarbonate–carbonic acidThe main buffer outside the cells (extracellular fluid)CO2 from cell metabolism combines with water to form carbonic acid, which carbonic anhydrase breaks down in the lungs and kidneys (see the equation below).
PhosphateThe main intracellular bufferPicks up a hydrogen ion inside the cell to balance the pH inside the cell. It can't be measured. You don't need to memorize its equation.
ProteinPrimarily intracellular (most proteins are in cells)Most proteins can buffer. Carboxyl groups act as weak acids and release hydrogen ions; amino groups act as weak bases and accept them. When pH increases, proteins release hydrogen ions; when pH decreases, they accept them.
HemoglobinA protein buffer in the blood (some call it a fourth buffer system)As pH increases, hemoglobin loses hydrogen ions, and the reverse when pH decreases. It also binds CO2 to form carbaminohemoglobin, which carries CO2 to the lungs to be exhaled. That keeps CO2, a potential acid, from dissociating into carbonic acid and releasing hydrogen ions.

The Carbonic Acid Equation

CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 (carbonic acid) ⇌ H⁺ + HCO3⁻ (bicarb). The instructor: "You really want to be familiar and comfortable with the equation and know this backwards and forwards."
WhereWhat Happens to Carbonic Acid
LungsCarbonic anhydrase breaks it down into CO2 and water, and the lungs exhale the CO2.
KidneysCarbonic anhydrase breaks it down into hydrogen ions and bicarb, and the kidneys excrete the hydrogen ions (and can generate bicarb).
  • The equation goes both directions: carbonic acid can break down into CO2 and water or form from them, and the same on the bicarb and hydrogen side.
  • A patient retaining CO2, like a COPD patient, pushes the equation in the other direction and increases bicarb.
  • CO2 also rises with respiratory depression, such as narcotic use or an overdose: the respiratory rate is so low the lungs can't remove the CO2, which changes the pH.
  • When a patient is acidotic and not compensating through the respiratory system, sodium bicarb is often given as a quick fix to raise the pH. This happens a lot in the ICU.

Cellular Compensation & Potassium

Cells like a neutral electrical balance. Hydrogen and potassium are both positively charged, and potassium is found primarily inside the cell.

StepWhat Happens
In acidosisHydrogen ions move into the cell to balance the pH, and potassium moves out of the cell, which can raise blood potassium.
With kidney dysfunctionThe kidneys can't get rid of the excess potassium: hyperkalemia, which can cause dysrhythmias.
When pH returns to normalHydrogen ions move back into the blood and potassium moves back into the cells.
With working kidneysThe kidneys will already have excreted the excess potassium, so this can lead to hypokalemia (to be revisited with DKA later in the course).

Cellular compensation is a temporary correction of the pH, not a permanent solution.

Respiratory Control of pH

  • The body produces CO2 continuously, which forms carbonic acid; in the lungs it breaks back down into water and CO2, and the excess CO2 is exhaled.
  • This works only on volatile acids, not on non-volatile acids like lactic acid.
  • The lungs adjust serum pH by changing the rate or depth of breathing. The brain and arterial receptors are stimulated by the pH change.
  • On mechanical ventilation in the ICU, these adjustments can be made through the ventilator settings.

pH too low (acidosis)

  • Breathing rate or depth increases → more CO2 blown off → less carbonic acid → pH rises.

pH too high (alkalosis)

  • Breathing rate or depth decreases → CO2 retained → more carbonic acid → pH falls.

Renal Control of pH

  • The kidneys are the last to alter pH and are the long-term regulators. They can eliminate large amounts of acid by getting rid of hydrogen ions, using an active transport mechanism.
  • They can also excrete bicarb (a base) if the pH is too high, or retain bicarb if the pH is too low.
  • Example: in a COPD patient retaining CO2, over time the kidneys compensate by holding on to more bicarb, so this patient has higher CO2 and higher bicarb to balance the serum pH.
  • Kidney function is extremely important: in renal failure, patients can no longer maintain a normal pH and often take bicarb daily.
StateBicarb ReabsorptionHydrogen Ion SecretionEffect
AcidosisIncreasedIncreasedRaises pH back to normal
AlkalosisDecreasedDecreasedLowers pH back to normal
The kidneys handle acidosis better than alkalosis. It is harder for the kidneys to excrete bicarb than to hold on to it.

Compensation

If one side of the equation is the problem, the body pulls toward the other side to rebalance. That movement is compensation: the body's response to an acid-base imbalance, aimed at bringing the pH back to normal. It is evaluated with arterial blood gases (ABGs).

Underlying problem is metabolic

  • The respiratory system compensates.

Underlying problem is respiratory

  • The renal system compensates, bringing about metabolic changes (metabolic compensation).
TermWhat It Means
UncompensatedNothing has changed in response to a pH outside normal limits.
Partial compensationBicarb and CO2 levels have changed, but the pH is still outside normal limits.
Complete compensationBicarb or CO2 levels are abnormal, but the pH is back within normal limits.
