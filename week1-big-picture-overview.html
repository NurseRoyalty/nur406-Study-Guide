Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new. It's a map of Week 1's four topics, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. The Comprehensive Study Guide is separate; it pulls the whole week together on its own page. Week 1 is all cardiovascular. It moves from the heart's muscle and the sac around it, to the coronary arteries that feed the heart (and the drugs used when they block), to the walls of the arteries themselves. No flashcards or quiz questions here, just the map.

The Lectures' Own Order, Applied to the Four Topics

Four pages are easier to hold in your head when you see what each lecture is really about. Topics 2 and 3 are the two halves of one PowerPoint.

StepWhere It LivesThe Common Thread
The Heart Muscle and Its Sac: InflammationTopic 1, Myocarditis, Pericarditis & Cardiac Tamponade (all of it)The first lecture of 406. Inflammation of the myocardium (myocarditis, usually viral) can range from mild to heart failure and disrupt conduction. Inflammation of the pericardium lets fluid build up; a little fluid is a lubricant, too much is an effusion, and about 200 mL squeezes the heart so it can't fill: cardiac tamponade.
The Coronary Arteries: BlockageTopic 2, Acute Coronary Syndrome & Myocardial Infarction (the ACS sections first, then the MI sections)The longest lecture. Blood supply to the heart muscle is suddenly blocked or compromised, and the size of the blockage sets where the patient lands: unstable angina, NSTEMI, or STEMI. The page follows the plaque from stable to unstable to ruptured, then follows the heart tissue from ischemia to injury to irreversible infarction, minute by minute.
Treating the BlockageTopic 3, Acute MI Pharmacology (all of it)The second, shorter half of the ACS PowerPoint. Each drug has a job: oxygen raises delivery to the ischemic muscle, morphine eases pain, aspirin keeps the thrombus from growing, nitroglycerin reduces preload and afterload, beta blockers lower oxygen demand, and alteplase dissolves the clot. PCI comes first for a STEMI; fibrinolytics are for when a cath lab isn't available. Then the cost of reopening the vessel: reperfusion injury.
The Artery Wall: Weakening and TearingTopic 4, Aneurysms & Aortic Dissection (all of it)Back out to the vessels. Uncontrolled hypertension weakens the arterial wall until it bulges (an aneurysm), and a weakened wall can tear (dissection) or burst (rupture). The shapes tell you how it is managed, and the location tells you what the patient shows.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, follow the lecture order, and read Topic 2 before Topic 3: the pharmacology lecture assumes the plaque, clot and "time is tissue" ideas from ACS.

The Four Topics at a Glance

#TopicOne-Line Takeaway
1Myocarditis, Pericarditis & Cardiac TamponadeMyocarditis is mostly viral (Coxsackie A and B), treated with antivirals and anti-inflammatories depending on severity. Pericarditis brings sharp chest pain that worsens with deep breathing, fever and a friction rub, and is treated with NSAIDs (colchicine if NSAIDs aren't working). Once the fluid compresses the heart, it's cardiac tamponade: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds, pulsus paradoxus, and an emergency pericardiocentesis.
2Acute Coronary Syndrome & Myocardial InfarctionACS is unstable angina, NSTEMI and STEMI, and time is tissue. Unstable plaque (big lipid core, thin cap, inflammation) ruptures under sympathetic stress and a thrombus forms. Unstable angina's ECG returns to normal with no troponin change; an MI's ECG changes stay and troponin rises. Myocardial cells can't store ATP, so injury becomes irreversible from 30 minutes to 4 hours; reperfuse within 4 hours. STEMI shows ST elevation and does worse; the LAD is the widow-maker.
3Acute MI PharmacologyOxygen, morphine (now used more cautiously), chewed aspirin, nitroglycerin and beta blockers, all after checking vital signs. Aspirin decreases mortality; nitroglycerin does not. PCI is first choice for STEMI; alteplase (plasminogen to plasmin) when there's no cath lab, followed by heparin and clopidogrel, with bleeding as the main risk. Watch for reperfusion injury and VTach or VFib.
4Aneurysms & Aortic DissectionAneurysms come primarily from hypertension and atherosclerosis, cause turbulent flow and a bruit, and are most common in the aorta and cerebral arteries. Saccular, berry, fusiform and false aneurysms differ in shape and management. Never deep-palpate a suspected AAA. An aneurysm can be silent; a dissection is sudden, severe tearing pain and a surgical emergency.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

The danger callouts and safety statements from across the four pages, condensed to a phrase, one row per topic. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation. Click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Myocarditis, Pericarditis & Cardiac TamponadeCardiac tamponade is a medical emergency: the fluid has to be removed quickly (pericardiocentesis), because the chambers can't stretch and fill. Watch for decreased cardiac output: hypotension, confusion, dizziness, JVD, muffled heart sounds, pulsus paradoxus.
Acute Coronary Syndrome & Myocardial InfarctionFirst-time chest pain is unstable angina until proven otherwise. Unstable angina is a medical emergency: call 911, never drive to the ER. Take an impending sense of doom very seriously. Time is tissue: reperfuse within 4 hours, ideally within 30 minutes. A tombstone ST elevation is a red flag. An LAD blockage (widow-maker) is very lethal if not treated quickly.
Acute MI PharmacologyTake heart rate, blood pressure and respiratory effort before giving any of these drugs. Morphine: respiratory depression and hypotension. Nitroglycerin: never with sildenafil (profound hypotension); IV only if blood pressure tolerates it. Beta blockers only without contraindications. Thrombolytics only if eligible (not after a recent hemorrhagic stroke); bleeding is the main risk. Monitor closely for reperfusion injury and VTach or VFib.
Aneurysms & Aortic DissectionNo deep palpation of a suspected AAA (rupture risk); light palpation only. A ruptured AAA bleeds out very quickly. Aortic dissection is a surgical emergency where minutes matter; without intervention it progresses to cardiovascular collapse, shock and death. Berry aneurysm rupture, especially in the brain, can be fatal.

Patterns You'll Notice Across These Rules

"The emergency is when blood can't get where it needs to go." Tamponade stops the heart from filling, a complete clot in a STEMI stops blood reaching the heart muscle, and a rupture or dissection lets blood escape the vessel. The lectures call tamponade, ACS and aortic dissection emergencies, and a ruptured AAA bleeds out very quickly.

"The quality of the pain is a clue." Pericarditis: sharp and worse with deep breathing. ACS: longer, more severe, an elephant on the chest, no relief with nitroglycerin. Aortic dissection: sudden, severe and tearing. And some don't hurt at all: an aneurysm can be completely asymptomatic, and people over 65 or with diabetes can have a silent MI.

"Look-alikes on the ECG." Pericarditis can show ST elevation like an MI; Prinzmetal's angina can show ST elevation that returns to normal; unstable angina's ECG changes return to normal while an MI's stay. The ECG alone isn't the whole story: symptoms and troponin are part of the picture.

"Check before you give." The MI drugs can push an unstable patient over the edge, so vital signs come first, and several drugs carry a condition: morphine if they can tolerate it, IV nitroglycerin if blood pressure tolerates it, beta blockers if there are no contraindications, thrombolytics if the patient is eligible.

"Don't let the patient wait." Hesitating or delaying care is a big reason for MI deaths, while treated patients survive about 90 to 95% of the time. Teach patients to call 911 for unstable angina and to seek care if they suspect an MI: better safe than sorry.
