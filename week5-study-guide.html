Part 1A — DKA & HHS: Physiology & Labs

Topic 1, first half: what DKA and HHS are and who gets them, the insulin and counter-regulatory hormone background, why DKA makes ketones and HHS doesn't, why HHS glucose runs higher, the anion gap, plasma osmolality and sodium, and potassium.

DKA vs HHS: What They Are & Who Gets Them

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

  • A crisis that occurs as an acute metabolic complication of diabetes, with ketosis.
  • More common in people under 65, and characteristically associated with type 1 diabetes.
  • Can occur in type 2 under extreme stress: really serious infections, sepsis, trauma, cardiovascular or other emergencies. Type 2 diabetics still make insulin, which is typically enough to prevent fat breakdown into ketones unless the situation is extreme (for example, a type 2 diabetic who requires insulin and gets a serious infection).
  • About 30% of all DKA cases occur in people with type 2 diabetes, particularly those taking SGLT2 inhibitors (the "-flozins," like dapagliflozin).

Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)

  • Also called hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome; in the past, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar non-ketotic (HHNK). HHS is the name you'll see most.
  • Another life-threatening complication of diabetes, but with no ketosis.
  • Mostly type 2 diabetes, and more common over 65.
  • Pretty rare.

Background: Insulin, Glucagon & Counter-Regulatory Hormones

  • Extracellular glucose is primarily regulated by two hormones: insulin and glucagon.
  • After a meal, glucose enters the pancreatic beta cells, which leads to insulin release. Insulin restores normal blood glucose by reducing hepatic glucose production (less glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis) and by increasing glucose uptake by skeletal muscle and adipose tissue.
  • Gluconeogenesis turns non-carbohydrate substrates (amino acids, lactate, glycerol) into glucose. Glycogenolysis breaks glycogen down into glucose, which the body can use for energy.
  • In times of stress, more insulin is needed than usual; in diabetics that insulin isn't available, and DKA or HHS can develop.
  • In both, the main problem is that insulin isn't working properly: in DKA the body isn't making enough; in HHS the insulin that is available isn't working effectively.
  • On top of that, the counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol, growth hormone) go up. They increase hepatic glucose production and impair the peripheral tissues' ability to use glucose.
  • Dehydration and electrolyte imbalances occur primarily from the osmotic diuresis caused by glycosuria (glucose in the urine).

The lecture uses the word "gluconeogenesis" twice in a row; the second definition (glycogen broken down into glucose) is glycogenolysis, which is how it is listed here.

Why DKA Makes Ketones & HHS Doesn't

  • DKA is marked by increased gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and ketogenesis, with reduced glycolysis: that is the "keto" in ketoacidosis.
  • The insulin deficiency is absolute in type 1 and relative in type 2, and the excess counter-regulatory hormones add to it.
  • Suppressing lipolysis and ketogenesis is more sensitive to insulin than suppressing gluconeogenesis. In HHS, the residual insulin is enough to minimize ketoacidosis but not enough to control the hyperglycemia.
StepKetone Production
1Insulin deficiency and resistance mean glucose can't get into the cells, which need it for energy.
2Without glucose, the body breaks down fat for energy (lipolysis), releasing free fatty acids and glycerol from peripheral fat stores.
3The free fatty acids travel to the liver, are taken up by hepatocytes and activated.
4They are converted into acetyl-CoA, enter the ketogenic pathway and form ketone bodies.

Why not in HHS? One theory: there may be enough insulin activity to minimize lipolysis (and so ketone formation), but not enough to block gluconeogenesis, promote glucose use and prevent the hyperglycemia.

Why HHS Glucose Runs Higher

DKA

  • Clients present earlier, with symptoms of ketoacidosis: shortness of breath, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting.
  • Because they seek treatment earlier, glucose is usually 350 to 450 (can be as high as 800).
  • Tend to be younger with a higher GFR, so a greater capacity to excrete glucose.

HHS

  • Clients present later, without those early ketoacidosis symptoms.
  • Glucose is typically higher: it can exceed 1,000.
  • Glycosuria first limits the rise in glucose, but the osmotic diuresis causes volume depletion and a lower GFR, which limits glucose excretion. The effect is more pronounced in HHS.

Anion Gap, Plasma Osmolality & Sodium

LabWhat the Lecture Says
Anion gapDKA typically presents as an elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis. The anion gap is the difference between negatively and positively charged electrolytes, calculated from sodium, chloride and bicarb (sodium minus chloride plus bicarb), and reported on the blood chemistry panel.
Beta-hydroxybutyrateKetones are no longer really measured directly; in DKA, beta-hydroxybutyrate (one of the main metabolic products in ketoacidosis) is measured instead.
Closing the gapKetoacid anion excretion depends on volume status and GFR. Isotonic IV fluids correct the hypovolemia, increase renal excretion of ketoacid anions and reduce the anion gap.
Plasma osmolalityIncreased in both, in proportion to the glucose; usually not as high in DKA because the glucose isn't as high.
SodiumHyperglycemia pulls water out of the cells and expands the extracellular fluid, so plasma sodium falls: a dilutional hyponatremia. It isn't treated directly, because it corrects as the hyperglycemia is corrected.

Potassium: High on Arrival, Depleted Underneath

Most body potassium is in the cells. Clients with DKA and HHS present with a normal to elevated serum potassium but a significant total body potassium depletion, for three reasons:

ReasonWhat Happens
AcidosisIn metabolic acidosis, hydrogen ions move into the cells and potassium moves out to balance the charge, raising serum potassium. Working kidneys can offset this by excreting the excess.
Insulin deficiencyInsulin normally moves potassium into the cells; without it, potassium stays in the extracellular fluid, raising serum levels further.
HyperosmolarityThe osmotic diuresis from high glucose causes massive urinary losses, and potassium is lost continuously in the urine, depleting the total body supply.
  • Serum potassium can be 5, 6 or beyond on arrival. Don't bring it down unless it's causing problems and the client is symptomatic: it returns toward normal as the pH is corrected.
  • Once treatment starts, insulin drives potassium back into the cells and the serum level falls quickly, which can cause severe hypokalemia. If the kidneys have already excreted potassium, there is even less left to move back in.
  • As soon as the potassium reaches a normal level, replacement typically starts, because it will keep moving into the cells and the serum still needs a normal level to prevent cardiac problems.
  • Watch the client: are they symptomatic, and are there EKG changes of hyper- or hypokalemia (including dysrhythmias)?

Part 1B — DKA & HHS: Presentation, Treatment & Resolution

Topic 1, second half: precipitating factors, how each presents, the treatment steps, how you know each has resolved, and why hydration matters so much in HHS.

Precipitating Factors

  • A precipitating factor can often be identified.
  • Infection is the most common: severe pneumonia or a UTI without an insulin adjustment.
  • Inflammatory events: MI, CVA, sepsis, pancreatitis, without an insulin adjustment.
  • Poor compliance with insulin, or increased insulin needs that aren't met. Compliance can be checked with a hemoglobin A1c.

Clinical Presentation

DKA: evolves rapidly, over about 24 hours

  • GI: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, from delayed gastric emptying or an ileus induced by the metabolic acidosis.
  • Neurologic: slight confusion up to obtunded, with severe acidosis.
  • Volume depletion: decreased skin turgor, decreased urine output, dry oral mucosa, tachycardia, hypotension.
  • Fruity breath (some say it smells like nail polish remover) from exhaled acetone.
  • Kussmaul respirations: really deep respirations and hyperventilation, compensating for the metabolic acidosis.

HHS: begins several days before admission

  • Marked hyperglycemia with the typical signs: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, plus weakness.
  • As it progresses: lethargy, obtunded, up to coma. Neurologic symptoms are most common in HHS because of the hyperosmolarity.
  • Extracellular volume depletion: dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes, hypotension, tachycardia.
  • Symptoms of a coexisting illness that may have precipitated it (pneumonia, UTI) are usually present.

Treatment

Hospitals usually have a protocol and algorithm; follow your hospital's protocol. The lecturer cites the University of Kentucky (UK) protocol for the potassium numbers.

StepWhat the Lecture Says
1. Fluid replacementUsually first: expands extracellular volume and stabilizes the client hemodynamically. Treat volume depletion rapidly (normal saline); fluid resuscitation in HHS should be pretty aggressive.
2. Correct the potassium deficitPer the UK protocol, a DKA client receives potassium replacement if potassium is less than 5 before insulin is started, to prevent the hypokalemia that follows. If potassium is less than 3.3, replace it first, because insulin will worsen the loss by driving potassium into the cells.
Sodium bicarbMay be given if the pH is low (around 7.1 to 7.2), depending on how the client presents.
3. IV insulinAn IV insulin infusion, with blood sugar checked regularly (typically once an hour at first) and the rate adjusted to the glucose. Continue until the anion gap is closed and the condition is resolving.
Add dextroseWhen glucose reaches about 200 to 250, fluids change from no glucose to D5W/normal saline (dextrose added to the saline) while the insulin infusion continues, to prevent hypoglycemia if the gap is still open.
MonitorGlucose very closely and frequently (it shouldn't fall too rapidly), electrolytes and the anion gap.
Insulin drives potassium into the cells. Check potassium before insulin: below 3.3, replace potassium first. Watch for EKG changes of hyper- and hypokalemia throughout treatment.

How You Know It's Resolved

DKA: look at the labs

Make sure the ketoacidosis has resolved: venous pH or the anion gap, and the level of ketoacidosis by beta-hydroxybutyrate, which falls as the acidosis is corrected.

HHS: look at the patient

Are they mentally alert and back to baseline? Also check the plasma osmolality: the goal is about 315, which the lecture calls a normal level.

Once the client can eat regularly, IV insulin is changed to subQ insulin.

HHS: Why Hydration Matters So Much

  • If adequate hydration isn't maintained, dehydration and worsening renal function follow.
  • Clients with poor fluid intake from altered thirst mechanisms or inability to access fluids (as may be seen in the elderly) are at increased risk of severe dehydration.
  • Diminished renal filtration further impairs glucose excretion, which exacerbates the hyperglycemia.

Part 2A — Thyroid Storm

Topic 2, first half: the two extremes of the thyroid spectrum, then thyroid storm: what it is, risk factors, clinical manifestations and treatment.

The Two Extremes of the Thyroid Spectrum

Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis)

The hyperthyroid extreme: an overwhelming release of thyroid hormones.

Myxedema coma

The hypothyroid extreme: severe hypothyroidism.

Many manifestations are the same as ordinary hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, just more dramatic or severe.

Thyroid Storm: What It Is

  • An overwhelming release of thyroid hormones that puts an intense stimulus on the body's metabolism.
  • Typically a reaction to a major stressor (a big infection, major surgery, a car wreck). The stressor releases stress hormones such as adrenaline, everything in the body is "revved up," and the body can't tolerate this for long.
  • Life-threatening: untreated, it can cause death within about 48 hours.
Risk FactorWhat the Lecture Says
Long-standing untreated hyperthyroidismThe lecture calls this Graves' disease.
A precipitating acute eventSurgery, trauma or infection, which may or may not involve the thyroid.
Irregular use or stopping antithyroid drugsCommonly reported before a thyroid storm.

Thyroid Storm: Clinical Manifestations

ManifestationWhat the Lecture Says
Fever (the most notable)Body temperature rises greatly, to 104 or 105; excessive sweating; the client is hot and can't tolerate heat.
TachycardiaOften above 140.
Blood pressureHypertension at first. With sustained tachycardia the heart can't fill properly, so BP may start to drop. Untreated: hypotension, heart failure and pulmonary edema that can lead to death.
DysrhythmiasA-fib and atrial flutter are the most common.
OtherNausea, vomiting, agitation, tremors, psychosis, up to stupor or coma.

The transcript says "heat tolerance"; with fever and excessive sweating, the page reads it as not tolerating heat.

Thyroid Storm: Treatment

Treatment hits several points where thyroid hormone acts. These clients are in the ICU and very closely monitored.

DrugPurpose & Details
Beta blockerControls the symptoms and signs of the increased adrenergic tone; adjusted as needed to keep a proper heart rate and blood pressure.
Thionamide (PTU)Blocks new hormone synthesis and suppresses conversion of T4 to T3. Synthesis is blocked within about 1 to 2 hours; within 24 hours, T3 levels can drop up to about 45%.
Iodine solutionBlocks the release of thyroid hormone. Given about 1 hour after the first dose of PTU.
GlucocorticoidsReduce T4 to T3 conversion. Evidence is conflicting, so they're given when there is clear evidence of thyroid storm, because they rapidly reduce active thyroid hormone and its effects.
Bile acid sequestrantsThyroid hormones are metabolized in the liver and excreted in bile; free hormone released into the intestines is reabsorbed. Sequestrants decrease enterohepatic circulation and recycling of thyroid hormone.

Enterohepatic circulation is the circulation of biliary acids, bilirubin and other substances from the liver to the bile, stored in the gallbladder before reaching the small intestine.

The lecture says "thionamide, which is also called PTU." PTU is the thionamide named here.

Part 2B — Myxedema Coma

Topic 2, second half: myxedema coma's risk factors, clinical manifestations and treatment, then thyroid storm vs myxedema coma side by side.

Myxedema Coma: What It Is

  • Severe hypothyroidism leading to decreased mental state, hypothermia and slowed function of other organs.
  • A medical emergency with a high mortality rate, but uncommon, because hypothyroidism can be diagnosed earlier and kept from getting worse.
Risk FactorWhat the Lecture Says
Severe, long-standing or poorly controlled hypothyroidismThe baseline risk, as with thyroid storm on the other end.
A precipitating acute eventInfection, MI or major surgery on top of poorly controlled hypothyroidism.
Sedative drugsEspecially opioids, which can trigger myxedema coma in someone who already has hypothyroidism.

Myxedema Coma: Clinical Manifestations

ManifestationWhat the Lecture Says
Altered consciousnessDespite the name, clients don't often present in coma: confusion, lethargy, obtunded, some with psychosis. Untreated, it can progress to coma.
HypothermiaDecreased thermogenesis from decreased metabolism. The lower the temperature, the higher the mortality.
Hypotension and bradycardiaThyroid hormone helps maintain BP homeostasis; severe hypothyroidism causes bradycardia, decreased contractility, low cardiac output and hypotension.
HyponatremiaAbout 50% of the time. When present it is severe and adds to the decreased mental status. Impaired free water excretion causes a dilutional hyponatremia.
HypoglycemiaCan be caused by hypothyroidism alone and may be worse in myxedema coma.
HypoventilationOverall depression of the ventilatory drive and decreased responsiveness, leading to hypoxia and hypercapnia (respiratory acidosis). Some clients need a mechanical ventilator.

The lecture words it as hypoventilation "results from a respiratory acidosis"; the page follows the rest of that sentence (depressed ventilatory drive leading to hypoxia and hypercapnia).

Myxedema Coma: Treatment

If myxedema coma is suspected, treat before lab confirmation.
TreatmentWhat the Lecture Says
Thyroid hormones (combination)Levothyroxine (T4) plus liothyronine (T3): the combination works better than T4 alone. Given IV until the client can take T4 orally. Thyroid levels are monitored daily to confirm the therapy is working.
GlucocorticoidsClients often have a coexisting adrenal insufficiency (decreased pituitary function, secondary adrenal insufficiency, or ACTH secretion blunted by severe hypothyroidism). IV hydrocortisone is given until adrenal insufficiency is ruled out.
Supportive measuresIV fluids, electrolyte replacement as needed, mechanical ventilation for hypoventilation, glucose monitoring, and treating any underlying infection.

Part 3A — Addison's Disease & Replacement Therapy

Topic 3, first half: the three S's, causes and the ACTH feedback loop, aldosterone vs cortisol deficiency, lifelong replacement therapy, and stress dosing and teaching.

Addison's Disease: The Three S's

Addison's disease is a disease of the adrenal cortex that causes hyposecretion of all three adrenocortical hormones.

SHormoneWhat It Does
SugarCortisolStimulates gluconeogenesis and raises blood sugar; mobilizes fat stores; helps break down proteins.
SaltAldosteroneAssists in fluid and electrolyte balance.
SexAndrogensMale sex hormones.
  • The most severe effects come from the lack of cortisol.
  • Long-term effects: immune suppression and bone breakdown (bone density problems).

Causes & the Feedback Loop

  • It can be idiopathic, but the most common cause is autoimmune. The adrenal gland can also be damaged or destroyed by disease or trauma.
  • In primary adrenocortical insufficiency (primary Addison's), the adrenal gland stops secreting cortisol, so there is no negative feedback. The anterior pituitary increases MSH and ACTH to try to stimulate cortisol, but despite very high levels, the adrenal cortex can't produce it, and the loop never closes.
  • Manifestations don't become evident until about 90% of the adrenal cortex is destroyed, so Addison's is usually advanced at diagnosis, or diagnosed during an Addisonian crisis.

Aldosterone Deficiency vs Cortisol Deficiency

Low aldosterone (hypoaldosteronism)

  • Aldosterone regulates sodium, and so water retention. Low aldosterone means loss of water and sodium, with potassium retention.
  • Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia and dehydration, with the signs of all three.
  • Hypotension from decreased vascular tone, cardiac output and circulating blood volume.
  • Salt craving.
  • The lecture adds that hypoaldosteronism signals more ADH, which stimulates water reabsorption and could lead to fluid volume excess in some cases.

Low cortisol (hypocortisolism)

  • Low energy: a lack of stress hormones.
  • Hypoglycemia, since cortisol normally raises blood glucose.
  • Hyperpigmentation: the pituitary increases ACTH to raise cortisol, and ACTH also stimulates melanin secretion by melanocytes, so unsuppressed ACTH darkens the skin.

Lifelong Replacement Therapy

Adrenal insufficiency requires lifelong replacement with the appropriate corticosteroids.

DrugWhat the Lecture Says
Hydrocortisone (Cortef)The drug of choice: has both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid activity. IM, IV and PO.
PrednisoneGlucocorticoid, PO.
DexamethasoneGlucocorticoid, IV or PO.
FludrocortisoneAdded when extra mineralocorticoid activity is needed; the only mineralocorticoid available. Works like aldosterone, so expect an impact on sodium and water balance. PO.
  • Dosing should mimic the body's normal pattern of corticosteroid secretion. Mineralocorticoids can be given once a day; glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid doses should approximate what the adrenal glands normally secrete.
  • Never stop therapy abruptly.

Stress Dosing & Teaching

  • Healthy adrenal glands increase glucocorticoid output in response to stress. These clients don't get that boost, so it has to be given exogenously: increase the dose in times of stress.
  • Supplemental dosing follows guidelines for specific medical conditions and surgical procedures, so it varies. Example: for a mild or febrile illness, the 3 x 3 rule (loosely, three times the usual dose for three days).
  • Keep an emergency supply: an injectable preparation plus an oral preparation.
  • Wear a medical alert bracelet so emergency personnel know about the glucocorticoid needs.
Not following the regimen (stopping abruptly, or not increasing the dose under stress) can cause an Addisonian crisis, which can be fatal.

Part 3B — Addisonian (Adrenal) Crisis

Topic 3, second half: what an Addisonian crisis is and what causes it, its symptoms, and its treatment.

Addisonian (Adrenal) Crisis: What It Is & What Causes It

  • A complication of Addison's disease: acute adrenal insufficiency, a sudden insufficiency of serum corticosteroids. A severe, life-threatening endocrine emergency.
  • More common with primary adrenal insufficiency, but can occur with secondary and tertiary.
TypeCause
PrimaryThe adrenal gland itself stops secreting cortisol (Addison's disease).
SecondaryACTH deficiency, usually from conditions affecting the hypothalamus or pituitary.
TertiaryTypically excessive glucocorticoid exposure.

Triggers

  • A sudden loss of the adrenal glands.
  • Increased stress in someone with underlying adrenal problems: surgery, infection, trauma, severe illness.
  • Sudden cessation of long-term steroid therapy, which is why clients stopping steroids are tapered.

Addisonian Crisis: Symptoms

Symptoms stem mainly from the lack of cortisol and aldosterone.

SymptomWhat the Lecture Says
Sudden painLower back, legs and abdomen; the lecture attributes it to hyponatremia from the lack of mineralocorticoids.
Severe vomiting and diarrheaLead to dehydration and hypotension; also tied to the mineralocorticoid deficiency.
CNS changesConfusion, slurred speech, up to loss of consciousness.
Electrolyte imbalancesHyperkalemia and hyponatremia, with their signs and symptoms. Put the client on an EKG and watch for dysrhythmias.

The lecture's video example shows shaking and decreased level of consciousness in a girl with no prior diagnosis: Addison's is often first diagnosed when the person has an Addisonian crisis, and family may not recognize it as an emergency.

Addisonian Crisis: Treatment

Untreated, it can be fatal. Rapidly replace fluid, salt and glucocorticoids, and give glucose.

TreatmentWhat the Lecture Says
Hydrocortisone IVAbout a 100 mg initial bolus, then about 50 mg every 6 hours IV, until vital signs stabilize and the client can take food and medications orally. Hydrocortisone has both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid actions.
Normal salineReplaces lost sodium: about 1 to 3 liters IV, or 5% dextrose in normal saline, based on volume status and urine output.
Once stableSwitch to PO medications, continue fluids, and taper to maintenance doses as the condition resolves.
